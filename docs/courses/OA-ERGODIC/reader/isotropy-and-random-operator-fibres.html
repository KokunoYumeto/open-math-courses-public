Introduction

An operator attached to an orbit must be compatible with every arrow. An arrow from a point to itself imposes an additional commutation condition. That condition survives even if the space has just one orbit.

This lesson calculates it for countable discrete groupoids and proves a finite-isotropy version of the averaged rank-one operator theorem. It explains both the role of isotropy and why ergodicity alone does not give the invariant-scalar centre formula for an arbitrary groupoid.

We use the definitions and regular representations of Measured groupoids and transverse measures and Square-integrable representations and random operators. The contrast with a principal relation is developed in Orbits, stabilizers, and relation algebras. Basic references are [Connes] and [Takesaki].

The discrete hypotheses below also make endpoint coordinates measurably recoverable. This feature matters beyond discrete examples: Principal groupoids with extra fibre information proves that trivial isotropy and one orbit alone need not give a scalar centre or generation of the full isotropy commutant at merely countably generated arrow scope. Its finite faithful counterexample does not change the discrete calculations proved here.

All groupoids in this lesson have countably many arrows and the discrete Borel structure. Every Hilbert space is separable. The measure on the unit space satisfies 0<μ({x})<∞0<\mu(\{x\})<\infty0<μ({x})<∞ at every unit xxx. Countability therefore makes it sigma-finite, and no nonempty subset of the unit space is negligible. These hypotheses make every choice used below measurable.

2. Equivariant operators are isotropy commutants

A bounded equivariant operator field A=(Ax)A=(A_x)A=(Ax​) satisfies

AyU(γ)=U(γ)Ax,γ:x→y.(2.1) A_yU(\gamma)=U(\gamma)A_x,\qquad \gamma:x\to y. \tag{2.1} Ay​U(γ)=U(γ)Ax​,γ:x→y.(2.1)

Its norm is sup⁡x∥Ax∥\sup_x\|A_x\|supx​∥Ax​∥. In the present atomic measured setting this is also its essential supremum norm with respect to the unit-space measure.

Theorem 2.1. The algebra of bounded equivariant fields is normally isomorphic to

π(H)′={B∈B(K):Bπ(h)=π(h)B for every h∈H}.(2.2) \pi(H)'=\{B\in B(K):B\pi(h)=\pi(h)B\text{ for every }h\in H\}. \tag{2.2} π(H)′={B∈B(K):Bπ(h)=π(h)B for every h∈H}.(2.2)

The isomorphism sends BBB to the field Ax=TxBTx∗A_x=T_xBT_x^*Ax​=Tx​BTx∗​.

Proof. Apply (2.1) to txt_xtx​. It forces Ax=TxAoTx∗A_x=T_xA_oT_x^*Ax​=Tx​Ao​Tx∗​. Apply it to the isotropy arrows at ooo: it forces Ao∈π(H)′A_o\in\pi(H)'Ao​∈π(H)′. Conversely, a BBB in this commutant gives an equivariant field by (1.3). The correspondence respects multiplication, adjoints, and norms.

The commutant in (2.2) is a von Neumann algebra because it is a weakly closed commutant in B(K)B(K)B(K). The field algebra has a concrete representation on ⨁x∈OKx\bigoplus_{x\in O}K_x⨁x∈O​Kx​. Under the unitary transports TxT_xTx​, this is the diagonal amplification B↦⨁xBB\mapsto\bigoplus_xBB↦⨁x​B. It preserves suprema of bounded increasing positive nets, so the isomorphism is normal. Positive atomic weights on the unit space change this Hilbert representation by the unitary multiplying the xxx-coordinate by the square root of its mass; they do not change the operator field algebra. □\square□

This conclusion applies in particular to the square-integrable representations used to define random Hilbert spaces. In this atomic setting the equivariant field itself already gives the concrete realization.

For a groupoid with several orbits, choose one point in each orbit and repeat the construction. There are at most countably many orbits. The bounded field algebra is the bounded product of the resulting isotropy commutants.

3. The regular representation keeps isotropy

On the range fibre Gy=r−1(y)\mathcal G^y=r^{-1}(y)Gy=r−1(y), the left regular representation acts by left translation. Formula (1.1) identifies this fibre with O×HO\times HO×H, through

ℓ2(Gy)≅ℓ2(O)⊗ℓ2(H).(3.1) \ell^2(\mathcal G^y)\cong\ell^2(O)\otimes\ell^2(H). \tag{3.1} ℓ2(Gy)≅ℓ2(O)⊗ℓ2(H).(3.1)

(1ℓ2(O)⊗λH(H))′.(3.3) \bigl(1_{\ell^2(O)}\otimes\lambda_H(H)\bigr)'. \tag{3.3} (1ℓ2(O)​⊗λH​(H))′.(3.3)

B(ℓ2(O))⊕B(ℓ2(O)).(3.4) B(\ell^2(O))\oplus B(\ell^2(O)). \tag{3.4} B(ℓ2(O))⊕B(ℓ2(O)).(3.4)

Proof. Apply Theorem 2.1 to (3.2). For C2C_2C2​, its regular representation has a one-dimensional +1+1+1 eigenspace and a one-dimensional −1-1−1 eigenspace. An operator commutes with that involution exactly when it preserves both eigenspaces, giving (3.4). □\square□

The second algebra has two independent central projections, despite transitivity of the groupoid. Its two spectral sectors come from isotropy. This does not conflict with the invariant-scalar centre theorem for principal groupoids, whose isotropy groups are trivial.

4. Averaged rank-one operators for finite isotropy

Let HHH now be finite, and let π\piπ be any unitary representation on a separable Hilbert space KKK. For B∈B(K)B\in B(K)B∈B(K), define

Q(B)=1∣H∣∑h∈Hπ(h)Bπ(h)∗.(4.1) Q(B)=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}\pi(h)B\pi(h)^*. \tag{4.1} Q(B)=∣H∣1​h∈H∑​π(h)Bπ(h)∗.(4.1)

Lemma 4.1. The map QQQ is a normal conditional expectation onto π(H)′\pi(H)'π(H)′.

Proof. Each conjugation is normal, unital, and completely positive, so the finite average has those properties. Left multiplication permutes the summation indices, showing that its range commutes with π(H)\pi(H)π(H). It fixes each element of that commutant. If A,CA,CA,C belong to the commutant, then Q(ABC)=AQ(B)CQ(ABC)=AQ(B)CQ(ABC)=AQ(B)C, proving bimodularity. □\square□

For v,w∈Kv,w\in Kv,w∈K, let ∣v⟩⟨w∣|v\rangle\langle w|∣v⟩⟨w∣ denote ζ↦⟨ζ,w⟩v\zeta\mapsto\langle\zeta,w\rangle vζ↦⟨ζ,w⟩v, with the inner product linear in its first variable. Put

Θ(v,w)=Q(∣v⟩⟨w∣).(4.2) \Theta(v,w)=Q(|v\rangle\langle w|). \tag{4.2} Θ(v,w)=Q(∣v⟩⟨w∣).(4.2)

Theorem 4.2. The linear span of the operators Θ(v,w)\Theta(v,w)Θ(v,w) is ultraweakly dense in π(H)′\pi(H)'π(H)′. It is enough to take v,wv,wv,w from any countable total set closed under rational complex linear combinations.

Proof. Choose finite-rank projections Pn↑1P_n\uparrow1Pn​↑1, built from such a total set by Gram–Schmidt. For A∈π(H)′A\in\pi(H)'A∈π(H)′, the finite-rank operators PnAPnP_nAP_nPn​APn​ are uniformly bounded and converge strongly to AAA. Each is a finite linear combination of rank-one operators using vectors in the span of the total set. Applying the finite sum (4.1) gives

Q(PnAPn)⟶Q(A)=A Q(P_nAP_n)\longrightarrow Q(A)=A Q(Pn​APn​)⟶Q(A)=A

strongly: each conjugation preserves strong convergence on this bounded sequence. This proves density using the linear span of the total set. Norm approximation of its vectors by rational complex combinations gives the last assertion, since ∥∣v⟩⟨w∣∥=∥v∥∥w∥\||v\rangle\langle w|\|=\|v\|\|w\|∥∣v⟩⟨w∣∥=∥v∥∥w∥ and QQQ is contractive. □\square□

The averaged rank-one operators exhaust the isotropy commutant. They exhaust all of B(K)B(K)B(K) precisely when that commutant is all of B(K)B(K)B(K), which happens when the isotropy representation is scalar. Trivial isotropy is one way to ensure that condition.

In the transitive groupoid, use counting measure on each range fibre and take a section supported at the base point ooo, with value vvv. Its coefficient operator has a sum over only ∣H∣|H|∣H∣ nonzero arrows at each target. Thus it is a bounded square-integrable coefficient section. Averaging the rank-one operators over all these arrows gives ∣H∣TyΘ(v,w)Ty∗|H|T_y\Theta(v,w)T_y^*∣H∣Ty​Θ(v,w)Ty∗​ at the point yyy. Sections supported at each xxx, with values in TxT_xTx​ of a countable total subset of KKK, give a countable total family of such coefficient sections. Theorem 4.2 consequently describes the fibre algebra generated by these groupoid coefficient operators, including finite nontrivial isotropy.

Example 4.3. Let H=C2H=C_2H=C2​, and decompose K=K+⊕K−K=K_+\oplus K_-K=K+​⊕K−​ according to the two eigenspaces of its involution. Formula (4.1) deletes the off-diagonal blocks of an operator:

Q(ABCD)=(A00D). Q\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\ C&D\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\0&D\end{pmatrix}. Q(AC​BD​)=(A0​0D​).

Thus the rank-one averages generate B(K+)⊕B(K−)B(K_+)\oplus B(K_-)B(K+​)⊕B(K−​), rather than all B(K)B(K)B(K) when both spaces are nonzero.

For infinite isotropy there is no normalized finite sum in (4.1). The finite-isotropy theorem keeps its exact range. 5. Exercises with solutions

Level 1 asks for a computation or a direct application. Level 2 asks for a proof using the lesson’s framework. Level 3 combines results or examines a hypothesis whose failure changes the conclusion.

Exercise 5.1 (changing the transports). Level 2. Replace txt_xtx​ by txaxt_xa_xtx​ax​, where ax∈Ha_x\in Hax​∈H. Determine the new coordinate of an arrow γ:x→y\gamma:x\to yγ:x→y.

Solution. Its new coordinate is ay−1haxa_y^{-1}ha_xay−1​hax​. The transport unitary changes to Txπ(ax)T_x\pi(a_x)Tx​π(ax​). If B∈π(H)′B\in\pi(H)'B∈π(H)′, the field TxBTx∗T_xBT_x^*Tx​BTx∗​ remains the same. Thus the description of the random-operator algebra does not depend on the chosen transports.

Exercise 5.2 (a nonfaithful isotropy representation). Level 2. Suppose H=C2H=C_2H=C2​ acts trivially on an infinite-dimensional KKK. What is its equivariant field algebra on one transitive orbit?

Solution. It is B(K)B(K)B(K), because π(H)\pi(H)π(H) consists of the identity. Nontrivial isotropy of the groupoid alone does not force a nontrivial centre; the isotropy representation matters.

Exercise 5.3 (two characters). Level 1. For K=C2K=\mathbb C^2K=C2 and the C2C_2C2​ representation diag⁡(1,−1)\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)diag(1,−1), compute all equivariant operators at the base point.

Solution. They are precisely the diagonal matrices diag⁡(a,b)\operatorname{diag}(a,b)diag(a,b). The commutation equation makes both off-diagonal entries zero. The algebra is C⊕C\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb CC⊕C, with two central minimal projections.

Exercise 5.4 (off-diagonal averages). Level 2. In Example 4.3 take v∈K+v\in K_+v∈K+​ and w∈K−w\in K_-w∈K−​. Compute Θ(v,w)\Theta(v,w)Θ(v,w). Then take both vectors in K+K_+K+​.

Solution. In the first case conjugation by the involution changes the rank-one operator's sign, so its average is zero. In the second case the rank-one operator is fixed, so Θ(v,w)=∣v⟩⟨w∣\Theta(v,w)=|v\rangle\langle w|Θ(v,w)=∣v⟩⟨w∣. This gives the two blocks separately.

Exercise 5.5 (several orbits). Level 2. A discrete groupoid has two transitive components. Its representations at chosen base points are the trivial representation of C2C_2C2​ on C3\mathbb C^3C3 and the regular representation of C2C_2C2​ on C2\mathbb C^2C2. Determine the random-operator algebra.

Solution. The first component contributes M3(C)M_3(\mathbb C)M3​(C). The second contributes C⊕C\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb CC⊕C. The bounded product over the two orbits is M3(C)⊕C⊕CM_3(\mathbb C)\oplus\mathbb C\oplus\mathbb CM3​(C)⊕C⊕C. Orbit decomposition and isotropy decomposition produce distinct central summands.
