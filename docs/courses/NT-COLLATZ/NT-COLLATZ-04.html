First passage and transport across scales

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs and exercises: CC0 1.0.

Suppose most starting points of a system eventually enter a smaller region. Can we repeat the statement inside that region? Not without examining where the first stage lands. Its outputs might concentrate on the exceptional starting points of the second stage.

The right object to keep is the first-passage distribution: the law of the first point where a trajectory enters the region. We will construct its map, account explicitly for trajectories that never enter, and prove how discrepancies between successive distributions accumulate. A final theorem explains why summable errors across scales give uniform control of orbit minima.

The main results apply to any map on a subset of the positive integers. Collatz supplies the motivating application, but none of the proofs assumes its conjecture. The prerequisites are iteration and well-ordering from Return maps and exact changes of clock, and countable probability masses, pushforwards, ℓ1\ell^1 contraction and event distance from Pushforward measures and logarithmic sampling. We also use elementary logarithms and geometric series. Tao’s first-passage reduction in Almost all orbits of the Collatz map attain almost bounded values, §§1.3 and 3, is a reference for the application. Its analytic estimates are a further subject, not assumptions silently inserted into this lesson.

Why a second application needs a distribution

Let Y={1,…,M}Y=\{1,\ldots,M\}, with M>1M>1. An event can hold at every point of YY except 1, so it has probability 1−1/M1-1/M under the uniform law. Yet a previous map can send every one of its inputs to 1. Under that landing law, the same event has probability zero. The size of the exceptional set under one measure is not its size under another.

A quantitative remedy is available from Lemma 2 of the sampling lesson. If a landing law λ\lambda is within ε\varepsilon in ℓ1\ell^1 of a law ν\nu, and ν(Ec)≤δ\nu(E^c)\leq\delta, then

λ(Ec)≤δ+ε/2. \lambda(E^c)\leq\delta+\varepsilon/2.

Indeed, the difference on any event is at most half the ℓ1\ell^1 distance. The next sections construct landing laws for deterministic orbits and make their composition exact.

A map that remembers unsuccessful entrance

Let DD be a nonempty subset of ℕ+\mathbb N_+, and let F:D→DF:D\to D. For a real threshold x≥1x\geq1, define

tx(n)=min⁡{j≥0:Fj(n)≤x}, t_x(n)=\min\{j\geq0:F^j(n)\leq x\},

with tx(n)=∞t_x(n)=\infty if there is no such time. This is an entrance time, so tx(n)=0t_x(n)=0 when n≤xn\leq x, unlike the strictly positive return time of the opening lesson.

Adjoin one symbol †∉D\dagger\notin D, and write D̂=D∪{†}\widehat D=D\cup\{\dagger\}. Define

px:D̂→D̂,px(n)={Ftx(n)(n),tx(n)<∞,†,tx(n)=∞,px(†)=†. p_x:\widehat D\longrightarrow\widehat D, \qquad p_x(n)=\begin{cases} F^{t_x(n)}(n),&t_x(n)<\infty,\\ \dagger,&t_x(n)=\infty, \end{cases} \quad p_x(\dagger)=\dagger.

The image is contained in (D∩[1,x])∪{†}(D\cap[1,x])\cup\{\dagger\}, and each point of this set is fixed. The symbol †\dagger is not an extra orbit value of FF. It records a failure to enter the chosen target. A successful landing at the integer 1 and unsuccessful entrance therefore remain different outcomes.

For example, on D={1,2,3,4,5,6}D=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}, take

F(1)=1,F(2)=1,F(3)=2,F(4)=3,F(5)=6,F(6)=5. F(1)=1,\quad F(2)=1,\quad F(3)=2,\quad F(4)=3,\quad F(5)=6,\quad F(6)=5.

At threshold 2, the first four starting points have passage values 1,2,2,21,2,2,2, and the last two have passage value †\dagger. At threshold 4, the first four are fixed by p4p_4, while 5 and 6 still give †\dagger. This example allows unsuccessful entrance without any unresolved conjecture.

Nested targets compose exactly

Theorem 1 (Nested passage). For 1≤x≤y1\leq x\leq y,

px∘py=px=py∘px. p_x\circ p_y=p_x=p_y\circ p_x.

If tx(n)<∞t_x(n)<\infty, then

tx(n)=ty(n)+tx(py(n)), t_x(n)=t_y(n)+t_x(p_y(n)),

and for every k≥0k\geq0,

Fk(px(n))=Fk+tx(py(n))(py(n)). F^k(p_x(n))=F^{k+t_x(p_y(n))}(p_y(n)).

Proof. If an orbit never reaches a value at most yy, it cannot reach one at most xx, so both sides of pxpy=pxp_xp_y=p_x are †\dagger. Otherwise, all states before its first entrance below yy are greater than yy, hence greater than xx. The first entrance below xx, if one exists, is found by continuing from py(n)p_y(n). This proves the time identity and pxpy=pxp_xp_y=p_x, including the case where the continued search fails. The other composition identity holds because a successful value of pxp_x is already at most yy, and †\dagger is fixed. Finally, iterating from the two points on the same trajectory proves the tail identity. ∎

Taking x=yx=y shows that pxp_x is idempotent: applying it twice gives the same answer as applying it once. It is a retraction onto its image, not generally a one-to-one coordinate change.

What the induced operator preserves and loses

For a summable signed mass function λ\lambda on D̂\widehat D, define

Kxλ=(px)*λ,(Kxλ)(z)=∑n:px(n)=zλ(n). K_x\lambda=(p_x)_*\lambda, \qquad (K_x\lambda)(z)=\sum_{n:p_x(n)=z}\lambda(n).

Proposition 1 of the sampling lesson proves that KxK_x preserves total mass and nonnegativity, and that ∥Kxλ∥1≤∥λ∥1\|K_x\lambda\|_1\leq\|\lambda\|_1. Theorem 1 and composition of pushforwards give

KxKy=Kx=KyKx(x≤y),Kx2=Kx. K_xK_y=K_x=K_yK_x\quad(x\leq y),\qquad K_x^2=K_x.

The kernel consists exactly of the signed masses that sum to zero on every fibre of pxp_x. This follows directly from the displayed definition. If two starts have the same landing point, the difference of their unit point masses lies in this kernel. The operator retains the distribution of landings, but not how mass was divided among starts with a common landing.

Conversely, any summable signed mass supported on (D∩[1,x])∪{†}(D\cap[1,x])\cup\{\dagger\} is fixed by KxK_x, since every point of that set is fixed. Every output is supported there. Thus this is exactly the image, and exactly the set of fixed vectors, of KxK_x. For an initial probability μ\mu on DD,

(Kxμ)(†)=μ{n:tx(n)=∞}. (K_x\mu)(\dagger)=\mu\{n:t_x(n)=\infty\}.

No probability has been discarded by excluding unsuccessful trajectories.

Adding errors over a finite chain

Let 1≤x0≤⋯≤xJ1\leq x_0\leq\cdots\leq x_J. Suppose νi\nu_i is a probability law supported on (D∩[1,xi])∪{†}(D\cap[1,x_i])\cup\{\dagger\}. Equivalently, Kxiνi=νiK_{x_i}\nu_i=\nu_i. Define

ei=∥νi−Kxiνi+1∥1(0≤i<J). e_i=\|\nu_i-K_{x_i}\nu_{i+1}\|_1\qquad(0\leq i<J).

These errors compare two laws on the same target: the chosen law νi\nu_i, and the law obtained by transporting the next one down to that target.

Theorem 2 (Finite-chain transport).

∥ν0−Kx0νJ∥1≤∑i=0J−1ei. \|\nu_0-K_{x_0}\nu_J\|_1\leq\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}e_i.

In particular,

(Kx0νJ)(†)≤ν0(†)+12∑i=0J−1ei. (K_{x_0}\nu_J)(\dagger) \leq\nu_0(\dagger)+\frac12\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}e_i.

Proof. For J=0J=0, the first expression is zero because Kx0ν0=ν0K_{x_0}\nu_0=\nu_0. For J≥1J\geq1, use the exact telescoping identity

ν0−Kx0νJ=∑i=0J−1Kx0(νi−Kxiνi+1). \nu_0-K_{x_0}\nu_J =\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}K_{x_0}(\nu_i-K_{x_i}\nu_{i+1}).

To verify it, replace each Kx0KxiK_{x_0}K_{x_i} by Kx0K_{x_0}, cancel consecutive terms, and use Kx0ν0=ν0K_{x_0}\nu_0=\nu_0. Contraction and the triangle inequality give the norm bound. The second bound is its consequence for the event {†}\{\dagger\}, using Lemma 2 of the sampling lesson. ∎

The number of steps matters only through the sum of the errors. Exact compatibility would make every eie_i zero. Small errors that remain nonsummable can accumulate, whereas summable errors can control arbitrarily long chains.

Geometrically separated logarithmic scales

We now specify a family of blocks rather than a single chain. Fix α>1\alpha>1. For a positive real ss, whenever D∩[s,sα]D\cap[s,s^\alpha] is nonempty, let μs\mu_s be a probability supported there. Assume there is s*≥2s_*\geq2 such that these measures are defined for every real s≥s*s\geq s_*. No particular choice of weights within a block is assumed.

For s≥s*s\geq s_*, put

νs=Ksμsα,d(s)=νs(†),e(s)=∥νs−Ksνsα∥1. \nu_s=K_s\mu_{s^\alpha},\qquad d(s)=\nu_s(\dagger),\qquad e(s)=\|\nu_s-K_s\nu_{s^\alpha}\|_1.

Thus νs\nu_s is the first-passage law at threshold ss for starts in the block [sα,sα2][s^\alpha,s^{\alpha^2}]. The error e(s)e(s) compares it with passage from the next block, whose starts lie in [sα2,sα3][s^{\alpha^2},s^{\alpha^3}]. The intervening entrance is harmless because Theorem 1 composes nested passage maps exactly.

For R≥s*R\geq s_*, define the possibly infinite quantity

ℰ(R)=supu≥R(d(u)+∑i=0∞e(uαi)). \mathcal E(R)=\sup_{u\geq R} \left(d(u)+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}e(u^{\alpha^i})\right).

The thresholds u,uα,uα2,…u,u^\alpha,u^{\alpha^2},\ldots have logarithms log⁡u,αlog⁡u,α2log⁡u,…\log u,\alpha\log u,\alpha^2\log u,\ldots. This geometric growth of the logarithms is what makes logarithmic error estimates summable.

For each n∈Dn\in D, define Fmin(n)=min⁡j≥0Fj(n)F_{\min}(n)=\min_{j\geq0}F^j(n). Well-ordering supplies the minimum even for an unbounded orbit. A family of these positive-integer-valued random variables is uniformly tight when, for each ε>0\varepsilon>0, a finite KK makes the probability of Fmin>KF_{\min}>K at most ε\varepsilon for every law in the family.

Theorem 3 (Summable transport controls orbit minima). For every K≥s*α3K\geq s_*^{\alpha^3} and every defined block law μs\mu_s,

μs{n:Fmin(n)>K}≤ℰ(K1/α3). \mu_s\{n:F_{\min}(n)>K\} \leq\mathcal E(K^{1/\alpha^3}).

Consequently, if ℰ(R)→0\mathcal E(R)\to0 as R→∞R\to\infty, the orbit-minimum laws under all the block measures are uniformly tight.

Proof. If sα≤Ks^\alpha\leq K, every start in the block is already at most KK, and the left side is zero. Otherwise s>K1/α>1s>K^{1/\alpha}>1. K1/α2≤y<K1/α. K^{1/\alpha^2}\leq y<K^{1/\alpha}.

u≥K1/α3≥s*,u<K,uαJ=s1/α. u\geq K^{1/\alpha^3}\geq s_*,\qquad u<K,\qquad u^{\alpha^J}=s^{1/\alpha}.

νxJ=Ks1/αμs,KuνxJ=Kuμs. \nu_{x_J}=K_{s^{1/\alpha}}\mu_s, \qquad K_u\nu_{x_J}=K_u\mu_s.

(Kuμs)(†)≤d(u)+12∑i=0J−1e(uαi)≤ℰ(K1/α3). (K_u\mu_s)(\dagger) \leq d(u)+\frac12\sum_{i=0}^{J-1}e(u^{\alpha^i}) \leq\mathcal E(K^{1/\alpha^3}).

The factors α,α2,α3\alpha,\alpha^2,\alpha^3 in this proof locate the original block, its lower comparison scale, and a threshold at which the failure probability is controlled. They are not interchangeable scale labels.

Two explicit summable error estimates

Suppose the established error bounds for a particular map and choice of block laws are

d(s)≤Ds−b,e(s)≤E(log⁡s)−c(s≥s*), d(s)\leq Ds^{-b},\qquad e(s)\leq E(\log s)^{-c} \quad(s\geq s_*),

where D,E,b,c>0D,E,b,c>0 are fixed. Since log⁡(uαi)=αilog⁡u\log(u^{\alpha^i})=\alpha^i\log u, summing a geometric series yields

ℰ(R)≤DR−b+E1−α−c(log⁡R)−c. \mathcal E(R)\leq DR^{-b} +\frac{E}{1-\alpha^{-c}}(\log R)^{-c}.

To verify the infinite geometric sum, its first J+1J+1 terms equal (1−α−c(J+1))/(1−α−c)(1-\alpha^{-c(J+1)})/(1-\alpha^{-c}); the omitted power tends to zero. Substitution into Theorem 3 gives the fully explicit bound

μs{Fmin>K}≤DK−b/α3+Eα3c1−α−c(log⁡K)−c. \mu_s\{F_{\min}>K\} \leq D K^{-b/\alpha^3} +\frac{E\alpha^{3c}}{1-\alpha^{-c}}(\log K)^{-c}.

A slower error can also be summable. If e(s)≤E(log⁡log⁡s)−1−ce(s)\leq E(\log\log s)^{-1-c} beyond a fixed threshold, let v=log⁡log⁡R>0v=\log\log R>0 and h=log⁡α>0h=\log\alpha>0. The decreasing function (v+ht)−1−c(v+ht)^{-1-c} gives

∑i=0∞(v+ih)−1−c≤v−1−c+∫0∞(v+ht)−1−cdt=v−1−c+v−cch. \sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(v+ih)^{-1-c} \leq v^{-1-c}+\int_0^\infty(v+ht)^{-1-c}\,dt =v^{-1-c}+\frac{v^{-c}}{ch}.

The inequality follows by comparing the ii-th term, for i≥1i\geq1, with the integral on [i−1,i][i-1,i]; the integral is evaluated by the substitution z=v+htz=v+ht. Together with a failure bound tending uniformly to zero on tails, this again proves tightness. Tao notes this slower sufficient error regime after his first-passage proposition.

The role of the Collatz application

For the Syracuse map, take D=𝒪D=\mathcal O and F(m)=(3m+1)/2ν2(3m+1)F(m)=(3m+1)/2^{\nu_2(3m+1)}. Logarithmic block laws assign an odd m∈[s,sα]m\in[s,s^\alpha] weight proportional to 1/m1/m. The constructions in this lesson then apply literally: pxp_x is its first odd iterate at most xx, and KxK_x transports the chosen starting distribution to that landing distribution.

The algebra of nested passage and the transport theorem are now available. Applying the quantitative conclusion requires estimates for the actual d(s)d(s) and e(s)e(s) of these laws. In Tao’s argument, this is where mixing, finite-group Fourier analysis and renewal estimates enter. Those are substantial further arguments; the general transport theorem does not supply their bounds. Conversely, once suitable bounds are proved, the deduction from them to tight orbit minima is the complete argument above, not a separate appeal to the conjecture.

The clock and sampling lessons identify how to transfer the resulting orbit-minimum events back to ordinary positive-integer iteration with the corresponding logarithmic weights. A time bound needs additional clock estimates, because a small landing value alone gives no upper bound for the number of elapsed ordinary steps.

Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1. In the six-state example, start with the uniform law. Compute K2μK_2\mu, including the failure mass, and verify K2K4μ=K2μK_2K_4\mu=K_2\mu.

Solution. The masses are 1/61/6 at 1, 3/63/6 at 2, and 2/62/6 at †\dagger. At threshold 4, the law has mass 1/61/6 at each of 1, 2, 3 and 4, and 2/62/6 at †\dagger. Applying K2K_2 sends 3 and 4 to 2 and fixes the other outputs, giving exactly the same law.

Exercise 2. For any summable signed mass λ\lambda, prove that

λ=Kxλ+(λ−Kxλ) \lambda=K_x\lambda+(\lambda-K_x\lambda)

is a unique decomposition into a fixed vector of KxK_x and a vector in its kernel.

Solution. Idempotence gives Kx(Kxλ)=KxλK_x(K_x\lambda)=K_x\lambda and Kx(λ−Kxλ)=0K_x(\lambda-K_x\lambda)=0. If a vector is both fixed and in the kernel, it equals zero. Subtracting two decompositions therefore proves uniqueness. This describes precisely which part of a signed distribution survives passage and which part is cancelled within fibres.

Exercise 3. In the first explicit error regime, take α=2\alpha=2, D=E=b=c=1D=E=b=c=1. State the bound for orbit minima, including its range of validity.

Solution. If the stated input estimates hold for every s≥s*s\geq s_*, then for every K≥s*8K\geq s_*^8 and every defined block law,

μs{Fmin>K}≤K−1/8+16log⁡K. \mu_s\{F_{\min}>K\}\leq K^{-1/8}+\frac{16}{\log K}.

The coefficient is 23/(1−1/2)=162^3/(1-1/2)=16. Probabilities are also at most 1, so the smaller of 1 and this expression is a valid bound. This numerical exercise does not assert that these input constants hold for the Syracuse map.

Exercise 4. Does e(s)≤E/(log⁡log⁡s)e(s)\leq E/(\log\log s), by itself, provide a finite sum of the displayed upper bounds along the scales uαiu^{\alpha^i}?

Solution. No. The upper bounds form E∑i(v+ih)−1E\sum_i(v+ih)^{-1}, where v=log⁡log⁡u>0v=\log\log u>0 and h=log⁡α>0h=\log\alpha>0. For sufficiently large ii, v+ih≤2hiv+ih\leq2hi, so this series is bounded below by a positive multiple of ∑i1/i\sum_i1/i, which diverges. The harmonic series diverges because the terms from 2j2^j through 2j+1−12^{j+1}-1 sum to at least 1/21/2 for each jj. This proves only that the proposed upper bound is not summable. The actual errors might still be much smaller; no failure of tightness has been proved.

What carries forward

The four lessons have connected several different kinds of information. Return clocks specify how much time a shorter orbit record omits. Symbolic words give exact affine blocks and their arithmetic domains. Pushforward measures determine how mass moves when starts are identified. First-passage operators then compose these distributional comparisons across scales.

Each construction can be studied for other systems with its hypotheses stated explicitly. The Collatz example keeps their interaction concrete: an orbit identity, a congruence count and a probability estimate each do a different part of the work. Further study can now focus on proving mixing or renewal estimates, with a precise understanding of what those estimates must control and how their conclusions will be used.
