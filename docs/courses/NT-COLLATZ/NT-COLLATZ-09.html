Conditional cancellation and arithmetic phase geometry

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs, exercises and illustration: CC0 1.0.

An average of complex numbers of modulus one can be small because their directions disagree. Taking absolute values before averaging destroys precisely that information. A useful alternative is to average a small part of the randomness first, keeping the rest fixed. Sometimes this small conditional average can be calculated exactly. The remaining question then becomes a problem about how often a random path reaches positions where the calculation gives a definite loss.

We develop this method for random affine sums on a finite cyclic group. Pairing two waiting times produces an exact cosine factor. The positions where this factor is close to one form separated triangular sets of lattice points. We prove that arithmetic geometry in full, including the boundary and separation estimates. It explains why the renewal paths from the preceding lessons are relevant to a Fourier coefficient rather than merely an unrelated probability model.

The prerequisites are complex exponentials, finite cyclic groups, conditional probabilities on countable sets, and elementary logarithms. Quotient distributions and Fourier mixing introduces the affine sum and explains which Fourier estimates matter for mixing. Renewal paths and first-crossing locations proves the independence of the marked blocks used at the end. Basic references are Keith Conrad’s Characters of finite abelian groups, Section 4, and Terence Tao’s Almost all orbits of the Collatz map attain almost bounded values, Section 7. The pairing and triangle mechanism here is Tao’s; the exposition and all arguments needed below are supplied independently.

Characters on fractions with powers of two as denominators

Fix an integer n≥2n\geq2, put Q=3nQ=3^n, and choose an integer ξ\xi not divisible by three. Let

D=ℤ[1/2]={a/2k:a∈ℤ,k≥0}. D=\mathbb Z[1/2]=\{a/2^k:a\in\mathbb Z,\ k\geq0\}.

The integer u=(Q+1)/2u=(Q+1)/2 satisfies 2u≡1(mod⁡Q)2u\equiv1\pmod Q. Thus

ρ:D→ℤ/Qℤ,ρ(a/2k)=auk(mod⁡Q) \rho:D\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/Q\mathbb Z, \qquad \rho(a/2^k)=au^k\pmod{Q} \tag{1} (1)

is well defined. Indeed, equality of two fractions gives a2h=b2ka2^h=b2^k; multiplication by the inverse of 2k+h2^{k+h} modulo QQ gives auk=buhau^k=bu^h. Using a common denominator proves that ρ\rho preserves addition and multiplication. It is onto because it includes the reduction of every integer. Its kernel is QDQD: since uu is a unit, auk=0(mod⁡Q)au^k=0\pmod Q holds exactly when Q∣aQ\mid a. Two fractions have the same image exactly when their difference lies in this kernel.

Define the additive character

χ(x)=exp⁡(−2πiξρ(x)/Q),x∈D. \chi(x)=\exp\bigl(-2\pi i\xi\rho(x)/Q\bigr),\qquad x\in D. \tag{2} (2)

Changing the representative of ρ(x)\rho(x) does not change this expression. Addition in DD becomes multiplication of character values. Because ξ\xi is a unit modulo QQ, the kernel of χ\chi is again QDQD; its image consists of all QQ-th roots of unity. This identifies exactly what the character forgets. It does not replace a rational number by its usual real fractional part.

For example, modulo nine the image of 1/21/2 is five, not the real number 1/21/2. For ξ=1\xi=1, its character is exp⁡(−2πi5/9)\exp(-2\pi i5/9). Negative powers of two below always have the meaning (1) when used in a residue or character.

Pairing waiting times before taking absolute values

Let A1,…,AnA_1,\ldots,A_n be independent positive integers with ℙ(Ai=a)=2−a\mathbb P(A_i=a)=2^{-a}. Write Si=A1+⋯+AiS_i=A_1+\cdots+A_i, and consider

Xn=∑i=1n3i−12−Si∈D,Fn(ξ)=𝔼χ(Xn). X_n=\sum_{i=1}^n3^{i-1}2^{-S_i}\in D, \qquad F_n(\xi)=\mathbb E\chi(X_n). \tag{3} (3)

The law of ρ(Xn)\rho(X_n) is the independent-word offset model of the Fourier lesson. There the deterministic orbit expansion has its exponents in the opposite order. Reversing a length-nn word is a bijection, its own inverse, and preserves its probability 2−Sn2^{-S_n}. This proves equality of the two random offset laws. It is not a claim that a particular integer orbit has independent exponents.

Put m=⌊n/2⌋m=\lfloor n/2\rfloor, Bj=A2j−1+A2jB_j=A_{2j-1}+A_{2j}, and Tj=B1+⋯+BjT_j=B_1+\cdots+B_j. The variables BjB_j are independent, with

Proposition 1 (Conditional product). Set xj=32j−22−Tjx_j=3^{2j-2}2^{-T_j}. Then

Fn(ξ)={𝔼∏j=1mf(xj,Bj),n=2m,𝔼[∏j=1mf(xj,Bj)g(3n−12−Tm)],n=2m+1. F_n(\xi)= \begin{cases} \mathbb E\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^m f(x_j,B_j),&n=2m,\\ \mathbb E\left[\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^m f(x_j,B_j) \,g(3^{n-1}2^{-T_m})\right],&n=2m+1. \end{cases} \tag{7} (7)

Proof. Condition on the vector of sums (B1,…,Bm)(B_1,\ldots,B_m). Formula (6) and the character identity turn the exponential of a sum into a product. The triangle inequality and |g|≤1|g|\leq1 prove (8). All regroupings are of absolutely summable terms, since the absolute values are bounded by the underlying probability masses. ∎

The order of operations matters. Replacing every original character in (3) by its modulus would give only |Fn(ξ)|≤1|F_n(\xi)|\leq1. The conditional average retains disagreement between the directions arising from different splits of the same sum.

A phase that measures the cancellation

For every (j,l)∈{1,…,m}×ℤ(j,l)\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\times\mathbb Z, let θ(j,l)\theta(j,l) be the unique number in (−1/2,1/2](-1/2,1/2] congruent modulo one to

Fix 0<ε≤e−100<\varepsilon\leq e^{-10}. Call a lattice position low-phase when |θ(j,l)|≤ε|\theta(j,l)|\leq\varepsilon, and cancelling otherwise. These are the black and white sets, respectively, in Tao’s terminology. Define

Corollary 2 (An occupation bound for the Fourier coefficient).

|Fn(ξ)|≤𝔼e−2ε2Nm≤𝔼e−ε3Nm. |F_n(\xi)|\leq\mathbb E e^{-2\varepsilon^2N_m} \leq\mathbb E e^{-\varepsilon^3N_m}. \tag{12} (12)

Proof. At each counted position, (10)–(11) bound its factor in (8) by e−2ε2e^{-2\varepsilon^2}. All other factors are at most one. Multiply these bounds for each realised vector of sums and then average. Since 2ε2≥ε32\varepsilon^2\geq\varepsilon^3, the second inequality follows. ∎

This is an upper bound, not an equality: cancellation between different vectors of pair sums may reduce the original coefficient further. The nonnegative expectation is useful because it records how rarely the path can avoid many definite losses. A large mean of NmN_m alone does not bound this expectation; Exercise 4 explains why.

Small phases can be lifted from the circle to the real line

The arithmetic in (9) implies

θ(j+1,l)≡9θ(j,l),θ(j,l−1)≡2θ(j,l)(mod⁡1). \theta(j+1,l)\equiv9\theta(j,l),\qquad \theta(j,l-1)\equiv2\theta(j,l)\pmod{1}. \tag{13} (13)

These are congruences, not unconditional real equalities. When the indicated real multiple has absolute value less than 1/21/2, it is already the centred representative and equality does hold. For nonnegative integers a,ba,b, whenever the positions lie in the strip,

θ(j+a,l−b)≡9a2bθ(j,l)(mod⁡1). \theta(j+a,l-b)\equiv9^a2^b\theta(j,l)\pmod{1}. \tag{14} (14)

|θ(j,l)|≥3−(n−2j+2). |\theta(j,l)|\geq3^{-(n-2j+2)}. \tag{15} (15)

In particular a low-phase point satisfies

j≤n2+1−log⁡(1/ε)2log⁡3≤n2−110log⁡(1/ε). j\leq\frac n2+1-\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{2\log3} \leq\frac n2-\frac1{10}\log(1/\varepsilon). \tag{16} (16)

To control the other boundaries, call a point small when its phase has absolute value at most h=1/100h=1/100. Every low-phase point is small. We need three elementary rules.

Lemma 3 (Local lifting rules). Provided all stated positions are in the strip:

  1. A small point whose right or lower neighbour is low-phase is itself low-phase.
  2. If the right and lower neighbours of a point are small, that point is small.
  3. If the left and lower neighbours of a point are small, that point is small.

Proof. Write u=θ(j,l)u=\theta(j,l). For rule 1, |u|≤h|u|\leq h makes both 9u9u and 2u2u lie strictly between −1/2-1/2 and 1/21/2. The corresponding equation in (13) is real equality. A low-phase neighbour therefore gives |u|≤ε/9|u|\leq\varepsilon/9 or ε/2\varepsilon/2.

For rule 2, write v=θ(j+1,l)v=\theta(j+1,l), w=θ(j,l−1)w=\theta(j,l-1). Equations (13) give u≡v−4w(mod⁡1)u\equiv v-4w\pmod1. Since |v−4w|≤5h<1/2|v-4w|\leq5h<1/2, this is real equality, and |u|≤5h|u|\leq5h. Now |2u|≤10h<1/2|2u|\leq10h<1/2, so w=2uw=2u, giving |u|≤h/2|u|\leq h/2.

For rule 3, the small left neighbour gives |u|≤9h|u|\leq9h, with real equality in the multiplication by nine. Since |2u|≤18h<1/2|2u|\leq18h<1/2, the small lower neighbour is again exactly 2u2u; thus |u|≤h/2|u|\leq h/2. ∎

These rules justify propagation of smallness, not just an informal picture of nearby angles. Multiplication on the circle can otherwise return a large phase to a small one. That possibility is exactly why a separation proof is needed.

The low-phase set consists of separated triangles

From pair indices to a renewal occupation count

Continue the independent sequence B1,B2,…B_1,B_2,\ldots with law (4), marking each occurrence of three. The probability of a mark is q(3)=1/4q(3)=1/4. With probability one there are arbitrarily many marks: for each fixed index kk, the probability of no mark among the next rr entries is (3/4)r(3/4)^r, which tends to zero; the union of these zero-probability events over kk still has probability zero.

Let (Jr,Lr)(J_r,L_r) be the number of entries and the sum of entries in the rr-th block ending at a mark. Set

(Ur,Vr)=∑i=1r(Ji,Li). (U_r,V_r)=\sum_{i=1}^r(J_i,L_i).

Cutting an entry word immediately after each mark is inverse to concatenating its marked blocks. The marked-block argument in the preceding lesson proves that these vectors are independent copies of the holding-time law, including all fibres of the map from words to vectors. The marked positions are exactly (Ur,Vr)(U_r,V_r). Therefore, with WW denoting the cancelling positions,

Nm=∑r≥1𝟏{Ur≤m}𝟏W(Ur,Vr). N_m=\sum_{r\geq1}\mathbf1_{\{U_r\leq m\}} \mathbf1_W(U_r,V_r). \tag{21} (21)

The sum has at most mm nonzero terms, because Jr≥1J_r\geq1. This is an exact change of the count’s clock, not a new assumption about independent visits to WW. The visits are generally dependent.

Local probabilities for lattice sums, Proposition 6 proves 𝔼(Jr,Lr)=(4,16)\mathbb E(J_r,L_r)=(4,16). Its mean direction thus has slope four, whereas the triangle diagonal has slope log⁡9/log⁡2<4\log9/\log2<4, since 9<169<16. The first-crossing estimate controls where a path passes a horizontal boundary. Together with Theorem 4, these are the ingredients for estimating losses after leaving low-phase regions. The slope comparison explains the geometry, but is not by itself an estimate on the random occupation count.

More precisely, for any λ>0\lambda>0 and integer K≥0K\geq0, splitting according to Nm≤KN_m\leq K gives

𝔼e−λNm≤ℙ(Nm≤K)+e−λK. \mathbb E e^{-\lambda N_m} \leq\mathbb P(N_m\leq K)+e^{-\lambda K}. \tag{22} (22)

Thus a Fourier-decay proof needs control of paths with few cancelling encounters. Tao’s remaining renewal argument supplies quantitative control of this kind. Its repeated-encounter estimates are the next mathematical step, not a consequence of the number of triangles or the mean slope alone. Equations (12) and (21) specify exactly the nonnegative quantity that those estimates must bound.

1. A character on dyadic fractions

4. Why an expected number of visits is insufficient

Construct nonnegative integer random variables ZmZ_m with 𝔼Zm→∞\mathbb EZ_m\to\infty, but 𝔼e−λZm↛0\mathbb E e^{-\lambda Z_m}\not\to0 for every fixed λ>0\lambda>0. For comparison, show that independent Bernoulli visits with common success probability p>0p>0 give an exponentially decaying expectation.

Solution. Let ZmZ_m equal zero and 2m2m, each with probability 1/21/2. Its mean is mm, but 𝔼e−λZm=(1+e−2λm)/2→1/2\mathbb E e^{-\lambda Z_m}=(1+e^{-2\lambda m})/2\to1/2. If instead Zm=I1+⋯+ImZ_m=I_1+\cdots+I_m, with independent Bernoulli variables of parameter pp, then independence gives

𝔼e−λZm=(1−p+pe−λ)m≤exp⁡[−mp(1−e−λ)]. \mathbb E e^{-\lambda Z_m} =(1-p+pe^{-\lambda})^m \leq\exp[-mp(1-e^{-\lambda})].

The last inequality is 1−x≤e−x1-x\leq e^{-x}. The renewal indicators in (21) do not have this independence, so the comparison identifies a useful mechanism but does not prove the required estimate for them.

What carries forward

Conditional cancellation converts a complex average into a nonnegative occupation estimate while retaining a precisely defined phase. Arithmetic lifting then shows that the exceptional positions have a rigid geometry: finite triangular pieces, a definite buffer between pieces, and a terminal strip free of them. The marked-block map identifies the count with visits of the same two-coordinate renewal process whose first-crossing law was already proved.

This places the argument at the intersection of harmonic analysis, conditional probability and renewal theory. The next task is quantitative: combine exit-location bounds and triangle separation to control paths with too few cancelling visits, and then combine Fourier decay with the arithmetic separation of prefixes in the mixing estimate. The exact reductions here explain both what those estimates accomplish and why a finite orbit calculation cannot replace them.
