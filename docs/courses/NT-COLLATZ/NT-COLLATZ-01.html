Return maps and exact changes of clock

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, exercises and diagram: CC0 1.0.

When studying an iterated rule, we need not record every step. We can record successive visits to a chosen set, keeping the times between visits. This produces a return map. The central questions are what the shorter record preserves, how to recover the original clock, and what is lost if the waiting times are discarded.

We will prove the general return-clock formula, then work it out completely for a map on positive integers. The Collatz rule halves an even number and replaces an odd number by three times that number plus one. Starting with 3 gives

3→10→5→16→8→4→2→1. 3\longrightarrow10\longrightarrow5\longrightarrow16 \longrightarrow8\longrightarrow4\longrightarrow2\longrightarrow1.

There are good reasons to record fewer of these steps. One can combine an odd step with the division by two that necessarily follows it. One can also record only the odd numbers. These choices produce different maps and different notions of elapsed time. They do not change the least value attained along the orbit.

This example makes the general distinction visible: counting returns is not the same as counting individual steps. We will identify the corresponding times, reconstruct every omitted state, and determine why the least value survives this particular change of record. The last property depends on the values inside the omitted blocks; it is not automatic for return maps.

We assume induction, arithmetic of positive integers and the well-ordering principle: every nonempty set of positive integers has a least member. The divisibility facts needed below are proved here. No probability or assumption that Collatz orbits reach 1 is needed. Tao’s Almost all orbits of the Collatz map attain almost bounded values, §1.2, is a reference for the three maps and the orbit-minimum identity. Terras’s A stopping time problem on the positive integers is an earlier reference for the shortened iteration. The arguments below give the clock conversion in full, independently of any density theorem.

Recording visits instead of steps

Let XX be a set, let F:X→XF:X\to X, and choose a subset A⊆XA\subseteq X. Define F0(x)=xF^0(x)=x and Fj+1(x)=F(Fj(x))F^{j+1}(x)=F(F^j(x)). For a∈Aa\in A, its first positive return time is

τ(a)=min⁡{r≥1:Fr(a)∈A}, \tau(a)=\min\{r\geq1:F^r(a)\in A\},

when that set is nonempty. Suppose for this section that it is nonempty for every a∈Aa\in A. The induced map is then the well-defined map

R:A→A,R(a)=Fτ(a)(a). R:A\longrightarrow A,\qquad R(a)=F^{\tau(a)}(a).

Fix a∈Aa\in A and set

Θ0=0,Θj=∑i=0j−1τ(Ri(a)). \Theta_0=0,\qquad \Theta_j=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\tau(R^i(a)).

Theorem A (Return-clock formula). For every j≥0j\geq0,

FΘj(a)=Rj(a). F^{\Theta_j}(a)=R^j(a).

The map j↦Θjj\mapsto\Theta_j is a strictly increasing bijection from the nonnegative integers onto the times tt for which Ft(a)∈AF^t(a)\in A. Its inverse counts the earlier visits to AA. Every time t≥0t\geq0 has a unique representation

t=Θj+r,0≤r<τ(Rj(a)), t=\Theta_j+r,\qquad 0\leq r<\tau(R^j(a)),

and at that time Ft(a)=Fr(Rj(a))F^t(a)=F^r(R^j(a)).

Proof. The formula at j=0j=0 is the identity. If it holds at jj, then applying Fτ(Rj(a))F^{\tau(R^j(a))} proves it at j+1j+1. The definition of first return excludes visits strictly between these two times. All return times are positive integers, so Θj≥j\Theta_j\geq j. Thus the listed times increase without bound, and their half-open intervening intervals partition the nonnegative integers. This proves the claimed description of every visit, the unique representation of tt, and the state formula. Exactly jj earlier visits precede Θj\Theta_j, giving the inverse. ∎

This reconstruction uses the original rule FF. The abstract data RR and τ\tau alone do not specify the intermediate states of a different, otherwise unknown system. Likewise, the assumption that every point of AA returns must be justified in an application. It is not an assumption that every trajectory eventually enters any smaller target set.

For a first example, let X={0,1,2,3,4,5}X=\{0,1,2,3,4,5\} and let FF add 1 modulo 6. Choose A={0,2,5}A=\{0,2,5\}. The induced orbit is 0→2→5→00\to2\to5\to0, and the return times at those points are 2,3,12,3,1. Its accumulated times are 0,2,5,6,8,11,…0,2,5,6,8,11,\ldots, not 0,1,2,3,…0,1,2,3,\ldots. Theorem A recovers the six-step original cycle from the three-return cycle.

Even a minimum can be lost by recording visits. For the cycle 4→1→7→44\to1\to7\to4, recording only A={4,7}A=\{4,7\} changes the least recorded value from 1 to 4. Below we will prove that the even blocks in the Collatz example have a special property that prevents this loss.

Odd parts and powers of two

Write ℕ+={1,2,3,…}\mathbb N_+=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}, ℕ0={0,1,2,…}\mathbb N_0=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}, and 𝒪={1,3,5,…}\mathcal O=\{1,3,5,\ldots\}. An integer is odd when it is not divisible by 2.

Lemma 1. Each n∈ℕ+n\in\mathbb N_+ has a unique expression

n=2am,a∈ℕ0,m∈𝒪. n=2^a m,\qquad a\in\mathbb N_0,\quad m\in\mathcal O.

Proof. If nn is even, divide it by 2 and repeat while the quotient is even. Every division strictly decreases a positive integer, so the procedure must terminate. Equivalently, infinitely many divisions would give an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers; its set of values would have a least element, followed by a smaller one, contradicting well-ordering. At termination the quotient mm is odd. If there were two expressions 2am=2bu2^a m=2^b u with m,um,u odd and a<ba<b, cancellation would give m=2b−aum=2^{b-a}u, which is even. Interchanging the expressions rules out b<ab<a. Thus a=ba=b and m=um=u. ∎

The exponent aa is called the 2-adic valuation of nn, denoted ν2(n)\nu_2(n). The word “2-adic” here needs no additional number system: it just counts the factors of 2. The odd part is

π(n)=n2ν2(n). \pi(n)=\frac{n}{2^{\nu_2(n)}}.

For instance, 40=23⋅540=2^3\cdot5, so ν2(40)=3\nu_2(40)=3 and π(40)=5\pi(40)=5. Lemma 1 gives inverse bijections

ℕ0×𝒪→ℕ+,(a,m)↦2am,ℕ+→ℕ0×𝒪,n↦(ν2(n),π(n)). \begin{aligned} \mathbb N_0\times\mathcal O&\longrightarrow\mathbb N_+, &(a,m)&\longmapsto2^a m,\\ \mathbb N_+&\longrightarrow\mathbb N_0\times\mathcal O, &n&\longmapsto(\nu_2(n),\pi(n)). \end{aligned}

In particular, retaining both coordinates loses no information. Retaining only π(n)\pi(n) forgets the exponent: the fibre over an odd number mm is exactly {m,2m,4m,8m,…}\{m,2m,4m,8m,\ldots\}.

The three rules

The ordinary Collatz map C:ℕ+→ℕ+C:\mathbb N_+\to\mathbb N_+ is

C(n)={3n+1,n odd,n/2,n even. C(n)=\begin{cases}3n+1,&n\text{ odd},\\n/2,&n\text{ even}. \end{cases}

The shortcut map T:ℕ+→ℕ+T:\mathbb N_+\to\mathbb N_+ includes one division by 2 in every step:

T(n)={(3n+1)/2,n odd,n/2,n even. T(n)=\begin{cases}(3n+1)/2,&n\text{ odd},\\n/2,&n\text{ even}. \end{cases}

If n=2u+1n=2u+1, then 3n+1=6u+43n+1=6u+4 is even. Thus the shortcut rule always produces a positive integer. At an odd state it performs two ordinary steps; at an even state it performs one.

The Syracuse map S:𝒪→𝒪S:\mathcal O\to\mathcal O removes all factors of 2 after the odd step:

S(m)=3m+12ν2(3m+1). S(m)=\frac{3m+1}{2^{\nu_2(3m+1)}}.

Lemma 1 proves that this quotient is a positive odd integer. For odd mm, the exponent ν2(3m+1)\nu_2(3m+1) is at least 1. Thus every Syracuse step includes one multiplication by 3, the addition of 1, and a positive number of divisions by 2.

For a map FF, the notation F0(n)=nF^0(n)=n and Fk+1(n)=F(Fk(n))F^{k+1}(n)=F(F^k(n)) denotes iteration. The orbit is the indexed sequence of these values. Keeping the indices matters: S(1)=1S(1)=1, but the successive occurrences of 1 are distinct visits.

One odd return at a time

Lemma 2. Let mm be odd, put q=ν2(3m+1)q=\nu_2(3m+1), and put u=S(m)u=S(m). Then 3m+1=2qu3m+1=2^q u, and

Cr(m)=2q+1−ru(1≤r≤q+1), C^r(m)=2^{q+1-r}u\qquad(1\leq r\leq q+1),

whereas

Tr(m)=2q−ru(1≤r≤q). T^r(m)=2^{q-r}u\qquad(1\leq r\leq q).

The first odd state after mm is uu. It occurs after exactly q+1q+1 ordinary steps or qq shortcut steps.

Proof. The ordinary first step produces 2qu2^q u. Until the exponent becomes zero the number is even, so the rule divides it by 2. This proves the first formula successively for every displayed rr. It also proves that the intermediate states are even and the final one is odd. The shortcut first step produces 2q−1u2^{q-1}u; the same argument proves the second formula. If q=1q=1, the shortcut map reaches uu immediately, so there are no intermediate even shortcut states. ∎

Here the subset in Theorem A is 𝒪\mathcal O. Lemma 2 proves existence and gives the precise first-return time for every odd starting point: q+1q+1 for CC, and qq for TT. Consequently SS is the induced map of either rule on 𝒪\mathcal O. We have verified the return hypothesis directly, without making any assertion about reaching 1.

Exact conversion between the clocks

Fix a positive integer N=2amN=2^a m with mm odd. Define

mj=Sj(m),qj=ν2(3mj+1), m_j=S^j(m),\qquad q_j=\nu_2(3m_j+1),

and define the accumulated number of halvings by

Q0=0,Qj=∑i=0j−1qi(j≥1). Q_0=0,\qquad Q_j=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}q_i\quad(j\geq1).

There are initially aa halvings to reach mm. Each subsequent odd return contributes qjq_j shortcut steps, but qj+1q_j+1 ordinary steps. This gives the following exact statement.

Theorem 3. For every j∈ℕ0j\in\mathbb N_0,

Ta+Qj(N)=mj=Ca+j+Qj(N). T^{a+Q_j}(N)=m_j=C^{a+j+Q_j}(N).

Moreover, the maps

θT:ℕ0→{t≥0:Tt(N) is odd},j↦a+Qj,θC:ℕ0→{t≥0:Ct(N) is odd},j↦a+j+Qj \begin{aligned} \theta_T:\mathbb N_0&\longrightarrow\{t\geq0:T^t(N)\text{ is odd}\}, &j&\longmapsto a+Q_j,\\ \theta_C:\mathbb N_0&\longrightarrow\{t\geq0:C^t(N)\text{ is odd}\}, &j&\longmapsto a+j+Q_j \end{aligned}

are strictly increasing bijections. For F=CF=C or F=TF=T, the inverse on its indicated domain is

θF−1(t)=#{h:0≤h<t,Fh(N) is odd}. \theta_F^{-1}(t)= \#\{h:0\leq h<t,\ F^h(N)\text{ is odd}\}.

Thus the inverse counts earlier odd visits, not distinct odd values.

Proof. For 0≤t≤a0\leq t\leq a, both trajectories are 2a−tm2^{a-t}m. Their first odd visit is at t=at=a, which proves the formulas for j=0j=0. Suppose an odd visit to mjm_j has been located. Lemma 2 places the next odd visit to mj+1m_{j+1} exactly qjq_j shortcut steps or qj+1q_j+1 ordinary steps later, with no odd visit in between. Since Qj+1=Qj+qjQ_{j+1}=Q_j+q_j, induction gives the stated times.

Every qj≥1q_j\geq1. Hence Qj≥jQ_j\geq j, so both time sequences are strictly increasing and unbounded. The initial segment and the intervals between successive listed times therefore cover every nonnegative integer time. The initial segment before aa has no odd states, and Lemma 2 excludes any extra odd states between consecutive listed visits. This proves surjectivity onto precisely the two sets of odd-visit times; strict increase proves injectivity. At the jj-th listed visit there are exactly jj earlier odd visits, proving the inverse formula. ∎

Reconstructing every omitted state

Theorem 3 is not merely a way to extract a shorter list. It has an exact reconstruction procedure.

Proposition 4. The initial exponent aa and the indexed odd orbit (mj)j≥0(m_j)_{j\geq0} determine the entire ordinary and shortcut trajectories uniquely.

Proof. Each qjq_j is determined by mjm_j, so all QjQ_j and visit times are known. For 0≤t<a0\leq t<a, set both trajectories equal to 2a−tm02^{a-t}m_0. After that, the increasing unbounded visit times determine a unique block containing any given time.

For the shortcut clock, write uniquely

t=a+Qj+r,0≤r<qj. t=a+Q_j+r,\qquad 0\leq r<q_j.

The state is mjm_j for r=0r=0, and 2qj−rmj+12^{q_j-r}m_{j+1} for 1≤r<qj1\leq r<q_j. At the next block’s first time it is mj+1m_{j+1}.

For the ordinary clock, write uniquely

t=a+j+Qj+r,0≤r<qj+1. t=a+j+Q_j+r,\qquad 0\leq r<q_j+1.

The state is mjm_j for r=0r=0, and 2qj+1−rmj+12^{q_j+1-r}m_{j+1} for 1≤r≤qj1\leq r\leq q_j. Again the next block starts at mj+1m_{j+1}. Lemma 2 verifies the rule at every transition, including the block boundaries. Hence these formulas recover the actual trajectories and leave no state unspecified. ∎

The exponent aa is essential if the starting integer was not retained. The starts 3 and 24 have the same odd-return sequence, but 24 first takes the three steps 24→12→6→324\to12\to6\to3. Knowing a=3a=3 restores that prefix.

There is also an important distinction between deleting an initial even prefix and deleting every even portion of an orbit. The projection π\pi alone does not intertwine a single ordinary step with a single Syracuse step. For example,

π(C(6))=3,S(π(6))=S(3)=5. \pi(C(6))=3,\qquad S(\pi(6))=S(3)=5.

This does not make the maps unrelated. Their precise relation is the induced-map identity of Lemma 2 and the time bijections of Theorem 3. A single ordinary step can remain inside a block; one Syracuse step crosses the whole block.

The least value attained does not depend on the clock

For a map FF on positive integers and an allowed starting point nn, define

Fmin(n)=min⁡{Fk(n):k∈ℕ0}. F_{\min}(n)=\min\{F^k(n):k\in\mathbb N_0\}.

The set is nonempty because it contains nn, and well-ordering supplies a minimum. This definition does not require the orbit to be bounded, periodic, or known to terminate.

Theorem 5. For every a∈ℕ0a\in\mathbb N_0 and m∈𝒪m\in\mathcal O,

Cmin(2am)=Tmin(2am)=Smin(m). C_{\min}(2^a m)=T_{\min}(2^a m)=S_{\min}(m).

Proof. Put b=Smin(m)b=S_{\min}(m). Every odd state mjm_j occurs in both other trajectories by Theorem 3. In particular, the value bb occurs, so the minima of those trajectories are at most bb.

Conversely, each state in the initial halving segment is at least mm, hence at least bb. Each later odd state is some mj≥bm_j\geq b. Each intervening even state, by Lemma 2, is a positive power of 2 times the following odd state mj+1m_{j+1}, so it too is at least bb. Every state in either trajectory is therefore at least bb. The two opposite inequalities prove the result. ∎

The same reasoning yields a useful finite statement. Through the jj-th odd visit, including its endpoint, the minima are exactly

min0≤t≤a+QjTt(N)=min0≤i≤jmi=min0≤t≤a+j+QjCt(N). \min_{0\leq t\leq a+Q_j}T^t(N) =\min_{0\leq i\leq j}m_i =\min_{0\leq t\leq a+j+Q_j}C^t(N).

Every omitted even state in these prefixes is followed by an odd state that still lies in the prefix, so the proof of Theorem 5 applies word for word with a finite range. Matching endpoints is necessary. For the start 3 and a horizon of five steps in each clock, the shortcut prefix has reached 1, but the ordinary prefix ends at 4 and has minimum 3.

As an immediate consequence, the assertion that every positive ordinary Collatz orbit reaches 1 is equivalent to the assertion that every positive odd Syracuse orbit reaches 1. In one direction restrict to odd starts and use Theorem 5. In the other, factor an arbitrary start as 2am2^a m and apply the same theorem. A minimum of 1 is attained by definition. This proves equivalence of the formulations, not either conjecture.

What carries forward

Theorem A applies to any discrete system with the stated return property. The Collatz calculation supplies explicit formulas for its return times and intervening states. Its preservation of minima comes from a further inequality: each omitted even state is at least the following odd state. Separating these two arguments tells us what can be reused in a different system and what needs to be checked afresh.

There are two different coordinate choices here. The factorization N=2amN=2^a m separates the initial halving prefix from the odd start. The sequence (qj)(q_j) then records the durations between odd visits. Together with the odd states, these data reconstruct every step. For questions about the least value ever attained, the durations can be discarded; for questions about time they cannot.

A further distinction will matter when starting integers are chosen at random. The projection π\pi has infinitely many points in each fibre. The orbit identities proved here therefore do not, by themselves, identify the distribution of projected starting points. To transfer statements about “most” starting integers, one must also calculate the weight carried by those fibres. This is the reason to develop sampling alongside dynamics rather than treating a change of clock as an automatic change of probability law.
