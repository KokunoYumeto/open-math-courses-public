Summable scale comparisons and timed orbit bounds

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs and exercises: CC0 1.0.

An estimate at one scale can describe arbitrarily large inputs when its errors remain summable as the scale changes. There is a second question when the estimate concerns a trajectory: can we retain how long the trajectory takes? Closeness of two endpoint distributions alone says nothing about their travel times. To retain time, we will compare sets of endpoints from which a suitable continuation can be completed within a stated budget.

This lesson joins three methods. A geometric series controls repeated distributional comparisons. A concentration estimate determines a suitable time allowance. Disjoint intervals turn local probability bounds into a count of all starting integers. The combination gives a quantitative natural-density conclusion for the odd-return Collatz map, with an explicit leading time coefficient and an error allowance of order log⁡Nlog⁡log⁡N\sqrt{\log N\,\log\log N}.

We use nested first-passage maps and finite-chain transport, geometric concentration, and the complete uniform-input entrance comparison. These internal lessons provide the required proofs. Basic references are Tao’s Section 3 on scale iteration and Allikvere’s Sections 7–8 on uniform sampling and timed target sets. The sharper time allowance below follows by keeping the explicit concentration estimate rather than replacing it by a fixed power such as (log⁡N)4/5(\log N)^{4/5}.

Counting every starting interval

A local probability theorem gives a proportion inside [y,yα][y,y^\alpha]. Keeping only the largest such interval below XX would leave the lower integers untreated. The following elementary count uses all intervals and keeps their boundary conventions exact.

Solution. The first sum is (log⁡u)−c(1−α−cJ)/(1−α−c)(\log u)^{-c}(1-\alpha^{-cJ})/(1-\alpha^{-c}), bounded independently of JJ. The second is ∑j<J(log⁡u+jlog⁡α)−c\sum_{j<J}(\log u+j\log\alpha)^{-c}. Its terms form a decreasing sequence. Comparison with ∫0J(log⁡u+tlog⁡α)−cdt\int_0^J(\log u+t\log\alpha)^{-c}\,dt shows divergence as J→∞J\to\infty for c≤1c\leq1: logarithmically at c=1c=1, and as a positive power at c<1c<1. The choice of scales is part of the summability argument.

2 Select the coefficient of the time allowance

Suppose the intervening-leg error in (19) is at most 17h(log⁡z)17h(\log z). Give an exact sufficient choice of BB for (21), and show directly why it absorbs that error at every scale. Compare with an allowance Blog⁡log⁡zB\log\log z.

Solution. Take B=17/(α−1)B=17/(\sqrt\alpha-1). Equation (6) gives B[h(αlog⁡z)−h(log⁡z)]≥17h(log⁡z)B[h(\alpha\log z)-h(\log z)]\geq17h(\log z). For Blog⁡log⁡zB\log\log z, the increment is only Blog⁡αB\log\alpha, independent of zz, whereas h(log⁡z)→∞h(\log z)\to\infty. No fixed BB of that form absorbs the stated error. This is a comparison of available bounds, not a proof that no better timing theorem exists.

3 The target margin has a precise role

Assume z≥H1/α3z\geq H^{1/\alpha^3} but z<Hz<H. Prove the inequality used to concatenate a first leg when the next landing is already below HH. Then explain why the target-dependent term disappears from the final clock.

Solution. Taking logarithms gives log⁡z≥α−3log⁡H\log z\geq\alpha^{-3}\log H. Hence log⁡(H/z)/d≤(1−α−3)log⁡H/d=ΔH\log(H/z)/d\leq(1-\alpha^{-3})\log H/d=\Delta_H. Thus log⁡(M/z)/d≤log⁡(M/H)/d+ΔH\log(M/z)/d\leq\log(M/H)/d+\Delta_H, exactly the needed comparison. At the final concatenation, log⁡(N/H)d+ΔH=log⁡Nd−α−3log⁡Hd≤log⁡Nd. \begin{aligned} \frac{\log(N/H)}d+\Delta_H &=\frac{\log N}d-\frac{\alpha^{-3}\log H}d\\ &\leq\frac{\log N}d. \end{aligned} The disappearance is an inequality with a known nonpositive term, not a replacement of the target by a constant.

4 Keep endpoint counts and strict thresholds

List the odd integers in (3,9](3,9] and [3,9][3,9]. Explain the factor-two comparison when every half-open interval contains at least one odd integer. If a diverging lower bound b(X)b(X) happens to be an integer, which choice of integer target guarantees a strict final inequality below b(X)b(X)?

Solution. The sets are {5,7,9}\{5,7,9\} and {3,5,7,9}\{3,5,7,9\}. In general the closed count is at most the half-open count plus one. If the latter is at least one, this is at most twice that count. The choice ⌈b(X)⌉−1\lceil b(X)\rceil-1 is strictly below b(X)b(X) both when b(X)b(X) is integral and when it is not. Choosing ⌊b(X)⌋\lfloor b(X)\rfloor would not guarantee strictness in the integral case.

The reusable mechanism

The endpoint comparison is applied to a set with an explicit continuation property. A deterministic inclusion carries that property to the preceding scale, and a growing allowance pays for its local timing error. Summable distributional errors then permit arbitrarily many comparisons. Finally, disjoint counting intervals retain every starting integer and make the fixed-target estimate uniform in the outer cutoff.

These are useful techniques for induced dynamics and quantitative probability transport beyond this example. In the Collatz application, the concentration scale sharpens the time allowance, while the weighted arithmetic and approximation estimates determine the exceptional-set exponent. The two improvements enter at different places and have been carried through the complete argument separately.
