Integer polynomials and separation of logarithms

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs and exercises: CC0 1.0.

An irrational rotation eventually visits every interval, but a useful error bound needs more than irrationality. It needs a lower bound on how closely the rotation number can approach rational numbers. For the slope λ=log⁡3/log⁡2\lambda=\log3/\log2, unique factorization proves that no multiple is an integer. It does not by itself give the polynomial lower bound needed for the weighted averages of the preceding lessons.

We construct that bound from integer polynomials. Integrating a polynomial divided by a power of xx produces a logarithm and a rational number. The polynomial must be small on the integration intervals, while its coefficients and the rational denominators remain controlled. A positivity argument makes three such approximations linearly independent. These ingredients turn small integral remainders into a lower bound valid for every integer linear form in two logarithms. The construction is Rhin’s; the presentation below proves its arithmetic, analytic and transference steps explicitly.

Basic references are Rhin’s Approximants de Padé et mesures effectives d’irrationalité and Zudilin’s An essay on irrationality measures of π and other logarithms. We use finite-dimensional linear algebra, prime factorization, elementary calculus and the prime number theorem in the form ψ(x)/x→1\psi(x)/x\to1. A complete internal proof of that last result is Newman’s analytic theorem and its prime-counting application, Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2. The application at the end uses rational grids and rotation averages and unimodal weights and sharp counting kernels. The polynomial construction can be studied independently of the Collatz case study.

The exponent is the coefficient growth rate divided by the remainder decay rate. Replacing all positive integers nn by a fixed arithmetic subsequence changes the constant through NN, not that ratio. The nonsingularity hypothesis is equally essential: one excellent approximation gives no separation for vectors in its integer kernel.

A sharper component yields a sharper final theorem only when the remaining components permit it.

4. Follow the denominator through the exponent

The prime number theorem improves the arithmetic approximation rate through a concrete denominator, not through a formal analogy between the subjects.

What the construction connects

Coefficient divisibility makes the integral rows integer-valued after one controlled multiplier. Positivity proves their full rank. Polynomial decay and coefficient growth set the separation exponent, and prime counting controls the cost of clearing denominators. Finally, rational separation controls discrepancy and hence weighted counting.

These connections are useful independently of an orbit problem. They explain how approximation theory, elementary arithmetic, analytic number theory and uniform distribution can form one proof. In the running application, they turn the previously proved analytic kernel formula into its actual logarithmic-slope version. The remaining arithmetic comparisons must still be assembled with their own hypotheses and error terms.
