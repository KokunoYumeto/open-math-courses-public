Rational grids and weighted rotation averages

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs and exercises: CC0 1.0.

Rounding an arithmetic boundary leaves a fractional part. In the preceding counting kernel that fractional part is {rlog⁡23+u}\{r\log_2 3+u\}, where rr is the return time. Evaluating the kernel therefore requires an average along a rotation of the circle. The weights are not independent random variables: they are a deterministic profile concentrated near the likely return time.

This lesson develops the general averaging mechanism. A short rotation orbit lies close to a rational grid. Long orbits can be divided into such blocks, and a lower bound on rational approximation controls how many blocks are needed. Interval counts then control functions of bounded variation. Finally, summation by parts transfers that control to unequal weights, including weights chosen using the rotation itself.

The arguments need division with remainder, the pigeonhole principle, finite sums and elementary real integration. Counting kernels and weighted probability errors supplies the motivating application but is not needed for the general theorems. Basic references are Kuipers and Niederreiter’s Uniform Distribution of Sequences for rotation discrepancy and Koksma’s inequality, and Allikvere’s Almost all Collatz orbits attain almost bounded values in natural density for the logarithmic rotation in the counting problem. The proofs below give all the discrepancy and variation estimates used here; the final concrete example uses 2\sqrt2, whose rational-approximation bound is also proved.

Counting an interval along a rotation

Write {x}=x−⌊x⌋\{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor and ∥x∥=minm∈ℤ|x−m|. \|x\|=\min_{m\in\mathbb Z}|x-m|. The circle is ℝ/ℤ\mathbb R/\mathbb Z, represented by [0,1)[0,1), with distance d𝕋(x,y)=∥x−y∥. d_{\mathbb T}(x,y)=\|x-y\|. For a real slope α\alpha, phase uu, and integer N≥1N\geq1, consider the points xi={u+iα}x_i=\{u+i\alpha\}, 0≤i<N0\leq i<N. Define their interval discrepancy in counting units by Δα(N,u)=sup0≤a<b≤1|∑i=0N−1𝟏[a,b)(xi)−N(b−a)|. \Delta_\alpha(N,u)= \sup_{0\leq a<b\leq1} \left|\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathbf1_{[a,b)}(x_i)-N(b-a)\right|. \tag{1} (1) Thus the discrepancy divided by NN is the largest error in an interval’s observed frequency. Half-open intervals specify the treatment of a point exactly on a boundary.

Translation of an interval on the circle may make it cross zero. Its complement is then an interval of the form used in (1). Since both actual and expected total counts equal NN, a circular arc and its complement have opposite count errors. Consequently (1) also bounds all half-open circular arcs.

A rational rotation gives a useful comparison object. If p,qp,q are coprime integers and q≥1q\geq1, the map i↦ipmod⁡q i\longmapsto ip\bmod q permutes the qq residue classes. Indeed, ip≡jp(mod⁡q)ip\equiv jp\pmod q implies i≡j(mod⁡q)i\equiv j\pmod q by Bézout’s identity, and an injective map on this finite set is bijective. Hence {{u+ip/q}:0≤i<q}={{u+j/q}:0≤j<q}. \{\{u+ip/q\}:0\leq i<q\} =\{\{u+j/q\}:0\leq j<q\}. \tag{2} (2) The right side is an equally spaced grid.

Lemma 1 (Grid count). Every half-open circular arc of length ℓ\ell contains a number of points of (2) differing from qℓq\ell by at most one.

Proof. Lift the arc to a half-open interval of length ℓ≤1\ell\leq1 on the real line. Counting grid points becomes counting integers in a half-open interval of length qℓq\ell. That count is either ⌊qℓ⌋\lfloor q\ell\rfloor or ⌈qℓ⌉\lceil q\ell\rceil: after removing the integer part of its left endpoint, the assertion follows by listing the successive integers. Each lifted grid point corresponds to exactly one point of the circular arc. ∎

A nearby grid controls a complete block

Lemma 2 (Perturbed rational block). Let M,qM,q be positive integers, q≤Mq\leq M, with gcd⁡(p,q)=1\gcd(p,q)=1. If |α−pq|<1qM, \left|\alpha-\frac pq\right|<\frac1{qM}, \tag{3} (3) then Δα(q,u)≤5for every real u. \Delta_\alpha(q,u)\leq5 \quad\text{for every real }u. \tag{4} (4)

Proof. If q=1q=1, the count error is at most one, so suppose q≥2q\geq2. Pair the point xi={u+iα}x_i=\{u+i\alpha\} with yi={u+ip/q}y_i=\{u+ip/q\}. This follows by moving from yiy_i to xix_i along the displacement i(α−p/q)i(\alpha-p/q): changing membership requires crossing the boundary. The assertion includes endpoint coincidences.

Each closed circular neighborhood of radius δ\delta contains at most two grid points. To see this, its length is 2δ<2/q2\delta<2/q, whereas three successive points of a qq-grid span length 2/q2/q. The pairing is i↦ii\mapsto i, not a relabelling of the orbit by increasing position. The displayed values use α=2\alpha=\sqrt2, p/q=7/5p/q=7/5, u=1/10u=1/10, and 0≤i<50\leq i<5. Decimal positions in the drawing are numerical; the pairing, interval endpoints and count statements are exact. Lemma 2 explains why count changes are confined to the two boundaries.

The estimate is uniform in uu. We may therefore begin the next block wherever the previous one ended. No independence or randomization of the starting phase is required.

Choosing blocks by the pigeonhole principle

The argument gives both a count estimate and a finite decomposition. At every stage it records a coprime rational approximation, a block length, a number of repetitions and a smaller remainder. The exponent comes from the lower bound on each usable denominator, not from treating the individual orbit points as independent.

The finite-type discrepancy theorem and rational-grid decompositions are established parts of uniform distribution theory. In particular, the powers in (6) have their classical antecedents in Kuipers and Niederreiter’s treatment of rotations. The explicit constants here come from the block construction just proved; they are not asserted to be optimal.

From interval counts to functions of bounded variation

For a real function ff on [0,1][0,1], define V(f)=sup0=t0<⋯<tm=1∑j=1m|f(tj)−f(tj−1)|. V(f)=\sup_{0=t_0<\cdots<t_m=1} \sum_{j=1}^m|f(t_j)-f(t_{j-1})|. \tag{8} (8) The supremum is over all finite partitions. A function has bounded variation when V(f)<∞V(f)<\infty. This controls the accumulated size of its changes, including isolated values at jumps.

For completeness, variation is additive across adjacent intervals. Inserting their common endpoint into any partition cannot decrease its sum, by the triangle inequality. Conversely, partitions whose sums approach the variations on the two pieces can be joined. Taking suprema proves additivity. It follows that the sum of the variations over all pieces of a finite partition is V(f)V(f).

A bounded-variation function is bounded, since |f(t)|≤|f(0)|+V(f)|f(t)|\leq|f(0)|+V(f), and is Riemann integrable. Indeed, for a partition with largest interval length hh, the upper Darboux sum minus the lower one is at most ∑j(tj+1−tj)V(f;[tj,tj+1])≤hV(f). \sum_j(t_{j+1}-t_j)V(f;[t_j,t_{j+1}]) \leq hV(f). Partitions of arbitrarily small mesh make this difference tend to zero.

A complete quadratic example

We can establish rational separation directly for α=2\alpha=\sqrt2. First, 2\sqrt2 is irrational: if p2=2q2p^2=2q^2 for coprime positive integers p,qp,q, then pp is even; writing p=2rp=2r makes qq even, a contradiction.

For q≥1q\geq1, choose pp nearest to q2q\sqrt2. Then 1≤p≤2q1\leq p\leq2q: the real number q2q\sqrt2 lies between qq and 2q2q, and a nearest integer can be chosen in that integer interval. Therefore ∥q2∥=|q2−p|=|2q2−p2|q2+p>14q. \|q\sqrt2\| =|q\sqrt2-p| =\frac{|2q^2-p^2|}{q\sqrt2+p} >\frac1{4q}. \tag{17} (17) The numerator is a nonzero integer. This proves (5) with c=1/4,ν=1c=1/4,\nu=1, without a numerical search.

For an integer R≥1R\geq1 and any integer center mm, define the triangular weight wR(n)={(R−|n−m|)/R2,|n−m|<R,0,|n−m|≥R. w_R(n)= \begin{cases} (R-|n-m|)/R^2,&|n-m|<R,\\ 0,&|n-m|\geq R. \end{cases} \tag{18} (18) Its sum is one, because R+2∑j=1R−1(R−j)=R2R+2\sum_{j=1}^{R-1}(R-j)=R^2. Its maximum is 1/R1/R, its variation is 2/R2/R, and its support has 2R−12R-1 integers. Applying (7), (10) and (12) gives the explicit estimate |∑nwR(n)2−{n2+u}−12log⁡2|≤20R(1+⌊log⁡2(2R−1)⌋). \left|\sum_n w_R(n)2^{-\{n\sqrt2+u\}} -\frac1{2\log2}\right| \leq \frac{20}{R}\bigl(1+\lfloor\log_2(2R-1)\rfloor\bigr). \tag{19} (19) In particular the weighted averages converge uniformly in both mm and uu. The constants are coarse, but the convergence and its uniformity follow from the proved inequalities.

This example separates the roles of the ingredients. Arithmetic supplies (17), interval geometry supplies the block count, and the height of the triangular profile supplies its small variation. Replacing 2\sqrt2 by a logarithmic rotation changes the arithmetic input, not the maps or the summation identity.

What the averaging mechanism provides

A Diophantine lower bound prevents all useful grid denominators from being too small. Complete grids then control interval counts. Bounded variation turns those counts into integration estimates, and summation by parts measures exactly how unequal weights affect them. The resulting bounds are deterministic and uniform in the starting phase. In an arithmetic counting problem, this is what permits the fractional-part oscillation to be averaged without pretending it is independent of the counting profile.
