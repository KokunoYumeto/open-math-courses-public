Quotient distributions and Fourier mixing

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs, exercises and illustration: CC0 1.0.

A probability distribution can look quite uneven while already being thoroughly mixed at the scale that matters. Suppose that we distinguish nine possible states, but an observation records only their residues modulo three. Uniformity inside each of the three observation classes is one question; equal probabilities for the three classes is another. Fourier analysis separates these questions exactly.

This lesson develops that separation and a quantitative use of it. We will prove that adding two independent random variables can produce good mixing when one summand has small Fourier coefficients and the other has small collision probability. Neither summand has to be uniform. We will then explain why this combination appears in the analysis of random affine sums associated with the Collatz iteration.

The prerequisites are finite sums, complex conjugation, the identity for a finite geometric series, and elementary probability on a countable set. Pushforward measures and logarithmic sampling supplies the probability-transport viewpoint; its Lemma 2 identifies event distance with half the full mass difference. All Fourier identities and estimates used here are proved below. Basic references are Keith Conrad’s Characters of finite abelian groups, Section 4, and Terence Tao’s Almost all orbits of the Collatz map attain almost bounded values, Section 6. For the surrounding group theory, the programme’s Fourier analysis on finite abelian groups, Theorems 2.2 and 3.1, treats general finite abelian groups and quotient duality. Here the focus is the probability estimate and its exact constants.

What a quotient observation forgets

Let NN be a positive integer, let MM divide NN, and write L=N/ML=N/M. Put

GN=ℤ/Nℤ,GM=ℤ/Mℤ,q:GN→GM,q(x)=xmod⁡M. G_N=\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z,\qquad G_M=\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z, \qquad q:G_N\longrightarrow G_M,\quad q(x)=x\bmod M.

Representatives in a sum run from zero to one less than the relevant modulus. Each fibre of qq has LL elements. Its kernel is the subgroup

H={0,M,2M,…,(L−1)M}⊆GN. H=\{0,M,2M,\ldots,(L-1)M\}\subseteq G_N.

A complex mass function on GNG_N is a function f:GN→ℂf:G_N\to\mathbb C. We use counting sums throughout:

∥f∥1=∑x∈GN|f(x)|,∥f∥22=∑x∈GN|f(x)|2. \|f\|_1=\sum_{x\in G_N}|f(x)|, \qquad \|f\|_2^2=\sum_{x\in G_N}|f(x)|^2.

A probability mass function is nonnegative and has sum one. A subprobability mass function is nonnegative and has sum at most one; it records, for example, the part of a law on an event without dividing by the probability of that event.

Define two linear maps on mass functions:

R:ℂGN→ℂGM,(Rf)(a)=∑q(x)=af(x), R:\mathbb C^{G_N}\longrightarrow\mathbb C^{G_M}, \qquad (Rf)(a)=\sum_{q(x)=a}f(x),

U:ℂGM→ℂGN,(Uh)(x)=h(q(x))L. U:\mathbb C^{G_M}\longrightarrow\mathbb C^{G_N}, \qquad (Uh)(x)=\frac{h(q(x))}{L}.

The first records the coarse observation. The second distributes each coarse mass equally among its LL possible original states. Their composition

P=UR:ℂGN→ℂGN P=UR:\mathbb C^{G_N}\longrightarrow\mathbb C^{G_N}

replaces the mass within each fibre by its average while keeping the total mass of that fibre unchanged.

Proposition 1 (Reduction and uniform lifting). These maps satisfy

RU=I,P2=P,RP=R,∥Rf∥1≤∥f∥1,∥Uh∥1=∥h∥1. RU=I,\qquad P^2=P,\qquad RP=R, \qquad \|Rf\|_1\leq\|f\|_1,\qquad \|Uh\|_1=\|h\|_1.

They preserve nonnegativity and total mass. The image of UU, which is also the image of PP, consists exactly of the functions constant on every fibre. The kernel of RR, which is also the kernel of PP, consists exactly of the functions whose sum in every fibre is zero. In particular, RR is onto and UU is one-to-one.

The information loss can be given coordinates. Write δx\delta_x for unit mass at xx. For each a=0,…,M−1a=0,\ldots,M-1, the L−1L-1 vectors

δa+tM−δa,1≤t<L, \delta_{a+tM}-\delta_a,\qquad 1\leq t<L,

form a basis for the zero-sum functions on that fibre: the coefficient of each δa+tM\delta_{a+tM} determines its coefficient in this basis, and the zero-sum condition then determines the coefficient of δa\delta_a. Together they form a basis of ker⁡R\ker R, of dimension N−MN-M. Thus UU is a specified right inverse of RR, not a recovery of the discarded data. A two-sided inverse exists only when M=NM=N.

For a probability μ\mu, the quantity ∥μ−Pμ∥1\|\mu-P\mu\|_1 measures the discrepancy from equal masses within the observation classes. It does not require the classes themselves to be equally probable.

The law gives mass 1/41/4 to each of 0,1,3,40,1,3,4. Its class masses are 1/2,1/2,01/2,1/2,0. Uniform lifting spreads the first two masses over their three-member fibres, assigning 1/61/6 to each member. The last class remains empty. The maps in Proposition 1 retain the class masses, not the individual-state masses.

Frequencies that distinguish the missing information

For r∈GNr\in G_N, define the character

χr(x)=exp⁡(2πirx/N). \chi_r(x)=\exp(2\pi i rx/N).

It is well defined on residues, and χr(x+y)=χr(x)χr(y)\chi_r(x+y)=\chi_r(x)\chi_r(y). Define the Fourier transform by

The transform is a mass sum, with no factor 1/N1/N. For a probability law it is the expectation of χr(X)¯\overline{\chi_r(X)}; hence its absolute value is at most one. At frequency zero it equals the total mass.

Adding independent uniform noise in HH performs exactly the uniform lifting operation. It randomizes inside the classes without changing which class is observed.

An energy estimate for mixing inside fibres

Let D={r∈GN:L∤r}D=\{r\in G_N:L\nmid r\} be the discarded frequencies. Parseval’s identity and Proposition 3 give the exact equality

N∥f−Pf∥22=∑r∈D|f̂N(r)|2. N\|f-Pf\|_2^2=\sum_{r\in D}|\widehat f_N(r)|^2. \tag{1} (1)

This is an estimate for all events simultaneously. For probabilities μ\mu and PμP\mu, the largest difference on an event is exactly half the left side, by the event-distance lemma.

Fine mixing is not the whole distribution

Write uN(x)=1/Nu_N(x)=1/N and uM(a)=1/Mu_M(a)=1/M for the full uniform laws. Since UuM=uNUu_M=u_N, Proposition 1 gives

∥Pμ−uN∥1=∥Rμ−uM∥1. \|P\mu-u_N\|_1=\|R\mu-u_M\|_1.

Consequently

∥Rμ−uM∥1≤∥μ−uN∥1≤∥μ−Pμ∥1+∥Rμ−uM∥1. \|R\mu-u_M\|_1 \leq\|\mu-u_N\|_1 \leq\|\mu-P\mu\|_1+\|R\mu-u_M\|_1. \tag{5} (5)

Why random affine sums lead to this estimate

The odd-return Collatz map sends a positive odd integer mm to

S(m)=3m+12ν2(3m+1). S(m)=\frac{3m+1}{2^{\nu_2(3m+1)}}.

For an actual orbit, let aja_j be the positive exponent removed at its jj-th step. Iterating the affine identity mj=32−ajmj−1+2−ajm_j=3\,2^{-a_j}m_{j-1}+2^{-a_j} gives

mn=3n2−(a1+⋯+an)m0+∑j=1n3n−j2−(aj+⋯+an). m_n=3^n2^{-(a_1+\cdots+a_n)}m_0 +\sum_{j=1}^n3^{n-j}2^{-(a_j+\cdots+a_n)}. \tag{6} (6)

For completeness, the induction step multiplies every term in the expression for mnm_n by 32−an+13\,2^{-a_{n+1}}, then appends 2−an+12^{-a_{n+1}}. This gives exactly the displayed formula with n+1n+1; the case n=1n=1 is the defining affine identity.

Now consider a probability model: let A1,…,AnA_1,\ldots,A_n be independent positive integers with

ℙ(Aj=b)=2−b,b=1,2,…. \mathbb P(A_j=b)=2^{-b},\qquad b=1,2,\ldots.

The probabilities sum to one by the geometric series. Reversing this random word preserves its joint law, since the probability of a word is 2−(b1+⋯+bn)2^{-(b_1+\cdots+b_n)}. Therefore the offset in (6), for this model, has the same distribution modulo 3n3^n as

Xn=∑i=1n3i−12−Si(mod⁡3n),Si=A1+⋯+Ai. X_n=\sum_{i=1}^{n}3^{i-1}2^{-S_i}\pmod {3^n}, \qquad S_i=A_1+\cdots+A_i. \tag{7} (7)

Every negative power of two in (7) means a multiplicative inverse modulo the stated power of three; it exists because two and three are coprime. Thus (7) is a well-defined random variable on G3nG_{3^n}. Equation (6) is an identity along a deterministic orbit; (7) is a separately defined independent-word model. Relating distributions of actual orbit words to this model requires a counting or sampling argument. Reversal of the independent word alone does not supply that argument.

Two exact conclusions explain the usefulness of this decomposition. First, if the residue map (A1,…,Ak)↦Yk(A_1,\ldots,A_k)\mapsto Y_k is one-to-one on the words in EE, then every nonzero value of ff is exactly 2−l2^{-l}. Indeed, there are finitely many positive words of length kk with sum ll; each has probability 2−l2^{-l}, and injectivity means that at most one contributes to any state. In that case ∑yf(y)2=2−lℙ(E)\sum_y f(y)^2=2^{-l}\mathbb P(E). This statement specifies precisely where an arithmetic separation result enters the collision estimate; it does not assert injectivity for arbitrary EE.

Second, the size of the cyclic group seen by a tail frequency is explicit. For a nonzero frequency write r=3vsr=3^v s, with 3∤s3\nmid s. If v<n−kv<n-k, the character in (10) depends only on ZZ modulo 3n−k−v3^{n-k-v}, and there its coefficient is the unit s2−ls2^{-l}. Thus reducing the modulus is not a harmless notational step; it states exactly which tail randomness a given frequency can detect.

Tao’s Section 6 combines this type of decomposition with quantitative separation of prefix offsets and decay of tail Fourier coefficients. For averaging modulo 3m3^m inside G3nG_{3^n}, Proposition 3 retains frequencies divisible by 3n−m3^{n-m}; the discarded ones have v<n−mv<n-m. Solution. Each fibre is {a,a+4,a+8}\{a,a+4,a+8\}, for a=0,1,2,3a=0,1,2,3. The image of RR is all of ℂG4\mathbb C^{G_4}; for example, Rδa=δaR\delta_a=\delta_a. Its kernel has the eight-element basis

δa+4−δa,δa+8−δa,a=0,1,2,3. \delta_{a+4}-\delta_a,\quad \delta_{a+8}-\delta_a, \qquad a=0,1,2,3.

The masses of δ1−δ5\delta_1-\delta_5 cancel in the same fibre, so its reduction is zero. Uniform lifting gives

Pδ1=13(δ1+δ5+δ9). P\delta_1=\tfrac13(\delta_1+\delta_5+\delta_9).

Here L=3L=3, so the surviving frequencies are 0,3,6,90,3,6,9. Both laws reduce to unit mass at one modulo four. But the event consisting of the state one has probabilities one and 1/31/3, respectively; this records information that the observation discarded.

2. Successive observations

Let K∣M∣NK\mid M\mid N. On functions on GNG_N, let PMP_M average within fibres of reduction modulo MM, and define PKP_K similarly. Prove PKPM=PMPK=PKP_KP_M=P_MP_K=P_K. Deduce

∥f−PKf∥1≤∥f−PMf∥1+∥PMf−PKf∥1. \|f-P_Kf\|_1\leq\|f-P_Mf\|_1+\|P_Mf-P_Kf\|_1.

Does equality always hold?

Solution. Proposition 3 says that PMP_M retains frequencies divisible by N/MN/M, while PKP_K retains those divisible by N/KN/K. Since N/K=(N/M)(M/K)N/K=(N/M)(M/K), the second set is contained in the first. Multiplying either pair of indicators gives the second indicator. Fourier inversion proves both composition identities. The stated bound is the triangle inequality after inserting PMfP_Mf.

Equality need not hold. On G4G_4, take M=2M=2, K=1K=1, and f=δ0f=\delta_0. Then PMf=(δ0+δ2)/2P_Mf=(\delta_0+\delta_2)/2 and PKf=u4P_Kf=u_4. The three norms in order are 3/2,1,13/2,1,1. Thus the inequality is strict.

3. The cost of splitting into events

In Corollary 5 suppose J≥1J\geq1, δj≤δ\delta_j\leq\delta, and fj(x)≤Bf_j(x)\leq B for every j,xj,x. Prove

∥μ−Pμ∥1≤2ε+δNBJ(1−ε). \|\mu-P\mu\|_1\leq2\varepsilon+ \delta\sqrt{NBJ(1-\varepsilon)}.

Explain why replacing every fjf_j by a probability law without retaining its original mass would change this calculation.

Solution. Put aj=∑xfj(x)a_j=\sum_xf_j(x). The sum of a convolution is the product of its two masses, by summing over (y,x−y)(y,x-y). Hence ∑jaj=1−ε\sum_ja_j=1-\varepsilon. Theorem 4 bounds the jj-th contribution by δNBaj\delta\sqrt{NBa_j}. The finite square-sum inequality used before Theorem 4, applied to aj\sqrt{a_j}, gives

∑jaj≤J∑jaj=J(1−ε). \sum_j\sqrt{a_j}\leq\sqrt{J\sum_ja_j} =\sqrt{J(1-\varepsilon)}.

Substitution in (4) proves the claim. For aj>0a_j>0, replacing fjf_j by fj/ajf_j/a_j changes its mass to one and its atom bound to B/ajB/a_j; the mixture must then carry an outside coefficient aja_j. Dropping that coefficient changes the law. A piece with aj=0a_j=0 is identically zero and needs no division.

4. A complete mixing estimate for a random walk

On GNG_N, where N≥2N\geq2, start at zero and at each step add an independent increment which is zero or one with equal probabilities. Let μt\mu_t be the law after t≥1t\geq1 steps. Prove

∥μt−uN∥1≤N−1(cos⁡(π/N))t. \|\mu_t-u_N\|_1 \leq\sqrt{N-1}\bigl(\cos(\pi/N)\bigr)^t.

Conclude that every event probability approaches its uniform value. Treat N=2N=2 explicitly.

Solution. The increment law is κ=(δ0+δ1)/2\kappa=(\delta_0+\delta_1)/2. Independence and convolution give μt=κ*t\mu_t=\kappa^{*t}, so

μt̂N(r)=(1+exp⁡(−2πir/N)2)t,|μt̂N(r)|=|cos⁡(πr/N)|t. \widehat{\mu_t}_N(r) =\left(\frac{1+\exp(-2\pi ir/N)}2\right)^t, \qquad |\widehat{\mu_t}_N(r)|=|\cos(\pi r/N)|^t.

For 1≤r<N1\leq r<N, symmetry about π/2\pi/2 and decrease of cosine on [0,π/2][0,\pi/2] give |cos⁡(πr/N)|≤cos⁡(π/N)|\cos(\pi r/N)|\leq\cos(\pi/N). Take M=1M=1 in (2): averaging onto the single fibre gives Pμt=uNP\mu_t=u_N, and the discarded frequencies are exactly r=1,…,N−1r=1,\ldots,N-1. Summing their squared bounds and taking square roots proves the estimate. For N>2N>2, 0<cos⁡(π/N)<10<\cos(\pi/N)<1, so this geometric sequence tends to zero. Every event discrepancy is at most half the displayed bound. For N=2N=2, one increment already has the uniform law, and the estimate is zero for every t≥1t\geq1, as required.

What carries forward

The central distinction is between a quotient distribution and the distribution inside its fibres. The maps RR and UU make that distinction precise, and Fourier characters identify exactly which information is retained. Convolution then turns independence into multiplication, allowing small Fourier coefficients from one random contribution to combine with dispersed mass from another.

These tools belong to probability on finite groups and harmonic analysis, independently of any particular integer iteration. The random walk exercise is one complete application. For the Collatz case study, the affine decomposition provides another route into the same mathematics: prefix separation controls collisions, tail cancellation controls frequencies, and concentration controls the part excluded from the decomposition. Understanding those three estimates is the next step toward the scale-transport arguments of the preceding lesson.
