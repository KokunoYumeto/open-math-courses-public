Symbolic itineraries and affine composition

Written by GPT-6 Astra, Ultra reasoning, in Codex, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition, proofs and exercises: CC0 1.0.

A rule with several branches has two records: the states it visits and the branches it uses. A symbolic itinerary records the second. Such a record is useful only when we know which itineraries can occur and how much of the original trajectory they determine.

This lesson develops a concrete answer for affine branches. We first compose the branches exactly. We then impose the integer restrictions that turn a formal word into an actual orbit. For the shortcut Collatz map, a word of length kk specifies exactly one residue class modulo 2k2^k. This connects symbolic dynamics with congruence arithmetic and, at the end, with a finite probability experiment.

We use integer arithmetic, induction, and the definitions of iteration from Return maps and exact changes of clock. Congruence means that the difference of two integers is a multiple of the modulus. The division algorithm and the basic rules for fractions are assumed. No measure theory or conjecture about eventual convergence is needed. Terras and Everett are historical sources for finite parity coding. Laarhoven and de Weger discuss its graph-theoretic form; Inselmann uses the affine and concatenation identities in quantitative orbit estimates. Full references appear below.

A word is not yet an orbit

Let a set XX be partitioned into disjoint sets XbX_b, indexed by symbols bb. Suppose a map F:X→XF:X\to X uses the rule fbf_b on XbX_b. The itinerary of a point xx begins with b0,…,bk−1b_0,\ldots,b_{k-1} precisely when

Fi(x)∈Xbi(0≤i<k). F^i(x)\in X_{b_i}\qquad(0\leq i<k).

The set of these starting points is the cylinder of the word. On this set, induction gives

Fk(x)=fbk−1∘⋯∘fb0(x). F^k(x)=f_{b_{k-1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{b_0}(x).

The order matters: the earliest rule is on the right. The composition may make sense on a larger space, but it describes FkF^k only on its cylinder. In particular, algebraically composing branches does not prove that the word is realizable.

Our running example is

T(n)={n/2,n even,(3n+1)/2,n odd,n∈ℕ+. T(n)=\begin{cases}n/2,&n\text{ even},\\(3n+1)/2,&n\text{ odd},\end{cases} \qquad n\in\mathbb N_+.

The branches extend to affine maps on the rational numbers:

fb(x)=3bx+b2,b∈{0,1}. f_b(x)=\frac{3^b x+b}{2},\qquad b\in\{0,1\}.

For a binary word w=(b0,…,bk−1)w=(b_0,\ldots,b_{k-1}), let Φw=fbk−1∘⋯∘fb0\Phi_w=f_{b_{k-1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{b_0}, with the empty word giving the identity. The cylinder Cw⊆ℕ+C_w\subseteq\mathbb N_+ consists of the starts whose first kk parities are ww.

Keeping the additive term

Put si=b0+⋯+bi−1s_i=b_0+\cdots+b_{i-1}, with s0=0s_0=0, and define

Aw=∑i=0k−1bi2i3sk−si+1. A_w=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1} b_i2^i3^{s_k-s_{i+1}}.

Theorem 1 (Affine formula). For every binary word of length kk and every x∈ℚx\in\mathbb Q,

Φw(x)=3skx+Aw2k. \Phi_w(x)=\frac{3^{s_k}x+A_w}{2^k}.

Proof. For the empty word the numerator is xx. Appending a bit bb to a length-kk word changes the affine expression to

fb(Φw(x))=3sk+bx+3bAw+b2k2k+1. f_b(\Phi_w(x)) =\frac{3^{s_k+b}x+3^b A_w+b2^k}{2^{k+1}}.

The coefficient of xx alone is not the iterate. The term AwA_w records every addition along the way. For instance, repeated use of f1f_1 gives

Φ(1,…,1)(x)=3kx+(3k−2k)2k. \Phi_{(1,\ldots,1)}(x)=\frac{3^k x+(3^k-2^k)}{2^k}.

Joining two blocks

Let uu and vv have lengths aa and bb, and let s(u)s(u) and s(v)s(v) denote their numbers of ones. Write uvuv for chronological concatenation: first follow uu, then vv.

Proposition 2 (Composition coordinates).

s(uv)=s(u)+s(v),Auv=3s(v)Au+2aAv,Φuv=Φv∘Φu. \begin{aligned} s(uv)&=s(u)+s(v),\\ A_{uv}&=3^{s(v)}A_u+2^a A_v,\\ \Phi_{uv}&=\Phi_v\circ\Phi_u. \end{aligned}

The realized cylinders satisfy

Cuv={n∈Cu:Ta(n)∈Cv}. C_{uv}=\{n\in C_u:T^a(n)\in C_v\}.

Proof. Counts add. Substituting the affine formula for uu into the one for vv gives

Φv(Φu(x))=3s(u)+s(v)x+3s(v)Au+2aAv2a+b. \Phi_v(\Phi_u(x)) =\frac{3^{s(u)+s(v)}x+3^{s(v)}A_u+2^a A_v}{2^{a+b}}.

The cylinder statement follows by separating the parity tests before and after time aa. This also proves that the algebraic composition acts on exactly the required starts when it is used as an orbit formula. ∎

One can store these coordinates in an integer matrix:

Mw=(3s(w)Aw02|w|),Muv=MvMu. M_w=\begin{pmatrix}3^{s(w)}&A_w\\0&2^{|w|}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad M_{uv}=M_v M_u.

To read the associated rational map, apply the matrix to (x,1)(x,1) and divide the first coordinate by the second. Its denominator is positive. Matrix multiplication explains the composition law; the cylinder still supplies its integer domain.

Another exact coordinate is rw=Aw/(2|w|3s(w))r_w=A_w/(2^{|w|}3^{s(w)}). Retaining the length and count makes this a reversible change of coordinates: Aw=2|w|3s(w)rwA_w=2^{|w|}3^{s(w)}r_w. Dividing the numerator formula by 2a+b3s(u)+s(v)2^{a+b}3^{s(u)+s(v)} gives

ruv=2−bru+3−s(u)rv. r_{uv}=2^{-b}r_u+3^{-s(u)}r_v.

Neither term may be dropped. This identity describes the same composition using rational rather than integer coordinates.

The arithmetic domain of a word

We now prove that every finite binary word occurs, and identify all its starting integers. For this proof only, extend TT to nonnegative integers by T(0)=0T(0)=0. This lets us use the representative 0 of a residue class without changing any positive orbit.

Lemma 3 (Lifting a finite itinerary). If nonnegative integers m,nm,n satisfy n−m=2ktn-m=2^k t for an integer tt, their first kk parities agree. If that common word has prefix counts sis_i, then

Ti(n)−Ti(m)=3si2k−it(0≤i≤k). T^i(n)-T^i(m)=3^{s_i}2^{k-i}t\qquad(0\leq i\leq k).

Proof. At i=0i=0 the difference is the assumed multiple of 2k2^k. Suppose the formula holds at an index i<ki<k. The difference is even, so the two current states have the same parity bib_i. Subtracting their branch formulas cancels the additive term and divides their difference by 2 after multiplication by 3bi3^{b_i}. This proves the next formula and the next shared parity test. Induction completes the proof. ∎

Theorem 4 (Words and residue classes). For each length-kk binary word ww, there is a unique cwc_w with 0≤cw<2k0\leq c_w<2^k such that

Cw={n∈ℕ+:n≡cw(mod⁡2k)}. C_w=\{n\in\mathbb N_+:n\equiv c_w\pmod{2^k}\}.

Below we write r=cwr=c_w when a single cylinder is fixed.

Proof. The empty word corresponds to the only class modulo 1. Suppose the length-kk words correspond bijectively to the classes modulo 2k2^k. A class represented by rr has exactly two lifts modulo 2k+12^{k+1}, represented by rr and r+2kr+2^k. Lemma 3 says that both have the same first kk bits. Their states at time kk differ by 3sk3^{s_k}, which is odd. Exactly one therefore has next bit 0, and exactly one has next bit 1. This proves existence and uniqueness for each extended word. Lemma 3 also shows that every integer in a chosen lifted class has that itinerary. Conversely, an integer with the extended itinerary must be in its prefix class and then in the uniquely specified lift. The induction proves the statement. Every class has a positive representative, including the class represented by zero. ∎

For a fixed word, write rr for its representative, s=s(w)s=s(w), and A=AwA=A_w. On its cylinder, TkT^k is an injective map onto the arithmetic progression

{3sr+A2k+3st:t∈ℤ,r+2kt>0}. \left\{\frac{3^s r+A}{2^k}+3^s t: t\in\mathbb Z,\ r+2^k t>0\right\}.

Its inverse on that image is y↦(2ky−A)/3sy\mapsto(2^k y-A)/3^s. The formula follows by substituting n=r+2ktn=r+2^k t into Theorem 1. It proves both directions: every allowed tt gives a cylinder point and its image, and the displayed inverse returns that point. Thus a word does not identify a single positive start. It identifies a congruence class; the additional integer tt locates the start within that class.

An actual concatenation

Consider the shortcut trajectory

37→56→28→14→7→11→17→26→13. 37\to56\to28\to14\to7\to11\to17\to26\to13.

The first block has word u=(1,0,0,0,1)u=(1,0,0,0,1), length 5 and two odd steps. The next has v=(1,1,0)v=(1,1,0), length 3 and two odd steps. Directly from the sum in Theorem 1,

Au=3+16=19,Av=3+2=5. A_u=3+16=19,\qquad A_v=3+2=5.

Consequently

The two separate congruence tests are compatible only after the second is imposed on the landing value 11, not on the original start 37.

There is also a time coordinate. One shortcut step uses one ordinary step at an even state and two at an odd state. Hence a block of length kk with ss ones consumes k+sk+s ordinary steps. This follows by adding the costs at each step. For the example, the first block costs 7 ordinary steps, the second costs 5, and the combined block costs 12. The identity (a+s(u))+(b+s(v))=(a+b)+s(uv)(a+s(u))+(b+s(v))=(a+b)+s(uv) expresses the compatibility of time with concatenation.

A finite probability law from an exact bijection

Choose a starting integer uniformly from any 2k2^k consecutive positive integers. Each residue class modulo 2k2^k occurs once, so Theorem 4 implies that each length-kk word has probability 2−k2^{-k}. In particular, specifying any jj of the bits leaves exactly 2k−j2^{k-j} words, so that specification has probability 2−j2^{-j}. This proves that the first kk bits in this experiment are independent fair bits.

The probability of exactly hh odd steps is therefore

2−k(kh)(0≤h≤k), 2^{-k}\binom{k}{h}\qquad(0\leq h\leq k),

because choosing the hh positions of the ones specifies the word. This is an exact counting theorem for the stated finite experiment.

For a uniform start in {1,…,X}\{1,\ldots,X\}, where XX is any positive integer, each word count differs from X/2kX/2^k by at most 1: divide the interval into complete blocks of length 2k2^k and one incomplete block. Consequently

∑w∈{0,1}k|ℙ(word=w)−2−k|≤2kX. \sum_{w\in\{0,1\}^k} \left|\mathbb P(\text{word}=w)-2^{-k}\right| \leq\frac{2^k}{X}.

The dependence on kk is essential. This bound tends to zero for fixed kk as XX grows, and more generally whenever 2k/X→02^k/X\to0. It does not provide a small error for an arbitrarily long itinerary at a fixed cutoff. The next lesson studies explicitly how sampling measures enter such statements.

Exercise 1. Find the cylinder and affine formula for w=(1,0,1)w=(1,0,1).

Solution. The prefix counts are 0,1,1,20,1,1,2, so Aw=3+4=7A_w=3+4=7, and Φw(x)=(9x+7)/8\Phi_w(x)=(9x+7)/8. The start 1 has trajectory 1,2,1,21,2,1,2, hence realizes the word. Exercise 2. Reverse the two blocks in the worked example. Compute the formal composition and decide whether 37 realizes the reversed word.

Solution. Reversal gives Avu=9⋅5+8⋅19=197A_{vu}=9\cdot5+8\cdot19=197, so Φvu(x)=(81x+197)/256\Phi_{vu}(x)=(81x+197)/256. The word begins with 1,1,01,1,0, whereas 37 begins with 1,0,01,0,0; thus 37 is not in its cylinder. Applying the rational formula at 37 is not computing its eighth iterate. The different numerators also show directly that the two affine block maps do not commute.

Exercise 3. A length-kk word with ss ones contains a periodic point nn, returning after these kk shortcut steps. Derive its value. Is the resulting rational number automatically a positive periodic point?

Solution. The equation Tk(n)=nT^k(n)=n gives (2k−3s)n=Aw(2^k-3^s)n=A_w. For k≥1k\geq1, the coefficient cannot vanish: when s=0s=0, 3s=1<2k3^s=1<2^k; when s≥1s\geq1, 3s3^s is odd and 2k2^k is even. The word (0)(0) gives only 0, outside the positive domain. The word (1,0)(1,0) gives 1 and realizes its two-step shortcut cycle. No assertion of least period follows without checking earlier returns.

Exercise 4. For a uniform start among 32 consecutive positive integers, what is the probability that the first five bits have exactly two ones? What if the start is uniform in {1,…,1000}\{1,\ldots,1000\}?

What carries forward

The composition of branches belongs to elementary algebra. The cylinder calculation belongs to arithmetic. The probability law comes from counting those cylinders under a specified distribution of starts. These are three connected arguments, not three interchangeable descriptions. Keeping them separate lets us ask the right question when we change the map or the sampling rule: which compositions remain valid, which words are realized, and how much weight does each cylinder carry?
