A deterministic primality test

Written by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Public domain (CC0).

The factoring process of Factoring with verifiable auxiliary data checks the factorizations that it produces, and for this it needs to decide exactly whether a given number is prime, by a classical algorithm with a polynomial bound on its running time. Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena gave the first such algorithm [AKS 2004]. This lesson proves a variant of their test with simpler bounds [OpenAI-EQF, Appendix A]: the auxiliary modulus is a prime \(s\le n^8\), and the tested residues are \(1\le a\le8s\). The original test uses an auxiliary modulus of size at most about \((\log_2m)^5\) and far fewer congruences, so its running time is a much smaller polynomial; the variant needs only elementary estimates.

We use from the core course Abstract Algebra I (Judson, Abstract Algebra: Theory and Applications) the unique factorization of integers (Theorem 2.2.7) and Fermat's little theorem (Theorem 6.3.3), and from the core course Abstract Algebra II that a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) over a field has at most \(d\) roots in that field (Corollary 17.2.4), that every nonconstant polynomial over a field has a root in some extension field (Theorem 21.1.5), and that \((u+v)^p=u^p+v^p\) in an integral domain of prime characteristic \(p\) (Lemma 22.1.3).

1. The algorithm

Throughout, \(n\ge128\) and \(2\le m<2^n\) are integers. For a prime \(s\) not dividing \(m\), \(\operatorname{ord}_s(m)\) is the least \(j\ge1\) with \(m^j\equiv1\pmod s\). For polynomials \(A,C\in\mathbb Z[X]\) we write \(A\equiv C\pmod{m,X^s-1}\) if \(A-C=mG+(X^s-1)H\) with \(G,H\in\mathbb Z[X]\); equivalently, the images of \(A\) and \(C\) in \((\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)[X]\) differ by a multiple of \(X^s-1\).

  1. Reject \(m\) if \(m=b^e\) with integers \(b,e\ge2\).
  2. Find the least prime \(s\le n^8\) such that \(s\nmid m\) and \(\operatorname{ord}_s(m)>n^2\).
  3. If \(m\le8s\), decide whether \(m\) is prime by trial division, and stop. Otherwise, reject \(m\) if some integer \(d\) with \(2\le d\le8s\) divides \(m\).
  4. Accept \(m\) if and only if \[ \begin{gathered}(X+a)^m\equiv X^m+a\\ \pmod{m,X^s-1}\\ \text{for all }1\le a\le8s .\end{gathered}\tag{1.1} \]

Theorem 1.1 ([OpenAI-EQF, Lemma A.1]). Step 2 always finds a prime \(s\), the algorithm accepts \(m\) if and only if \(m\) is prime, and its number of bit operations is bounded by a polynomial in \(n\).

The three claims are proved in Lemma 2.1, in Section 3 and in Section 4.

2. An auxiliary prime

Lemma 2.1. There is a prime \(s\le n^8\) with \(s\nmid m\) and \(\operatorname{ord}_s(m)>n^2\).

Proof. Put \(R=2\lfloor n^8/2\rfloor\), so that \(R\) is even and \(n^8-1\le R\le n^8\), and put \(C=\binom{R}{R/2}\). Let \(P_R\) be the product of the primes \(\ell\le R\), and \(\pi(R)\) their number.

A lower bound for \(C\). The \(R+1\) numbers \(\binom Rj\) add up to \(2^R\), and \(C\) is the largest of them (Exercise 5.1), so \(C\ge2^R/(R+1)\).

An upper bound for \(C\). For a prime \(\ell\) and an integer \(j\ge1\), the exponent of \(\ell\) in \(j!\) is \(\sum_{i\ge1}\lfloor j/\ell^i\rfloor\), since \(\lfloor j/\ell^i\rfloor\) of the numbers \(1,\ldots,j\) are divisible by \(\ell^i\). Hence the exponent of \(\ell\) in \(C=R!/((R/2)!)^2\) is \[ \sum_{i\ge1}\Bigl(\Bigl\lfloor\frac R{\ell^i}\Bigr\rfloor-2\Bigl\lfloor\frac R{2\ell^i}\Bigr\rfloor\Bigr). \] Each summand has the form \(\lfloor2y\rfloor-2\lfloor y\rfloor\in\{0,1\}\) (Exercise 5.2), and it vanishes when \(\ell^i>R\). So the exponent \(e\) of \(\ell\) in \(C\) is at most the number of \(i\ge1\) with \(\ell^i\le R\), and \(\ell^e\le R\). The prime factors of \(C\) divide \(R!\), so they are at most \(R\), and unique factorization gives \(C\le R^{\pi(R)}\). As every prime is at least \(2\), \(\pi(R)\le\log_2P_R\). Together, \[ \begin{aligned}R-\log_2(R+1)&\le\log_2C\\&\le\log_2P_R\,\log_2R .\end{aligned} \]

The estimate. The function \(x\mapsto x-8\log_2x-1\) is positive at \(x=128\), where it equals \(71\), and increasing for \(x\ge128\), since its derivative is \(1-8/(x\ln2)>0\). So \(8\log_2n+1<n\), which gives \(\log_2R\le8\log_2n<n\) and \(\log_2(R+1)\le\log_2(2n^8)<n\). Therefore \[ \begin{aligned}\log_2P_R&\ge\frac{R-\log_2(R+1)}{\log_2R}\\&>\frac{n^8-1-n}n>n^6 .\end{aligned} \]

The choice of \(s\). The positive integer \(Y=m\prod_{j=1}^{n^2}(m^j-1)\) satisfies \[ \begin{aligned}\log_2Y&<n+\sum_{j=1}^{n^2}jn\\&=n\Bigl(1+\frac{n^2(n^2+1)}2\Bigr)\\&\le n^5<n^6 ,\end{aligned} \] using \(m<2^n\) and \(1+\frac{n^2(n^2+1)}2\le n^4\) for \(n\ge2\). If every prime \(\ell\le R\) divided \(Y\), then their product \(P_R\) would divide \(Y\) by unique factorization, and \(\log_2P_R\le\log_2Y<n^6\), a contradiction. So some prime \(s\le R\le n^8\) divides neither \(m\) nor any \(m^j-1\) with \(1\le j\le n^2\), which means \(s\nmid m\) and \(\operatorname{ord}_s(m)>n^2\). \(\square\)

3. Correctness

Lemma 3.1. If \(m\) is prime, the algorithm accepts \(m\).

Proof. A prime is not of the form \(b^e\) with \(b,e\ge2\), since \(b\) would be a divisor with \(1<b<m\). Step 2 finds \(s\) by Lemma 2.1. If \(m\le8s\), trial division accepts. Otherwise no integer \(d\) with \(2\le d\le8s<m\) divides \(m\). The ring \((\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)[X]\) is an integral domain of characteristic \(m\), so \((X+a)^m=X^m+a^m\) there, and \(a^m=a\) in \(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\) by Fermat's little theorem. So (1.1) holds even without the modulus \(X^s-1\), and the algorithm accepts. \(\square\)

Proposition 3.2 ([OpenAI-EQF, Lemma A.1]; [AKS 2004, Section 4]). If \(m\) is composite, the algorithm rejects \(m\).

Proof. Suppose that a composite \(m\) is accepted. Then \(m\) is not a perfect power; step 2 found a prime \(s\le n^8\) with \(s\nmid m\) and \(\operatorname{ord}_s(m)>n^2\); \(m>8s\), since trial division rejects composite numbers; no integer from \(2\) to \(8s\) divides \(m\); and (1.1) holds. Fix a prime \(p\) dividing \(m\). Then \(p>8s\). Since \(m\) is composite and not a perfect power, it is not a power of \(p\), so it has a prime divisor \(p'\ne p\).

Introspective exponents. For \(f\in\mathbb F_p[X]\) and an integer \(k\ge1\), say that \(k\) is introspective for \(f\) if \[ \begin{gathered}f(X)^k\equiv f(X^k)\pmod{X^s-1}\\ \text{in }\mathbb F_p[X],\end{gathered} \] following [AKS 2004, Section 4]. Let \(\mathcal P\subseteq\mathbb F_p[X]\) be the set of products of polynomials \(X+a\) with \(1\le a\le8s\), repetitions allowed, including the empty product \(1\). We show that every number \(m^up^v\) with integers \(u,v\ge0\) is introspective for every \(f\in\mathcal P\).

  1. Reducing (1.1) modulo \(p\), which divides \(m\), shows that \(m\) is introspective for each \(X+a\), \(1\le a\le8s\).
  2. If \(k\) is introspective for \(f\) and for \(g\), then \((fg)^k=f^kg^k\) and \(f^kg^k\equiv f(X^k)g(X^k)=(fg)(X^k)\), so \(k\) is introspective for \(fg\). With (1), \(m\) is introspective for every \(f\in\mathcal P\).
  3. For \(f=\sum_ic_iX^i\in\mathbb F_p[X]\), the identity \((u+v)^p=u^p+v^p\) in \(\mathbb F_p[X]\), applied repeatedly, and Fermat's little theorem \(c_i^p=c_i\) give \(f(X)^p=\sum_ic_i^pX^{ip}=f(X^p)\). So \(p\) is introspective for every \(f\).
  4. If \(b\) and \(c\) are introspective for \(f\), then so is \(bc\). Indeed \(f(X)^{bc}=(f(X)^b)^c\equiv f(X^b)^c\). Substituting \(X^b\) for \(X\) in \(f(X)^c-f(X^c)=(X^s-1)G(X)\) gives \(f(X^b)^c-f(X^{bc})=(X^{bs}-1)H\) with \(H=G(X^b)\), and \(X^s-1\) divides \(X^{bs}-1\). Hence \(f(X)^{bc}\equiv f(X^{bc})\).

As \(1\) is introspective for every \(f\), (2), (3) and (4) give the claim.

A root of unity of order \(s\). By the theorem on extension fields there is a field \(E\supseteq\mathbb F_p\) containing a root \(w\) of \(1+X+\cdots+X^{s-1}\). Since \(X^s-1=(X-1)(1+X+\cdots+X^{s-1})\), \(w^s=1\). The value of \(1+X+\cdots+X^{s-1}\) at \(1\) is \(s\), which is nonzero in \(\mathbb F_p\) because \(p>s\); so \(w\ne1\), and as \(s\) is prime, \(w\) has multiplicative order exactly \(s\). Thus \(w^k\) depends only on \(k\bmod s\), and \(w^0,\ldots,w^{s-1}\) are distinct. Evaluating at \(w\), where \(X^s-1\) vanishes, gives \[ \begin{gathered}f(w)^k=f(w^k)\\ (f\in\mathcal P,\ k=m^up^v,\\ u,v\ge0).\end{gathered}\tag{3.1} \]

The group \(H\). The residues of \(m\) and \(p\) are units modulo \(s\), because \(s\nmid m\) and \(p\ne s\). Let \(H\subseteq(\mathbb Z/s\mathbb Z)^\times\) be the subgroup they generate and \(h=|H|\). It contains the \(\operatorname{ord}_s(m)\) distinct powers of \(m\), so \[ n^2<h\le s-1 . \] In a finite group the inverse of an element \(x\) is the power \(x^{o-1}\), where \(o\) is the order of \(x\); hence every element of \(H\) is the residue of some \(m^up^v\) with \(u,v\ge0\).

Many values. For a set \(A\subseteq\{1,\ldots,8s\}\) with \(h-1\) elements put \(f_A=\prod_{a\in A}(X+a)\in\mathcal P\); this is possible because \(h-1<s\). The numbers \(1,\ldots,8s\) are distinct modulo \(p\), as \(p>8s\), so \(f_A\) has the roots \(-a\), \(a\in A\), and different sets give different polynomials. Suppose \(f_A(w)=f_{A'}(w)\) with \(A\ne A'\). For every \(k=m^up^v\), (3.1) gives \(f_A(w^k)=f_A(w)^k=f_{A'}(w)^k=f_{A'}(w^k)\). As \(k\bmod s\) runs through \(H\), the elements \(w^k\) are \(h\) distinct roots of \(f_A-f_{A'}\), a nonzero polynomial of degree less than \(h-1\), since both are monic of degree \(h-1\). This contradicts the bound on the number of roots. So the values \(f_A(w)\) are distinct, and the set \(\mathcal V=\{f(w):f\in\mathcal P\}\subseteq E\) satisfies \[ |\mathcal V|\ge\binom{8s}{h-1}\ge\Bigl(\frac{8s}{h-1}\Bigr)^{h-1}\ge8^{h-1}. \] Here \(\binom Mt=\prod_{i=0}^{t-1}\frac{M-i}{t-i}\ge(M/t)^t\) for \(1\le t\le M\), since \(\frac{M-i}{t-i}\ge\frac Mt\) is equivalent to \(M\ge t\); and \(8s/(h-1)\ge8\) since \(h-1<s\).

Few values. Put \(q=\lfloor\sqrt h\rfloor\). The \((q+1)^2>h\) pairs \((u,v)\) with \(0\le u,v\le q\) give residues \(m^up^v\bmod s\) in \(H\), so two different pairs \((u,v)\ne(u',v')\) give numbers \(k_1=m^up^v\) and \(k_2=m^{u'}p^{v'}\) with \(k_1\equiv k_2\pmod s\). They are different integers: the exponent of \(p'\) in \(k_1\) is \(u\) times its exponent in \(m\), so \(k_1=k_2\) would force \(u=u'\) and then \(p^v=p^{v'}\), \(v=v'\). For every \(f\in\mathcal P\), (3.1) and \(w^{k_1}=w^{k_2}\) give \[ f(w)^{k_1}=f(w^{k_1})=f(w^{k_2})=f(w)^{k_2}, \] so every element of \(\mathcal V\) is a root of the nonzero polynomial \(T^{k_1}-T^{k_2}\in E[T]\). Its degree is at most \(\max(k_1,k_2)\), and \[ \begin{aligned}\max(k_1,k_2)&\le(mp)^q\le m^{2q}\\&<2^{2nq}\le2^{2n\sqrt h}<2^{2h},\end{aligned} \] where the last step uses \(n<\sqrt h\). Hence \(|\mathcal V|<2^{2h}\).

Conclusion. Since \(h>n^2\ge3\), \(8^{h-1}=2^{3h-3}\ge2^{2h}\), so the two bounds on \(|\mathcal V|\) contradict each other. Therefore no composite \(m\) is accepted. \(\square\)

The lower bound on \(|\mathcal V|\) adapts [AKS 2004, Lemma 4.7], which is credited there to H. W. Lenstra Jr. and was found independently by M. Macaj; the upper bound adapts [AKS 2004, Lemma 4.8], in a formulation credited there to A. Kalai, A. Sahai and M. Sudan.

4. Running time

Integers below \(2^n\) have \(n\) bits, and all integers that occur have \(O(n)\) bits.

  • Step 1: if \(m=b^e\) with \(b\ge2\), then \(2^e\le m<2^n\), so \(e<n\). For each \(e\) from \(2\) to \(n-1\), a binary search over \(1\le b\le m\) decides whether \(b^e=m\) for some integer \(b\), with \(O(n)\) evaluations of \(b^e\), each stopped as soon as the partial product exceeds \(m\).
  • Step 2: for each \(s\le n^8\), trial division by the numbers up to \(\sqrt s\le n^4\) decides whether \(s\) is prime, and \(m\bmod s\) and the residues \(m^j\bmod s\), \(1\le j\le n^2\), are computed by successive multiplication. By Lemma 2.1 a suitable \(s\) is found.
  • Step 3: at most \(\max(\sqrt{8s},8s)\le8n^8\) trial divisions of \(n\)-bit numbers.
  • Step 4: elements of \((\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)[X]/(X^s-1)\) are represented by their \(s\) coefficients, residues modulo \(m\). A product is computed with \(s^2\) multiplications of residues, reducing \(X^{s+i}\) to \(X^i\). Repeated squaring computes \((X+a)^m\) with at most \(2n\) products, and \(X^m+a\) reduces to \(X^{m\bmod s}+a\). With \(8s\le8n^8\) values of \(a\), step 4 uses \(O(n^{25})\) operations on residues.

The prime \(p\), the field \(E\) and the numbers \(k_1,k_2\) occur only in the proof and are never computed. So the number of bit operations is bounded by a polynomial in \(n\), and Theorem 1.1 is proved. \(\square\)

5. Exercises

5.1. Show that \(\binom R{j+1}\ge\binom Rj\) if and only if \(j<R/2\), and deduce that \(\binom R{R/2}\) is the largest of the numbers \(\binom Rj\) for even \(R\).

5.2. Show that \(\lfloor2y\rfloor-2\lfloor y\rfloor\in\{0,1\}\) for every real \(y\).

5.3. Let \(s\ge7\) be prime. Show that \((X+1)^6\not\equiv X^6+1\pmod{6,X^s-1}\).

5.4. Find the least prime \(s\) with \(s\nmid31\) and \(\operatorname{ord}_s(31)>4\).

5.5. In the proof of Proposition 3.2, show that \(f(w)\ne0\) for every \(f\in\mathcal P\).

6. Solutions

5.1. \(\binom R{j+1}/\binom Rj=\frac{R-j}{j+1}\), which is at least \(1\) exactly when \(R-j\ge j+1\), that is, \(j<R/2\) for even \(R\). So the numbers increase up to \(j=R/2\) and decrease after it.

5.2. Write \(y=\lfloor y\rfloor+\theta\) with \(0\le\theta<1\). Then \(\lfloor2y\rfloor=2\lfloor y\rfloor+\lfloor2\theta\rfloor\), and \(\lfloor2\theta\rfloor\in\{0,1\}\).

5.3. Modulo \(6\), \[ \begin{aligned}(X+1)^6&=X^6+6X^5+15X^4\\&\quad+20X^3+15X^2+6X+1\\&\equiv X^6+3X^4\\&\quad+2X^3+3X^2+1 .\end{aligned} \] The difference \(3X^4+2X^3+3X^2\) is not a multiple of \(X^s-1\) in \((\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z)[X]\): reducing modulo \(X^s-1\) does not change a polynomial of degree less than \(s\), and this one is nonzero.

5.4. \(31\equiv1\) modulo \(2\), \(3\) and \(5\), so the order is \(1\) for these primes. Modulo \(7\), \(31\equiv3\), with powers \(3,2,6,4,5,1\), so \(\operatorname{ord}_7(31)=6>4\), and \(s=7\).

5.5. If \(f(w)=0\), then some factor \(X+a\) of \(f\) vanishes at \(w\), that is, \(w=-a\). By (3.1) for this factor, \(w^k+a=(w+a)^k=0\) for every \(k=m^up^v\), so \(w^k=w\) and \(k\equiv1\pmod s\). Then \(H\) would be trivial, contradicting \(h>n^2\).
