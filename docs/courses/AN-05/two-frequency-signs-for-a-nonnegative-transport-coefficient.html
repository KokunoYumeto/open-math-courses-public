Two frequency signs for a nonnegative transport coefficient

A nonnegative coefficient can vanish along a moving set. A finite collection of its time derivatives still controls a fractional transverse derivative of the solution. The positive-symbol theorem already proved in this course supplies that control, but the differential symbol \(G(t,z)\eta\) has opposite signs on the two frequency half-lines. Both signs, the low frequencies and the coefficient extension must be handled explicitly.

The analytic provider is Theorem 1.1 of From scalar time estimates to an operator bound, with one transverse variable. Its full proof supplies the potential norm and gain \(1/(k+1)\), uniformly on the whole space. The positive extension below refines the construction in Section 1 of From a constant sign to a conic gain: the nonzero derivative order may vary from point to point. For the exact cutoff commutator we use the full classical product remainder, (E19)–(E21), and the finite-derivative bound (E23) in Symbols, operators and Sobolev scales, Sections 5–6. Those providers retain their declared Fourier, integration and completeness prerequisites. No further theorem is imported here.

We use \(D_t=-i\partial_t\), \(D_z=-i\partial_z\), and Fourier inversion coefficient \((2\pi)^{-1}\) in the transverse variable.

1. The local estimate, including the full potential norm

Let \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2_{t,z}\) be open and \(K\subset\Omega\) compact. Suppose \(G\in C^\infty(\Omega;\mathbb R)\), \(G\geq0\), and, for one fixed integer \(k\geq0\),

\[ \max_{0\leq j\leq k}|\partial_t^jG(t,z)|\geq1 \quad((t,z)\in\Omega). \tag{1.1} \]

The source additionally gives uniform derivative bounds on \(\Omega\). Our proof only needs bounds on a fixed compact neighborhood of \(K\); they follow from smoothness. Thus it includes the original uniform class and also the corresponding purely local statement.

Put

\[ \begin{gathered} P=D_t+iG(t,z)D_z,\\ J=(1+D_z^2)^{1/2},\qquad a=\frac1{k+1}. \end{gathered} \tag{1.2} \]

All norms below are \(L^2(\mathbb R^2)\) norms, after extending compactly supported test functions by zero.

Theorem 1.1. There is a constant \(C_K\) such that

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|D_tu\|+\|GD_zu\|+\|J^au\|\\ &\qquad\leq C_K(\|Pu\|+\|u\|),\\ &\qquad u\in C_c^\infty(K). \end{aligned} \tag{1.3} \]

In particular, the same estimate holds with \(\||D_z|^au\|\) in place of \(\|J^au\|\), which is the exact fractional term in the source. Here \(C_c^\infty(K)\) means smooth functions whose support is contained in \(K\). No regularity of the boundary of \(K\) is needed.

The constant uses a chosen cutoff neighborhood, its cutoff derivatives, and finitely many derivative bounds for \(G\) on that neighborhood. It is independent of \(u\) and is uniform when these derivative bounds and the lower bound in (1.1) are fixed. The construction below proves this uniformity rather than assuming that a cutoff preserves the finite jet.

2. Extend the coefficient without losing a finite jet

Choose a bounded open set \(U\) with \(K\subset U\), \(\overline U\subset\Omega\), and a function \(\theta\in C_c^\infty(U)\) with \(0\leq\theta\leq1\), equal to one on a neighborhood of \(K\). Define on the whole plane

\[ E=\theta G+(1-\theta). \tag{2.1} \]

The product \(\theta G\) is extended by zero. It is smooth because the support of \(\theta\) stays inside \(\Omega\). Consequently \(E\geq0\), \(E=G\) near \(K\), and \(E=1\) outside a compact set. Every mixed derivative of \(E\) is bounded.

Lemma 2.1. There is a number \(c_E>0\) such that

\[ \sum_{j=0}^k|\partial_t^jE(t,z)|\geq c_E \quad((t,z)\in\mathbb R^2). \tag{2.2} \]

Proof. If \(E(t,z)>0\), its zeroth derivative makes the sum positive. At a zero of \(E\), both nonnegative summands in (2.1) vanish. Hence \(\theta=1\) and \(G=0\) at that point.

Condition (1.1) then gives a first nonzero pure time derivative of \(G\), of order \(j\) with \(1\leq j\leq k\). Taylor's formula on that time line and nonnegativity show that \(j\) is even and its Taylor coefficient is positive. Indeed an odd first power changes sign, and a negative even first coefficient gives negative values on either side. If \(k=0\), (1.1) already excludes this zero case.

The product \(\theta G\) has the same first nonzero coefficient: all earlier time derivatives of \(G\) vanish and \(\theta=1\) at the point. The nonnegative smooth function \(1-\theta\) either has a positive even first nonzero coefficient through order \(j\), or all its derivatives through order \(j\) vanish. In the first case its earlier coefficient already survives, or its coefficient of order \(j\) adds to that of \(\theta G\). In the second case the coefficient of \(\theta G\) survives unchanged. No cancellation is possible. Thus a derivative of \(E\) of order at most \(k\) is nonzero.

The sum in (2.2) is continuous and positive on a compact set containing the support of \(\theta\). It has a positive minimum there. Outside that set \(E=1\), so the sum is one. The lesser of these two bounds is \(c_E\). This proves (2.2) for each coefficient.

We also need a uniform lower bound for a bounded class of coefficients. Fix bounds for all mixed derivatives of \(G\) through order \(k+1\) on a slightly larger compact neighborhood of the support of \(\theta\). Choose a second nonnegative compactly supported cutoff \(\psi\), equal to one near that support, inside this larger neighborhood. The functions \(g=\psi G\), extended by zero, have uniform global derivative bounds through order \(k+1\), and are nonnegative. The formula for the extension is still exactly \(E=\theta g+1-\theta\).

Here is the elementary compactness fact needed for these bounded functions. On a fixed closed ball, each derivative through order \(k\) is uniformly bounded and has a common Lipschitz bound, by the derivative bounds of order \(k+1\) and the mean-value formula along a segment. Given a sequence, take successively convergent subsequences of its derivative values at a countable dense set, and then a diagonal subsequence; there are only finitely many derivative orders at each point. For every tolerance, a finite net in the ball and the common Lipschitz bound turn convergence at those net points into uniform convergence of every derivative through order \(k\). The limits are the derivatives of one \(C^k\) function: pass to the limit in the fundamental-theorem identity along coordinate segments, first on the interior and then on the closed ball by continuity. This proves the required compactness without an additional operator or source theorem.

If the lower bound in (2.2) were not uniform, there would be coefficients \(g_n\) in this bounded class and points \(x_n\) in a fixed ball containing the support of \(\theta\), with their extended jet sums tending to zero. After passing to subsequences, \(x_n\to x_*\) and \(g_n\to g_*\) in \(C^k\) on that ball. The limit is nonnegative. At every point where \(\theta=1\), it retains the lower time-jet bound (1.1), because \(\psi=1\) there and the jets converge. At \(x_*\), the extension \(E_*=\theta g_*+1-\theta\) has all time derivatives through order \(k\) equal to zero. Its zeroth derivative forces \(\theta(x_*)=1\) and \(g_*(x_*)=0\). The preceding first-nonzero-coefficient argument applies to the \(C^k\) function \(g_*\), using Taylor's formula with its small-o remainder. It shows that some jet of \(E_*\) through order \(k\) is nonzero, a contradiction. For \(k=0\), \(g_*(x_*)\geq1\) is already the contradiction. Thus \(c_E\) can be chosen uniformly using only the stated finite derivative bounds and the fixed cutoffs. ∎

This argument allows a different first nonzero order at different zeros. It also allows \(1-\theta\) to have nonflat contact with zero. Both points matter when extending a coefficient with a moving zero set.

3. The exact positive-symbol interface

On the whole plane consider

\[ q_0(t,z,\eta)=E(t,z)|\eta|, \qquad \mathcal Q=E(t,z)J. \tag{3.1} \]

The principal symbol \(q_0\) is nonnegative, smooth for \(\eta\ne0\), and positively homogeneous of degree one. All its ordinary symbol and time derivatives are uniformly bounded in the appropriate order. Equation (2.2) gives

\[ \sum_{j=0}^k|\partial_t^jq_0(t,z,\eta)| \geq c_E|\eta|. \tag{3.2} \]

The full left symbol of \(\mathcal Q\) is exactly \(E(t,z)(1+\eta^2)^{1/2}\). It differs from a smooth low-frequency cutoff of \(q_0\) by an ordinary order-zero symbol. To check this directly, outside \(|\eta|\geq2\) use

\[ \sqrt{1+\eta^2}-|\eta| =\frac1{\sqrt{1+\eta^2}+|\eta|}, \tag{3.3} \]

whose derivatives have decreasing orders. On the remaining compact frequency interval the cutoff and full symbol are smooth and bounded, with all derivatives bounded. The same bounds hold after any time or spatial derivatives because those only differentiate \(E\).

Every hypothesis of the internal positive-symbol theorem is therefore satisfied, with one transverse variable and this same integer \(k\). That theorem gives

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|EJw\|+\|J^aw\|\\ &\quad\leq C_E \big(\|(D_t+iEJ)w\|+\|w\|\big),\\ &\qquad w\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R^2). \end{aligned} \tag{3.4} \]

It supplies the full \(EJw\) norm, which will control both the original potential and the time derivative. A fractional norm alone would not prove (1.3).

Let \((Rw)(t,z)=w(-t,z)\) and \(E^-(t,z)=E(-t,z)\). The map \(R\) is unitary and preserves \(\mathcal S\). Direct differentiation gives

\[ \begin{aligned} RD_t&=-D_tR,\qquad RJ=JR,\\ R(EJ)&=E^-JR,\\ R(D_t-iEJ)&=-(D_t+iE^-J)R. \end{aligned} \tag{3.5} \]

The coefficient \(E^-\) has the same nonnegativity, bounded derivatives and finite-jet lower bound; the signs \((-1)^j\) disappear in the absolute jet sum. Apply (3.4) to \(E^-\) and \(Rw\), then use (3.5). The resulting estimate is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|EJw\|+\|J^aw\|\\ &\quad\leq C_E \big(\|(D_t-iEJ)w\|+\|w\|\big). \end{aligned} \tag{3.6} \]

Increase the common constant if necessary. This proves the second operator orientation; positivity of \(E\) does not imply positivity of \(E\eta\) on negative frequencies.

4. A fixed three-part frequency split

Choose a smooth function \(h:\mathbb R\to[0,1]\), zero on \(( -\infty,1]\) and one on \([2,\infty)\). Set

\[ \begin{aligned} \chi_+(\eta)&=h(\eta),\qquad \chi_-(\eta)=h(-\eta),\\ \chi_0(\eta)&=1-\chi_+(\eta)-\chi_-(\eta). \end{aligned} \tag{4.1} \]

Their sum is one, each takes values in \([0,1]\), and \(\chi_0\) is supported in \([-2,2]\). Write \(T_s=\chi_s(D_z)\) for \(s\in\{+, -,0\}\), and

\[ w_s=T_su,\qquad u=w_++w_-+w_0. \tag{4.2} \]

Each multiplier preserves \(\mathcal S\), commutes with \(D_t,D_z,J\), and has \(L^2\) norm at most one by Plancherel. If \(u\in C_c^\infty(K)\), the functions \(w_s\) remain compactly supported in time, but need not remain compactly supported in space. They do belong to \(\mathcal S(\mathbb R^2)\). This is why Section 2 extended the coefficient globally before applying the analytic theorem.

Define \(P_E=D_t+iED_z\) and \(f=P_Eu\). Since \(E=G\) near \(K\), we have \(f=Pu\) as smooth functions on the whole plane. The exact commutator identity on \(\mathcal S\) is

\[ \begin{gathered} P_Ew_s=T_sf+i\mathcal R_su,\\ \mathcal R_s=[E,T_s]D_z. \end{gathered} \tag{4.3} \]

Indeed the time commutator vanishes and \(ED_zT_s-T_sED_z=[E,T_s]D_z\). The frequency split is independent of time, so there is no additional derivative of \(\chi_s\).

Lemma 4.1. Uniformly in time,

\[ \|\mathcal R_sv\|_{L^2_z} \leq C_E\|v\|_{L^2_z}. \tag{4.4} \]

Proof. Multiplication by \(E\) and each \(T_s\) have scalar symbols in \(S^0_{1,0}\), with uniform parameter seminorms. The exact product (E19) and its \(N=1\) remainder (E21) show that \([E,T_s]\) has a symbol in \(S^{-1}_{1,0}\): the two zeroth products are both \(E\chi_s\) and cancel. Composition on the right with the symbol \(\eta\) gives an \(S^0_{1,0}\) symbol. In fact this last right composition is multiplication of the symbol by \(\eta\), since the symbol \(\eta\) has no base derivatives. The operator identity is valid on \(\mathcal S\) by that same exact product theorem.

The finite-derivative bound (E23) supplies (4.4), with finitely many uniform derivatives of \(E\) and the fixed \(\chi_s\). These symbol derivatives are bounded uniformly in \(t\). Integration of the squared estimate in \(t\) proves the full \(L^2_{t,z}\) bound. ∎

Thus (4.3) gives, for every branch,

\[ \|P_Ew_s\|\leq\|f\|+C_E\|u\|. \tag{4.5} \]

No replacement of the cutoff commutator by a pointwise product has been made.

5. Both high-frequency branches and the low remainder

On the positive branch the multiplier \((\sqrt{1+\eta^2}-\eta)\chi_+(\eta)\) is bounded. Its support has \(\eta>1\), and (3.3) gives the more precise bound \(\sqrt2-1\). Consequently

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|E(J-D_z)w_+\|\leq C_E\|u\|,\\ &(D_t+iEJ)w_+=P_Ew_+\\ &\qquad\qquad+iE(J-D_z)w_+. \end{aligned} \tag{5.1} \]

Insert (4.5) and (5.1) in (3.4). We obtain

\[ \|EJw_+\|+\|J^aw_+\| \leq C_E(\|f\|+\|u\|). \tag{5.2} \]

Equations (5.1)–(5.2) also bound \(ED_zw_+\), and \(D_tw_+=P_Ew_+-iED_zw_+\) bounds its time derivative. Hence

\[ \|D_tw_+\|+\|ED_zw_+\|+\|J^aw_+\| \leq C_E(\|f\|+\|u\|). \tag{5.3} \]

On the negative branch use the bounded multiplier \((\sqrt{1+\eta^2}+\eta)\chi_-(\eta)\), with the same bound. Now the exact comparison is

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|E(J+D_z)w_-\|\leq C_E\|u\|,\\ &(D_t-iEJ)w_-=P_Ew_-\\ &\qquad\qquad-iE(D_z+J)w_-. \end{aligned} \tag{5.4} \]

Apply (3.6), rather than (3.4), to (5.4). It bounds \(EJw_-\) and \(J^aw_-\). Since \(ED_zw_-=-EJw_-+E(D_z+J)w_-\), (4.5) then bounds the time derivative as well. Thus

\[ \|D_tw_-\|+\|ED_zw_-\|+\|J^aw_-\| \leq C_E(\|f\|+\|u\|). \tag{5.5} \]

The low branch needs no finite-type inequality. Its compact frequency support gives directly

\[ \begin{aligned} \|ED_zw_0\|&\leq2\|E\|_\infty\|u\|,\\ \|J^aw_0\|&\leq5^{a/2}\|u\|. \end{aligned} \tag{5.6} \]

Finally \(D_tw_0=P_Ew_0-iED_zw_0\), so (4.5)–(5.6) imply

\[ \|D_tw_0\|+\|ED_zw_0\|+\|J^aw_0\| \leq C_E(\|f\|+\|u\|). \tag{5.7} \]

Sum (5.3), (5.5) and (5.7), using (4.2), linearity and the triangle inequality. This proves (1.3) with \(E\) in place of \(G\) and \(f=P_Eu\). On the support of \(u\) and its derivatives, \(E=G\), so those expressions equal \(GD_zu\) and \(Pu\). This proves Theorem 1.1. Finally \(|\eta|^a\leq\langle\eta\rangle^a\); Plancherel gives its stated homogeneous fractional consequence. ∎

The two high-frequency branches use the same finite type and gain. The time reflection changes the operator orientation while retaining the coefficient bounds. The original coefficient may depend on both variables throughout; no spatially frozen coefficient has been substituted in this proof.

6. Bounded perturbations and the next analytic obstacle

For any bounded complex multiplication coefficient \(B\) on a neighborhood of \(K\), let \(P_B=P+B\). Theorem 1.1 and \(Pu=P_Bu-Bu\) give

\[ \begin{aligned} &\|D_tu\|+\|GD_zu\|+\|J^au\|\\ &\quad\leq C_K\big(\|P_Bu\| +(1+\|B\|_\infty)\|u\|\big). \end{aligned} \tag{6.1} \]

This includes a bounded real affine intercept through \(B=iF\). However, an intercept of size \(\rho\) leaves an ordinary norm error of size \(\rho\). Equation (6.1) alone does not absorb that error into a desired \(\rho^\varepsilon\|u\|\) term when \(0<\varepsilon<1\). The subsequent polynomial and almost-increasing-quotient model estimates must use the full bracket lower bound and oriented sign information. They remain separate obligations.

7. Graded exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 1 — basic. Derive (4.3), including its sign. Explain why the same formula would acquire another term for a cutoff \(\chi_s(t,D_z)\).

Solution. Set \(f=D_tu+iED_zu\). Since \(D_tT_s=T_sD_t\) and \(D_zT_s=T_sD_z\), subtract \(T_sf\) from \(P_ET_su\). The remainder is \(i(ET_s-T_sE)D_zu=i[E,T_s]D_zu\). If the cutoff depends on time, \([D_t,T_s]=-i(\partial_t\chi_s)(t,D_z)\) appears as well. A phase-symbol bound at each time does not bound this extra time derivative.

Exercise 2 — basic. Compute the orientation under time reflection. Verify exactly the last identity in (3.5) and identify which estimate applies to negative transverse frequencies.

Solution. Differentiation of \(w(-t,z)\) gives \(RD_t=-D_tR\), whereas transverse Fourier multipliers commute with \(R\). Multiplication by \(E\) becomes multiplication by \(E^-\). Therefore

\[ \begin{aligned} R(D_t-iEJ)&=-D_tR-iE^-JR\\ &=-(D_t+iE^-J)R. \end{aligned} \]

On negative frequencies \(D_z\) is approximately \(-J\), so the comparison operator is \(D_t-iEJ\), and (3.6) applies. Both norms are unchanged by the reflection.

Exercise 3 — intermediate. For

\[ G(t,z)=2\big(1-\cos(t-\sin z)\big), \tag{7.1} \]

verify a global version of (1.1) with \(k=2\). State the estimate and describe its zero set.

Solution. All mixed derivatives are bounded: repeated differentiation produces finite sums of products of bounded sine and cosine functions. Write \(s=t-\sin z\). Then \(G_t=2\sin s\) and \(G_{tt}=2\cos s\). If \(\cos s\leq1/2\), then \(G\geq1\). If \(\cos s>1/2\), then \(G_{tt}>1\). Thus the maximum of the jets through order two is at least one everywhere. The zeros are the moving curves \(t=\sin z+2\pi n\), \(n\in\mathbb Z\). Theorem 1.1 gives gain \(1/3\), together with the full \(GD_z\) and \(D_t\) norms, for each compact test-function support. The finite type is uniform although the zero curves are not stationary in \(z\).

Exercise 4 — intermediate. Show why smooth nonnegativity alone gives no positive uniform transverse gain. On a neighborhood of \(t=0\), take \(G(0)=0\) and \(G(t)=e^{-1/t^2}\) for \(t\ne0\), independent of \(z\).

Solution. Choose nonzero \(f\in C_c^\infty((-1,1))\) and \(v\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)\), with norms one and supports inside a fixed compact rectangle. For \(\Lambda>1\), choose \(A>1\), put \(\ell=(A\log\Lambda)^{-1/2}\), and set

\[ u_\Lambda(t,z)=\ell^{-1/2}f(t/\ell) e^{i\Lambda z}v(z). \tag{7.2} \]

Its norm is one, and \(\|D_tu_\Lambda\|=\ell^{-1}\|f'\|\). On its time support \(G\leq e^{-1/\ell^2}=\Lambda^{-A}\), so \(\|GD_zu_\Lambda\|\leq\Lambda^{-A}(\Lambda+\|v'\|)\). Thus the equation norm is \(O(\sqrt{\log\Lambda})\).

For every \(s>0\), the transverse norm is at least \(c_s\Lambda^s\) for large \(\Lambda\). To see this without a compact Fourier support assumption, choose a fixed frequency interval on which \(\widehat v\) has positive \(L^2\) mass. Modulation translates that interval by \(\Lambda\), where \(|\eta|\geq\Lambda/2\) for large \(\Lambda\). Plancherel gives the lower bound. It contradicts any estimate of that norm by the equation norm plus \(\|u_\Lambda\|\). Every pure time derivative of \(G\) vanishes at zero; (1.1) fails for every finite \(k\).

Exercise 5 — intermediate. Explain the endpoint \(k=0\), and then derive the exact bounded-perturbation dependence in (6.1). Why does this dependence not prove the later large-parameter estimate for a real intercept of size \(\rho\)?

Solution. For \(k=0\), (1.1) says \(G\geq1\). The extension is strictly positive, the provider has \(a=1\), and the same three-branch proof gives the complete first transverse derivative, time derivative and potential norm. No zero-jet Taylor argument is needed. For the perturbation, \(\|Pu\|\leq\|P_Bu\|+\|B\|_\infty\|u\|\), which inserted in (1.3) gives exactly (6.1). If \(B=iF\) and \(\|F\|_\infty\) is comparable to \(\rho\), its residual norm is comparable to \(\rho\|u\|\). It cannot be absorbed uniformly by \(\rho^\varepsilon\|u\|\) when \(\varepsilon<1\), since \(\rho^{1-\varepsilon}\) is unbounded. Further bracket and quotient information is required; a constant allowed to grow with \(\rho\) does not establish that later uniform theorem.

Exercise 6 — advanced. On a fixed rectangle around the origin take \(G(t,z)=t^{2m}\), \(m\geq1\). Prove that the gain \(1/(2m+1)\) in Theorem 1.1 cannot be enlarged for this coefficient.

Solution. The coefficient is nonnegative and its \(2m\)-th time derivative is \((2m)!\), so the hypotheses hold with \(k=2m\). Put \(a=1/(2m+1)\), choose nonzero normalized compactly supported \(f,v\) as in Exercise 4, and define

\[ u_\Lambda(t,z)=\Lambda^{a/2}f(\Lambda^at) e^{i\Lambda z}v(z). \tag{7.3} \]

The norm is one, the time derivative norm is \(O(\Lambda^a)\), and \(G\leq C\Lambda^{-2ma}\) on the time support. Hence the potential norm is \(O(\Lambda^{1-2ma})=O(\Lambda^a)\), and so is the equation norm. The same translated fixed-frequency interval argument gives \(\||D_z|^su_\Lambda\|\geq c_s\Lambda^s\) for every \(s>0\). For \(s>a\) this contradicts a purported stronger estimate with a constant independent of \(\Lambda\). All test-function supports lie in one fixed compact rectangle for large \(\Lambda\). Thus the fractional exponent is sharp in this even-order finite-type example.
