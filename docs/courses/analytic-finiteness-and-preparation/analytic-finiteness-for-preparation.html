Analytic preparation, cells and curves

Analytic finiteness for preparation

Written by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), October 2026; self-checked by the writing AI. Original text: public domain (CC0). The theorems and the route of proof are those of Guillaume Valette, On subanalytic geometry, arXiv:2507.23622v1, Chapter 1 and §2.3. Global subanalytic sets are defined by the projective product convention of Jean-Marie Lion and Jean-Philippe Rolin, Théorème de préparation pour les fonctions logarithmico-exponentielles.

This reading derives the finiteness theorems of global subanalytic geometry from Weierstrass division. Division makes the ring of convergent real power series Noetherian, and the Artin–Rees lemma and Krull’s intersection theorem follow. A linear approximation theorem then shows that a real analytic function of (u,z)(u,z) is controlled, near a point, by finitely many of its Taylor coefficients in zz:

ψ(u,z)=∑i=0dci(u)(z−z0)iAi(u,z),Ai(u0,z0)=1, \psi(u,z)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}c_i(u)(z-z_0)^iA_i(u,z), \qquad A_i(u_0,z_0)=1,

with analytic functions AiA_i. Dividing by the largest coefficient produces a family whose order in zz is bounded uniformly. This is the analytic input to the preparation theorem: on the cells of a finite decomposition, every function built from restricted analytic functions and rational powers becomes a base coefficient times a power of a distance |z−θ(x)||z-\theta(x)| times an analytic unit. The cell decomposition theorem for globally subanalytic sets, Gabrielov’s complement theorem, convergent Puiseux expansions and the dimension theory of the last section are consequences.

Real analytic division and finite generation

Let Rn=ℝ{x1,…,xn}R_n=\mathbb R\{x_1,\ldots,x_n\} be the ring of germs at 0∈ℝn0\in\mathbb R^n of real analytic functions, that is, of real power series with a positive radius of convergence, and let 𝔪n⊂Rn\mathfrak m_n\subset R_n consist of the germs vanishing at 00. Translation transfers every statement below to germs at an arbitrary point.

The ring RnR_n is local with maximal ideal 𝔪n\mathfrak m_n. A germ with f(0)≠0f(0)\ne0 has an analytic reciprocal near 00, and a germ with f(0)=0f(0)=0 has no inverse. The ideal 𝔪n\mathfrak m_n is generated by x1,…,xnx_1,\ldots,x_n. To see this, give each monomial xα≠1x^\alpha\ne1 of a series without constant term to the smallest index ii with αi>0\alpha_i>0, and write it as xixα−eix_i x^{\alpha-e_i}. The monomials given to ii form xigix_ig_i, where gig_i is a subseries of an absolutely convergent series, so convergent on the same polydisc.

The complex preparation and division theorems are proved in Preparation by the zeros in one fibre and Division, finite generation and elementary algebra, Theorem 1 and Corollary 3 there. They descend to real germs because the factors are unique. Call g∈Rng\in R_n regular of order ss in xnx_n if g(0,…,0,xn)=xnsh(xn)g(0,\ldots,0,x_n)=x_n^s\,h(x_n) with h(0)≠0h(0)\ne0. Regard gg as a complex germ and write g=uPg=uP, with uu a unit and P=xns+∑k=1sak(x′)xns−kP=x_n^s+\sum_{k=1}^sa_k(x')x_n^{s-k} a Weierstrass polynomial. Replacing every coefficient of the power series of uu and PP by its complex conjugate gives a second such factorization of the real germ gg. By uniqueness the two coincide, so uu and PP have real coefficients. In the same way, for real ff the unique division

f=qP+∑j=0s−1rj(x′)xnj f=qP+\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}r_j(x')x_n^j

has real qq and real rj∈Rn−1r_j\in R_{n-1}. Thus a real germ regular of order ss in xnx_n is a real unit times a real Weierstrass polynomial of degree ss, and every f∈Rnf\in R_n is uniquely qPqP plus a polynomial of degree less than ss in xnx_n with coefficients in Rn−1R_{n-1}.

Regularity can be produced by a real linear change of coordinates. A nonzero real polynomial FF does not vanish on all of ℝn\mathbb R^n. Indeed, view FF as a polynomial in xnx_n whose coefficients are polynomials in the other variables. By induction one of these coefficients is nonzero at some real point, and there the resulting nonzero polynomial in xnx_n has a non-root. If g≠0g\ne0 has order mm and lowest homogeneous part gmg_m, choose v∈ℝnv\in\mathbb R^n with gm(v)≠0g_m(v)\ne0 and a linear isomorphism LL with L(en)=vL(e_n)=v. Then g(L(0,…,0,t))=g(tv)=tmgm(v)+O(tm+1)g(L(0,\ldots,0,t))=g(tv)=t^mg_m(v)+O(t^{m+1}), so g∘Lg\circ L is regular of order mm in xnx_n. Applying this to a product of finitely many germs makes all of them regular at once.

We use the module fact established in the proof of Theorem 6 of the division reading: over a Noetherian ring every submodule of a finite free module is finitely generated.

Polynomial rings and stable filtrations

Hilbert basis theorem. If AA is a Noetherian ring, so is A[T]A[T].

Proof. Let J⊂A[T]J\subset A[T] be an ideal. The leading coefficients of the elements of JJ, together with 00, form an ideal of AA: multiplying by a power of TT aligns degrees before adding. Pick finitely many elements f1,…,fkf_1,\ldots,f_k of JJ such that their leading coefficients generate that ideal, and let DD be the largest of their degrees. If f∈Jf\in J has degree N≥DN\ge D and leading coefficient ∑cilc⁡(fi)\sum c_i\operatorname{lc}(f_i), then f−∑ciTN−deg⁡fifif-\sum c_iT^{N-\deg f_i}f_i lies in JJ and has smaller degree. Krull intersection for modules

Let AA be a Noetherian local ring with maximal ideal 𝔪\mathfrak m. Its non-units are exactly the elements of 𝔪\mathfrak m.

Nakayama’s lemma. If a finitely generated module LL satisfies L=𝔪LL=\mathfrak mL, then L=0L=0.

Proof. Suppose L≠0L\ne0 and let e1,…,epe_1,\ldots,e_p, p≥1p\ge1, be a generating set of minimal size. Write ep=∑jbjeje_p=\sum_jb_je_j with all bj∈𝔪b_j\in\mathfrak m. Proof. The intersection LL of all 𝔪iM\mathfrak m^iM is a submodule of MM. For ii large, the Artin–Rees lemma with N=LN=L gives L=L∩𝔪i+1M=𝔪(L∩𝔪iM)=𝔪LL=L\cap\mathfrak m^{i+1}M=\mathfrak m(L\cap\mathfrak m^iM)=\mathfrak mL. Nakayama’s lemma gives L=0L=0. ▫\square

Applied to M/NM/N, whose submodules 𝔪i(M/N)\mathfrak m^i(M/N) are the images of 𝔪iM\mathfrak m^iM, the theorem says that every submodule of a finitely generated module is closed in the 𝔪\mathfrak m-adic sense:

⋂i≥0(N+𝔪iM)=N. \bigcap_{i\ge0}\left(N+\mathfrak m^iM\right)=N.

Formal linear solutions have convergent replacements

Let A=RnA=R_n, let Â=ℝ[[x1,…,xn]]\widehat A=\mathbb R[[x_1,\ldots,x_n]] be the ring of formal power series, and let HH be a b×ab\times a matrix with entries in AA. It defines linear maps Aa→AbA^a\to A^b and Âa→Âb\widehat A^a\to\widehat A^b.

Proof. Let pkp_k be the vector of polynomials obtained by truncating each component of f̂\widehat f after degree kk. Then v−Hpk=H(f̂−pk)v-Hp_k=H(\widehat f-p_k) has no terms of degree at most kk, because multiplication by a power series cannot lower the order. It is also a vector of convergent series. A convergent series without terms of degree at most kk belongs to 𝔪k+1\mathfrak m^{k+1}. To see this, assign each of its monomials to one of the finitely many monomials of degree k+1k+1 dividing it, and collect: the series becomes a finite sum of such monomials times convergent subseries. Hence v∈H(Aa)+𝔪k+1Abv\in H(A^a)+\mathfrak m^{k+1}A^b for every kk, and the closedness of submodules gives v∈H(Aa)v\in H(A^a).

For the second statement let q≥1q\ge1 and let pp be the truncation of f̂\widehat f below degree qq. Grouping as before, f̂=p+∑|α|=qxαĝα\widehat f=p+\sum_{|\alpha|=q}x^\alpha\widehat g_\alpha with formal vectors ĝα\widehat g_\alpha. So the linear system ∑|α|=qxαHgα=v−Hp\sum_{|\alpha|=q}x^\alpha Hg_\alpha=v-Hp, in the unknowns gα∈Aag_\alpha\in A^a, has a formal solution. By the first part it has a convergent solution gαg_\alpha, and f=p+∑xαgαf=p+\sum x^\alpha g_\alpha solves Hf=vHf=v with the required low-degree coefficients. ▫\square

The theorem replaces a formal solution by a convergent one. It says nothing about convergence of the given formal solution, and it concerns linear systems only.

A finite Taylor family with analytic units

Signed normalization and a uniform order bound

Suppose that near (u0,z0)(u_0,z_0)

ψ(u,z)=∑i=0dci(u)(z−z0)iAi(u,z),Ai(u0,z0)≠0. \psi(u,z)=\sum_{i=0}^dc_i(u)(z-z_0)^iA_i(u,z),\qquad A_i(u_0,z_0)\ne0.

Here is how the bound is used. Let ϕ\phi be a map from a set BB to the parameter space and put gi=ci∘ϕg_i=c_i\circ\phi. On a part of BB where gm≠0g_m\ne0 and |gi|≤|gm||g_i|\le|g_m| for every ii,

ψ(ϕ(x),z)=gm(x)Q(g0(x)gm(x),…,gd(x)gm(x),ϕ(x),z), \psi(\phi(x),z)=g_m(x)\,Q\Bigl(\frac{g_0(x)}{g_m(x)},\ldots,\frac{g_d(x)}{g_m(x)},\phi(x),z\Bigr),

Arranging the gig_i into pieces of this kind is part of the cell induction below and is not assumed here. If ψ\psi is analytic near a compact set of parameters, finitely many of the neighbourhoods above cover it, and the induction refines their preimages compatibly.

How this supplies the geometric argument

The analytic chain proved so far is: real division gives Noetherianity of convergent germs; the Rees algebra gives the Artin–Rees lemma; Artin–Rees and Nakayama give Krull’s theorem and the closedness of submodules; closedness gives convergent solutions of formal linear systems; these give the finite Taylor family, whose normalization has a uniformly bounded order. The remaining sections build the cell induction on this input. They prove the preparation of functions built from restricted analytic functions and rational powers, the cell theorem, the complement theorem and convergent Puiseux expansions under the projective product definition. The curve-selection and Łojasiewicz reading uses the cell theorem and the Puiseux expansions proved here.

A convergent split before restricting to a hyperbola

On the hyperbola c=zwc=zw a monomial ziwjz^iw^j can be written as a power of cc times a pure power of zz or of ww, according to whether i>ji>j or i≤ji\le j. Analytic units and the lower-dimensional induction

The preparation theorem is proved for a class of functions closed under the operations it needs. A restricted analytic function is a function on a closed cube Q⊂ℝmQ\subset\mathbb R^m that extends analytically to a neighbourhood of QQ. An ℒ\mathcal L-function on a set E⊂ℝmE\subset\mathbb R^m is a function given on EE by a finite expression built from the coordinate functions and real constants with sums, products, rational powers t↦tλt\mapsto t^\lambda and restricted analytic functions, each operation applied only where it is defined. A non-integer power requires t>0t>0, a negative integer power requires t≠0t\ne0, and a restricted analytic function requires its argument to lie in its cube.

ℒ\mathcal L-cells are defined inductively, one ambient dimension at a time. The only cell in ℝ0\mathbb R^0 is the point. The global definition and its elementary set operations

Let 𝑷=ℝ∪{∞}\mathbf P=\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\} be the real projective line, and regard ℝn\mathbb R^n as a subset of the compact analytic manifold 𝑷n\mathbf P^n. A subset ZZ of an analytic manifold is semianalytic if every point of the manifold has a neighbourhood VV such that Z∩VZ\cap V is a finite Boolean combination of sets {g=0}\{g=0\} and {g>0}\{g>0\} with gg analytic on VV. Dimension, fibrewise closure and the frontier

The results below are those of §2.3 of Valette’s notes cited at the head of this reading. In this section definable means globally subanalytic, in the projective product convention above. Closures and interiors are taken in the ambient Euclidean space, and the Boolean and projection calculus shows that they are definable. Besides that calculus, the proofs use the cell theorem, the bounded-chart comparison and definable choice. They use no resolution, uniformization or regular-locus theorem.

Every cell of a cylindrical decomposition is an analytic manifold. Its dimension is the number of bands used to build it: a graph over a cell has the dimension of that cell, and a band over it has one more. If a cell has dimension kk, forgetting its graph coordinates maps it homeomorphically onto an open set V⊂ℝkV\subset\mathbb R^k. The inverse σ:V→ℝn\sigma:V\to\mathbb R^n is analytic, because each graph coordinate is an analytic function of the coordinates before it.

For a nonempty definable set A⊂ℝnA\subset\mathbb R^n and a finite cylindrical decomposition into analytic cells compatible with AA, put

dim⁡A=max⁡{dim⁡C:C a cell of the decomposition with C⊂A},(D1) \dim A=\max\{\dim C:\ C\text{ a cell of the decomposition with }C\subset A\}, \qquad\text{(D1)}

and dim⁡⌀=−1\dim\varnothing=-1. We also use the frontier and the topological boundary,

fr⁡A=A¯\A,∂A=A¯\int⁡A.(D2) \operatorname{fr}A=\overline A\setminus A,\qquad \partial A=\overline A\setminus\operatorname{int}A. \qquad\text{(D2)}

They are different sets, and the strict inequality of this section concerns the frontier.

In the same way, (D5) applies to an analytic or locally subanalytic map once its graph is restricted to a closed box in a chart, where it becomes globally subanalytic.
