Draft lesson.

Essential extensions and filtered test categories

Original exposition: CC0 1.0.

Essential extensions prevent a nonzero subobject from escaping the original object. Filtered colimits preserve this property in a Grothendieck category. We use these extensions to give another construction of injectives, and then examine the categorical tests that make filteredness and split maximality visible.

Fix a Grothendieck universe \(\mathcal U\) and ambient choice. Hom sets and indexing families called small belong to that universe. A category can have a larger ambient object set. An infinite cardinal \(\pi\) below is small; a bounded diagram has fewer than \(\pi\) total arrows, including identities and composites. Complete encodings let us transport any such shape into the working universe without discarding arrows.

The prerequisites are the complete small-subobject and quotient constructions, generator injectivity criterion and filtered unions, ordinary module colimits and exactness, cardinal-filtered Set comparison, ordinary Ind construction, and categorical maximality. Each is used through its stated hypotheses.

1. Essentiality through kernels

Let \(\mathcal A\) be abelian. A monomorphism \(i: X\hookrightarrow Y\) is essential when every subobject \(W\hookrightarrow Y\) whose pullback intersection with \(X\) is zero must itself be zero.

Theorem 1.1. A monomorphism \(i: X\hookrightarrow Y\) is essential if and only if, for every \(g: Y\to Z\), monicity of \(gi\) implies monicity of \(g\).

Proof. The pullback \((\ker g)\cap X\) is the kernel of \(gi\), by the kernel and pullback universal properties. If \(gi\) is monic, this intersection is zero. Essentiality gives \(\ker g=0\), so \(g\) is monic.

Conversely, for a subobject \(W\subseteq Y\) disjoint from \(X\), take \(q: Y\to Y/W\). Its kernel is \(W\), and the same pullback identifies the kernel of \(qi\) with \(W\cap X=0\). The assumed test makes \(q\) monic. Hence \(W=\ker q=0\), proving essentiality. \(\square\)

Lemma 1.2. Essential monomorphisms compose. A subobject containing an essential subobject is essential in the same target.

Proof. For essential \(X\subseteq Y\subseteq Z\), if \(W\cap X=0\), then \((W\cap Y)\cap X=0\), so \(W\cap Y=0\), and then \(W=0\). For the second assertion, \(W\cap Y=0\) implies \(W\cap X=0\), so \(W=0\). Pullback associativity justifies all these intersection equalities. \(\square\)

An arrow between two essential extensions of a fixed \(X\), commuting with their specified maps from \(X\), is automatically monic by Theorem 1.1. The identity extension is essential. For \(X=0\), its only essential extensions have zero target.

2. Quotients that retain the essential part

Now let \(\mathcal A\) be Grothendieck. Its subobject classes are small, and its filtered colimits are exact.

Theorem 2.1. Every monomorphism \(i: X\hookrightarrow Y\) factors through an essential monomorphism \[ X\overset{e}{\hookrightarrow}Q, \qquad e=qi,\qquad q: Y\twoheadrightarrow Q. \tag{2.1} \]

Proof. Consider the set of subobjects \(K\subseteq Y\) with \(K\cap X=0\). It contains zero. Every nonempty inclusion chain has a union in \(Y\), and that union still has zero intersection with \(X\): filtered exactness preserves the pullback intersection, so \[ X\cap\bigcup_tK_t=\bigcup_t(X\cap K_t)=0. \tag{2.2} \] For the displayed intersection identity, apply exact filtered colimits to the kernels of \(X\oplus K_t\to Y\), whose two components are the inclusion of \(X\) and minus the inclusion of \(K_t\). The colimit kernel is the intersection of \(X\) with the union subobject. The categorical union construction is proved in the cited filtered-union lesson. Zero bounds the empty chain. Zorn's lemma supplies a maximal \(K\). Set \(Q=Y/K\), with quotient \(q\). The kernel of \(qi\) is \(K\cap X=0\), so \(e=qi\) is monic.

Let \(L\subseteq Q\) have \(L\cap e(X)=0\), and let \(P=q^{-1}(L)\subseteq Y\). It contains \(K\). The image under \(q\) of \(P\cap X\) lies in that zero intersection, so \(P\cap X\subseteq K\cap X=0\). Thus \(P\cap X=0\), and maximality forces \(P=K\). The pullback map \(P\to L\) is epic, since an epimorphism in an abelian category remains epic after pullback. Its image is zero when \(P=K\); consequently \(L=0\). Hence \(e\) is essential. The pullback-epimorphism assertion is also proved by the signed cokernel argument in module presentations, Lemma 8.1. \(\square\)

Proposition 2.2. An object \(E\) of a Grothendieck category is injective if and only if every essential monomorphism \(E\hookrightarrow V\) is an isomorphism.

Proof. If \(E\) is injective, extend \(1_E\) across any monomorphism \(j: E\to V\), obtaining \(r: V\to E\) with \(rj=1_E\). Its kernel is disjoint from \(j(E)\). If \(j\) is essential, that kernel is zero. The split-kernel decomposition \(V\simeq E\oplus\ker r\) makes \(j\) invertible.

Conversely, apply Theorem 2.1 to an arbitrary monomorphism \(j: E\hookrightarrow V\). The induced essential map \(e: E\to V/K\) is invertible by hypothesis. Then \(e^{-1}q: V\to E\) splits \(j\). Thus every monomorphism out of \(E\) splits.

To prove injectivity from this property, take a monomorphism \(A\hookrightarrow B\) and a map \(A\to E\). Their pushout gives a monomorphism \(E\hookrightarrow P\), because pushouts preserve monomorphisms in an abelian category. Choose its retraction \(P\to E\). Composing \(B\to P\to E\) extends the original map from \(A\), by the pushout square. The pushout-monomorphism fact is the dual of the cited pullback-epimorphism fact; finite kernel/cokernel constructions establish it with no injective-existence hypothesis. This proves injectivity. \(\square\)

This criterion was proved using quotients and pushouts. It is therefore available before constructing enough injectives.

3. Filtered colimits of essential extensions

Theorem 3.1. Let \(I\) be small and filtered, and let \(i\mapsto(e_i: X\hookrightarrow Y_i)\) be a diagram of essential extensions. Then \[ e: X\longrightarrow L=\operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}Y_i \tag{3.1} \] is an essential monomorphism.

Proof. A filtered category is nonempty and connected, so the colimit of its constant \(X\)-diagram, with identity transition maps, is \(X\). A compatible cocone on that constant diagram has one common map, since any two objects have a receiver. Exactness of the filtered-colimit functor, applied to the monomorphisms \(X\to Y_i\), makes (3.1) monic. The transition arrows themselves are also monic: they restrict to the identity on the essential copy of \(X\), so Theorem 1.1 applies.

Let \(W\subseteq L\) have \(W\cap X=0\). Put \(W_i=Y_i\times_LW\). Its intersection with the given \(X\subseteq Y_i\) is zero by pullback associativity, so essentiality gives \(W_i=0\).

Filtered colimits commute with these pullbacks. Explicitly, \(W_i\) is the kernel of \[ Y_i\oplus W\longrightarrow L, \qquad(y,w)\longmapsto q_i(y)-j(w). \tag{3.2} \] This notation specifies the map by its two summand restrictions; it does not assume underlying elements in \(\mathcal A\). Filtered colimits preserve kernels by exactness, finite biproducts by the coproduct universal property, and the two constant diagrams by connectedness. Thus \[ \operatorname{colim}_iW_i \simeq\ker(L\oplus W\xrightarrow{(1,-j)}L) \simeq W. \tag{3.3} \] The final isomorphism sends \(w\) to the pair \((j(w),w)\) in the biproduct universal notation; projection to \(W\) is its inverse. Since every \(W_i=0\), we get \(W=0\). This proves essentiality for the entire filtered diagram, including diagrams with parallel arrows. \(\square\)

In particular the category of essential extensions of \(X\), with arrows commuting with \(X\), has every small filtered colimit. The underlying colimit just constructed is essential, and its cocone factors are precisely the factors in the undercategory.

4. A module test and another construction of injectives

Choose a generator \(G\) of \(\mathcal A\), set \(R=\operatorname{End}(G)^{\mathrm{op}}\), and write \[ \Gamma(Y)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal A}(G,Y), \qquad a^{\mathrm{op}}\cdot h=h\circ a. \tag{4.1} \] The ring multiplication and module laws are those in the finite Hom comparison. Here \(G\) need not be projective. The functor is faithful and left exact.

Theorem 4.1. If \(X\hookrightarrow Y\) is essential in \(\mathcal A\), then \(\Gamma(X)\hookrightarrow\Gamma(Y)\) is essential as a monomorphism of left \(R\)-modules.

Proof. Let \(U\subseteq\Gamma(Y)\) be a nonzero submodule and choose a nonzero \(h: G\to Y\) in it. Its image \(H\) is nonzero, so \(H\cap X\ne0\). Put \(P=G\times_YX\), a subobject of \(G\). The restriction \(P\to Y\) has image \(H\cap X\) and is therefore nonzero: pullback of the epimorphism \(G\to H\) onto that intersection is epic.

Faithfulness of Hom from \(G\) supplies \(b: G\to P\) for which the composite to \(Y\) is nonzero. If \(p: P\hookrightarrow G\), this composite is \(hpb\). It lies in \(\Gamma(X)\) by the pullback square and lies in \(U\) because \(pb\) is an endomorphism of \(G\) and \(U\) is closed under the action (4.1). Thus \(U\cap\Gamma(X)\ne0\). \(\square\)

We will need enough injectives for ordinary modules without deriving them from the general Grothendieck theorem. Here is the full independent interface.

Lemma 4.2. Over every small unital ring \(R\), every left module embeds into an injective left module.

Proof. Put \(D=\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\). It is divisible: \(q/n+\mathbb Z\) is an \(n\)-th root of \(q+\mathbb Z\). The complete generator injectivity criterion, applied to abelian groups with generator \(\mathbb Z\), makes it injective. Indeed the subgroups of \(\mathbb Z\) are \(n\mathbb Z\), and a map from one extends precisely when its value at \(n\) has an \(n\)-th root; the zero subgroup imposes no constraint. This use of the criterion involves its Zorn proof, not the later Grothendieck existence theorem. The module and filtered-colimit prerequisites of that criterion are the explicit ordinary module constructions cited above.

Characters into \(D\) separate nonzero group elements. On the cyclic subgroup generated by a nonzero \(m\), send a finite-order generator of order \(n>1\) to \(1/n+\mathbb Z\); for infinite order send it to \(1/2+\mathbb Z\). These are nonzero homomorphisms on \(m\). Injectivity of \(D\) extends each to the whole group.

Set \(J=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(R,D)\), with left action \((a\phi)(r)=\phi(ra)\). The mutually inverse maps \[ \begin{gathered} \operatorname{Hom}_R(M,J)\simeq\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(M,D),\\ f\longmapsto(m\mapsto f(m)(1)),\qquad \chi\longmapsto\bigl(m\mapsto(r\mapsto\chi(rm))\bigr) \end{gathered} \tag{4.2} \] are additive and natural. The second map is \(R\)-linear because its value on \(am\) at \(r\) is \(\chi(ram)\), which equals the \(a\)-action on its value at \(m\). Evaluation at \(1\) inverts it; the reverse equality uses \(f(rm)=r f(m)\).

For a submodule \(M\subseteq N\), every group character \(M\to D\) extends to \(N\). Under (4.2), this proves injectivity of \(J\). Products of injectives are injective by componentwise extension and small choice. The evaluation map \[ M\longrightarrow J^{\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(M,D)} \tag{4.3} \] using the second map of (4.2) in each coordinate is monic: a nonzero \(m\) is detected by a character and evaluation at \(1\). Its target is injective. All indices and modules are small under the fixed universe conventions. \(\square\)

Theorem 4.3. Every Grothendieck object \(X\) has an essential embedding into an injective object. This construction uses module injectives, the essential-extension tests above and categorical maximality.

Proof. Embed \(\Gamma(X)\) into an injective \(R\)-module \(J\) by Lemma 4.2. For any essential extension \(X\hookrightarrow E\), Theorem 4.1 makes \(\Gamma(X)\hookrightarrow\Gamma(E)\) essential. Injectivity of \(J\) extends the chosen embedding to a module map \(\Gamma(E)\to J\). By Theorem 1.1 in the module category it is monic, because its restriction to the essential submodule is monic. Consequently the small set \(\operatorname{Hom}(G,E)\) embeds in the underlying set of \(J\).

Let \[ P=\bigoplus_{j\in J}G. \tag{4.4} \] For each such \(E\), choose the injection of its Hom set into \(J\). On the summand indexed by an element in its image, use the corresponding map \(G\to E\); on every remaining summand use zero. The resulting \(P\to E\) is epic, since maps agreeing after it agree after every \(G\to E\), and \(G\) generates.

Ordinary quotient classes of \(P\) form a small set by the small-subobject theorem and the kernel description of quotients. Choose a small representative family of quotient targets. Decorate each by the small set of maps \(X\to E\) that are essential monomorphisms. Every essential extension is isomorphic, over \(X\), to one of these decorated representatives. Thus their category \(\mathcal E_X\) is essentially small. Its Homs are subsets of the small ambient Hom sets. It is nonempty because it contains \(1_X\), and it has all small filtered colimits by Theorem 3.1.

Apply the complete categorical maximality theorem to \(\mathcal E_X\). It gives an essential extension \(e: X\hookrightarrow E\) such that every arrow from it in \(\mathcal E_X\) has a left inverse there. For any essential monomorphism \(j: E\to V\), composition makes \(je\) essential, so \(j\) is such an arrow. Choose \(r: V\to E\) with \(rj=1_E\). The kernel of \(r\) is disjoint from \(j(E)\), hence from the essential copy \(je(X)\), so it is zero. The split-kernel decomposition makes \(j\) an isomorphism. Proposition 2.2 now makes \(E\) injective. This proves the asserted essential embedding, and in particular enough injectives. \(\square\)

The size bound uses only the faithful generator, its left exact Hom functor and preservation of essentiality. It does not require projectivity of \(G\), preservation of filtered colimits by \(\Gamma\), or full faithfulness of \(\Gamma\).

5. Comparing envelopes and computing one explicitly

An injective envelope is an essential monomorphism into an injective object.

Theorem 5.1. Every Grothendieck object has an injective envelope. If \(i: X\hookrightarrow E\) and \(i': X\hookrightarrow E'\) are two envelopes, there is an isomorphism \(v: E\to E'\) with \(vi=i'\). Every arrow satisfying that equation is invertible; such an arrow need not be unique.

Proof. Existence is Theorem 4.3, or the complete direct-summand construction in the cited envelope lesson. Injectivity of \(E'\) extends \(i'\) across \(i\) to \(v\). By Theorem 1.1 it is monic. Injectivity of \(E\) extends its identity across \(v\), giving \(r: E'\to E\) with \(rv=1_E\). The kernel of \(r\) is disjoint from \(v(E)\) and hence from \(i'(X)\). Essentiality of \(i'\) makes that kernel zero, so the splitting makes \(v\) invertible. The argument applies to every arrow over \(X\), proving the stronger comparison assertion. \(\square\)

Example 5.2 (two comparisons over the same simple module). Take \(k=\mathbb F_2\) and \(B=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\). Its only ideals are \(0,k\varepsilon,B\): an element with nonzero constant coefficient is a unit, and every nonzero element with zero constant coefficient spans \(k\varepsilon\). Thus \[ k\varepsilon\hookrightarrow B \tag{5.1} \] is essential as a map of left \(B\)-modules.

The module \(B\) is injective by the generator injectivity criterion with generator \(B\). A map \(k\varepsilon\to B\) sends \(\varepsilon\) to an element annihilated by \(\varepsilon\), hence to \(c\varepsilon\) for \(c\in k\). It extends by sending \(1\) to \(c\). Maps from the other two ideals extend respectively by zero and by themselves. Thus the criterion covers every subobject of the generator.

Multiplication by \(1+\varepsilon\) is an automorphism, since \((1+\varepsilon)^2=1\) in characteristic two. It fixes \(\varepsilon\) but sends \(1\) to \(1+\varepsilon\). It and the identity therefore give two different envelope comparisons fixing (5.1). The ambiguity is in the comparison arrow, not in the isomorphism class over the original object.

6. Recovering filteredness from Set comparisons

An index is \(\pi\)-filtered when every diagram with fewer than \(\pi\) total arrows admits a cocone. This includes the empty diagram, so requires nonemptiness.

Theorem 6.1. For a small category \(I\) and any infinite \(\pi\), the following are equivalent:

  1. \(I\) is \(\pi\)-filtered.
  2. For every bounded small \(J\) and \(\alpha: J^{\mathrm{op}}\times I\to\operatorname{Set}_{\mathcal U}\), the canonical map \[ \operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}\lim_{j\in J^{\mathrm{op}}}\alpha(j,i) \longrightarrow \lim_{j\in J^{\mathrm{op}}}\operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}\alpha(j,i) \tag{6.1} \] is bijective.

Surjectivity of all these comparisons alone suffices to deduce (1).

Proof. For (1) implies (2), the complete bounded Set comparison cited above synchronizes representatives and all arrow equations at one stage. It covers arbitrary infinite cardinals, including singular ones, and the empty shape.

For the converse, use the empty \(J\). The source is the colimit of the constant singleton over \(I\); surjectivity onto the singleton requires \(I\) to be nonempty. Now let \(d: J\to I\) be any bounded diagram and use \[ \alpha(j,i)=\operatorname{Hom}_I(d(j),i). \tag{6.2} \] Its two actions are precomposition by \(d\) and postcomposition in \(I\); associativity makes them commute. For each \(j\), its colimit over \(i\) is a singleton. Indeed each arrow \(a: d(j)\to i\) is identified with \(1_{d(j)}\) along the index arrow \(a\), so all elements have the same class, and that identity supplies an element.

Their limit over \(J^{\mathrm{op}}\) is consequently a singleton, including when \(J\) is empty. Surjectivity of (6.1) gives a stage \(i\) and a compatible tuple \[ a_j: d(j)\to i,\qquad a_{j'}d(s)=a_j \quad(s: j\to j'). \tag{6.3} \] It is precisely a cocone on \(d\). Thus every bounded diagram has a cocone. Neither regularity nor an injectivity argument for (6.1) was needed in this implication. \(\square\)

7. Colimits detected by comma categories

Let \(F: \mathcal C\to\mathcal D\) be a functor between locally small categories. For \(Y\in\mathcal D\), define \((F\downarrow Y)\) to have objects \((X,u: F(X)\to Y)\) and arrows \(a: X\to X'\) satisfying \(u'F(a)=u\).

Theorem 7.1. Suppose \(\mathcal C\) has every bounded-shape colimit, for a fixed infinite \(\pi\). Then \(F\) preserves all these colimits if and only if \((F\downarrow Y)\) is \(\pi\)-filtered for every \(Y\in\mathcal D\).

Proof. If \(F\) preserves them, a bounded diagram \((X_j,u_j)\) has a colimit in the comma category: take \(L=\operatorname{colim}_jX_j\); the compatible \(u_j\) give \(u: F(L)\to Y\) by preservation. The underlying colimit factorization, followed by its uniqueness in \(\mathcal D\), proves the comma universal property. The empty shape gives \((0,u)\), where \(F(0)\) is initial. Having these colimits supplies a cocone on every bounded diagram, so the comma category is \(\pi\)-filtered.

Conversely, fix a bounded diagram \(X_j\), with colimit \(L\) and legs \(c_j\). For any compatible family \(u_j: F(X_j)\to Y\), apply \(\pi\)-filteredness to its diagram in \((F\downarrow Y)\). A cocone gives \(a_j: X_j\to Z\), with \(h: F(Z)\to Y\) and \(hF(a_j)=u_j\). Let \(a: L\to Z\) be the colimit factor. Then \(hF(a): F(L)\to Y\) realizes the prescribed family. This proves existence of factors.

For uniqueness, let \(f,g: F(L)\to Y\) agree after every \(F(c_j)\). Adjoin to \(J\) two new objects, each receiving one arrow from every old object, with those arrows compatible with every old arrow. Besides the old arrows, this shape has \(2|\operatorname{Ob}J|+2\) arrows. Its total cardinal remains below the infinite \(\pi\); no regularity is needed. In the comma category put \((L,f)\) and \((L,g)\) at the two new objects, and use \(c_j\) on their incoming arrows. The assumed agreements make this a diagram.

A receiving cocone at \((Z,h)\) gives \(a,b: L\to Z\) with \[ ac_j=bc_j,\qquad hF(a)=f,\qquad hF(b)=g. \tag{7.1} \] The colimit property of \(L\) implies \(a=b\), so \(f=g\). For empty \(J\), that same uniqueness is the initial-object property of \(L\); the adjoined shape has just two identities. Thus \((F(L),F(c_j))\) is a colimit cocone for every bounded shape, proving preservation and existence of the required image colimits in \(\mathcal D\). \(\square\)

8. Retractions, their uniqueness and a two-object category

The first two lemmas apply to any category, with no abelian or colimit hypothesis.

Call an object split maximal when every arrow out of it has a left inverse.

Lemma 8.1. Any arrow between two split maximal objects is invertible.

Proof. For \(u: X\to Y\), choose \(v: Y\to X\) with \(vu=1_X\), then choose \(w: X\to Y\) with \(wv=1_Y\). Thus \[ w=w(vu)=(wv)u=u,\qquad uv=wv=1_Y. \tag{8.1} \] So \(v\) is a two-sided inverse. The statement does not assert existence of an arrow between two such objects. \(\square\)

Lemma 8.2. If \((X,1_X)\) is terminal in the undercategory \((X\downarrow\mathcal C)\), then \(X\) is split maximal.

Proof. The unique arrow from \((X,u: X\to Y)\) to \((X,1_X)\) is exactly a map \(r: Y\to X\) with \(ru=1_X\). It supplies a left inverse to every outgoing arrow. Terminality also gives uniqueness of each left inverse, which split maximality by itself does not require. \(\square\)

We now keep all parameter cases explicit. For a small set \(A\), consider the full subcategory \(\mathcal T_A\) of \((A\downarrow\operatorname{Set})\) on \[ x_0=(A,1_A),\qquad x_1=(A\sqcup\{\ast\},\text{the inclusion of }A). \tag{8.2} \] This is an undercategory, not a category of \(A\)-indexed families. Its arrows fix the specified copy of \(A\). Consequently \[ \begin{gathered} \operatorname{Hom}(x_0,x_0)=\{1\},\quad \operatorname{Hom}(x_0,x_1)=\{j\},\\ \operatorname{Hom}(x_1,x_0)=\{r_a: a\in A\},\quad \operatorname{End}(x_1)=\{1\}\sqcup\{e_a: a\in A\}. \end{gathered} \tag{8.3} \] Here \(r_a\) sends \(\ast\) to \(a\), \(j\) includes \(A\), and \(e_a=jr_a\). All act identically on \(A\). Their compositions satisfy \[ r_aj=1,\quad e_ae_b=e_b,\quad r_ae_b=r_b,\quad e_aj=j. \tag{8.4} \] These formulas and identities determine every composable pair; associativity follows from composition of actual functions. They give a fully faithful realization of the abstract two-object category with this arrow table in \((A\downarrow\operatorname{Set})\).

Theorem 8.3. For every small \(A\), the constant embedding \[ \mathcal T_A\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ind}(\mathcal T_A) \tag{8.5} \] is an equivalence, and \(\mathcal T_A\) has all small filtered colimits. The object \(x_0\) is split maximal precisely when \(A\ne\varnothing\). Its outgoing arrow \(j\) has exactly \(|A|\) left inverses, so nonuniqueness occurs precisely when \(|A|\ge2\).

Proof. The constant embedding is fully faithful by the complete Yoneda proof. We show directly that every formal filtered presentation represents one of these two objects.

Let \(B=\operatorname{colim}_{i\in I}h_{X_i}\), with \(I\) small and filtered, \(X_i\in\{x_0,x_1\}\), and \(h_X=\operatorname{Hom}(-,X)\). At \(x_0\), each value is a singleton, so \(B(x_0)\) is a singleton.

At \(x_1\), the values are copies of \(A\) when \(X_i=x_0\), and copies of \(A\sqcup\{\ast\}\) when \(X_i=x_1\). A postcomposition map fixes every label \(a\in A\). Its extra label \(\ast\), when present, either stays extra along an identity of \(x_1\), or moves to a label \(a\) along \(r_a\) or \(e_a\); the arrow \(j\) merely includes the \(A\)-labels. Thus all stages contain a common copy of \(A\). Distinct labels in it remain distinct, by the same-stage equality test for filtered Set colimits.

There is at most one colimit class outside this copy of \(A\). Receive any two representatives at a common stage. If either has become a label in \(A\), its class was already in \(A\); otherwise both are the one extra label at that stage and are equal. Hence \(B(x_1)\) is either \(A\) or \(A\sqcup\{\text{one extra class}\}\).

These value descriptions identify the entire presheaf. Restriction along \(j\) goes to the singleton. Restriction along \(r_a\) sends that singleton to the label \(a\). Restriction along \(e_a\) is the constant function with value \(a\): composing any represented arrow from \(x_1\) with \(e_a\) gives the arrow labelled \(a\), by (8.4). Consequently, in the first case the unique element at \(x_0\) gives a Yoneda map \(h_{x_0}\to B\) bijective at both objects. In the second case the extra class at \(x_1\) gives \(h_{x_1}\to B\), again bijective at both objects. These are presheaf isomorphisms. This argument works also for \(A=\varnothing\): then a stage at \(x_1\) has its extra element, and no arrow can send that element to a nonexistent label.

Thus every ind-object is represented, proving essential surjectivity and the equivalence. It also proves the asserted actual colimits. The pointwise presheaf colimit of any filtered represented diagram is one of the displayed representables; its universal maps into any \(h_Z\), together with Yoneda, give the unique compatible cocone factor in \(\mathcal T_A\). The original cocone legs are thereby retained.

The only arrows out of \(x_0\) are its identity and \(j\). Each \(r_a\) satisfies \(r_aj=1\), and (8.3) lists every possible left inverse. If \(A=\varnothing\), \(j\) has none, so \(x_0\) is not split maximal. If \(A\) is a singleton, it has one. If \(A\) has at least two elements, it has distinct left inverses. These are all cases. \(\square\)

Accordingly, the assertion that this two-object category always has a split maximal \(x_0\) needs \(A\ne\varnothing\), while the assertion of nonunique left inverses needs the stronger \(|A|\ge2\). The category, its fully faithful realization and its filtered-colimit conclusions remain valid for the empty and singleton parameters.

Example 8.4 (a filtered chain whose colimit is the retract). Take \(A=\{0,1\}\), let \(a_n\) alternate between \(0\) and \(1\), and consider the chain with every term \(x_1\) and successor arrow \(e_{a_n}\). Its colimit is \(x_0\), with leg \(r_{a_n}\) at stage \(n\). Indeed \[ r_{a_{n+1}}e_{a_n}=r_{a_n}. \tag{8.6} \] For a cocone into \(x_0\), write its legs \(r_{b_n}\); the compatibility equation forces \(b_n=a_n\) for every \(n\). For a cocone into \(x_1\), each leg is either an identity or an \(e_b\), and composition with \(e_{a_n}\) is always \(e_{a_n}\); compatibility forces that leg at stage \(n\) to be \(e_{a_n}\). There is therefore one cocone to either target. The maps \(1_{x_0}\) and \(j: x_0\to x_1\) give these two unique factors respectively. This proves the colimit universal property in the entire two-object category.

9. Four graded exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 1 (standard: an essential rational extension). Prove that \(\mathbb Z\hookrightarrow\mathbb Q\) is an injective envelope in abelian groups, whereas \(z\mapsto(z,0)\) into \(\mathbb Q\oplus\mathbb Q\) is not essential. Is the comparison automorphism of \(\mathbb Q\) fixing every integer unique?

Solution. A nonzero subgroup of \(\mathbb Q\) contains some nonzero \(a/b\), hence also the nonzero integer \(a\) after multiplication by \(b\). Thus it meets \(\mathbb Z\), proving essentiality. The group \(\mathbb Q\) is divisible, so the generator criterion used in Lemma 4.2 makes it injective; hence this is an envelope. In the two-factor target, the nonzero second summand has zero intersection with the specified integers, so the embedding is not essential. A group endomorphism of \(\mathbb Q\) fixing \(1\) fixes every rational \(a/b\): multiplying its image by \(b\) gives \(a\), and multiplication by \(b\) on \(\mathbb Q\) is injective. Therefore the only comparison fixing the integers is the identity. Nonuniqueness in Theorem 5.1 is possible, not compulsory; Example 5.2 exhibits it.

Exercise 2 (advanced: keep the empty colimit and every target). For the constant functor \(F: \operatorname{Set}\to\operatorname{Set}\) with value a singleton, determine when \((F\downarrow Y)\) is filtered. Explain why testing just one target or just nonempty connected colimits does not give Theorem 7.1.

Solution. An object of the comma category is a set \(X\) with a label \(y\in Y\). Its arrows are all functions between sets carrying the same label, and none between different labels. If \(Y=\varnothing\), the comma category is empty and not filtered. If \(Y\) has at least two elements, objects with different labels have no common receiver, so it is not filtered. If \(Y\) is a singleton, the comma category is equivalent to all sets, which has all small colimits and consequently is \(\pi\)-filtered for every infinite \(\pi\). Thus filteredness holds exactly for a singleton target.

The constant functor does preserve every nonempty connected colimit: the image diagram is constant at a singleton, whose colimit over a nonempty connected category is a singleton. It fails the empty colimit, because \(F(\varnothing)\) is not initial, and fails the two-object discrete coproduct comparison \(1\sqcup1\to1\). The theorem requires all bounded shapes and every \(Y\), precisely excluding both weakened tests.

Exercise 3 (expert: essentiality is detected without projectivity). Prove the converse of Theorem 4.1. Then show explicitly that Hom from a generator need not preserve epimorphisms, using \(G=\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/2\) in abelian groups.

Solution. Suppose \(\Gamma(X)\subseteq\Gamma(Y)\) is essential and \(W\subseteq Y\) is nonzero. Since \(G\) generates, \(\Gamma(W)\ne0\): otherwise faithfulness applied to \(1_W,0_W\) would give \(W=0\). Left exactness identifies \[ \Gamma(W\cap X)=\Gamma(W)\cap\Gamma(X) \tag{9.1} \] inside \(\Gamma(Y)\), by the pullback property of Hom. Essentiality of the right-hand submodule makes this intersection nonzero, so \(W\cap X\ne0\). Hence \(X\subseteq Y\) is essential.

The indicated \(G\) generates because it has \(\mathbb Z\) as a direct summand. For the epimorphism \(q: \mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2\), take the map \(G\to\mathbb Z/2\) that is zero on \(\mathbb Z\) and identity on the torsion summand. It cannot lift to \(\mathbb Z\), since every homomorphism \(\mathbb Z/2\to\mathbb Z\) is zero. Thus \(\Gamma(q)\) is not surjective. Essentiality preservation and detection needed only left exactness and the generator test; exactness of this Hom functor would be an additional projectivity assertion.

Exercise 4 (expert: nonunique retractions do not give terminality). In \(\mathcal T_{\{0,1\}}\), determine the arrows from \((x_1,j)\) to \((x_0,1_{x_0})\) in the undercategory \((x_0\downarrow\mathcal T_{\{0,1\}})\). Decide whether the latter object is terminal, and compare with \(A=\varnothing\) and a singleton.

Solution. An arrow in that direction is a map \(r: x_1\to x_0\) with \(rj=1\). The arrow table gives exactly \(r_0,r_1\), distinct because their values on \(\ast\) differ. Thus \((x_0,1)\) is not terminal, even though \(x_0\) is split maximal. With empty \(A\), there is no arrow from the object \(j\) to \((x_0,1)\), so terminality and split maximality both fail. With singleton \(A\), that arrow is unique, and the only other object of the undercategory is the identity object, whose arrow to itself is unique. Hence \((x_0,1)\) is terminal in this case. These computations separate existence from uniqueness of left inverses for all parameter sizes.
