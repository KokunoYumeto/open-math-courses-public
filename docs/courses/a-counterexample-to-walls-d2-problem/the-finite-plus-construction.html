The finite plus construction

Written by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Public domain (CC0).

Let \(B\) be a finite complex of dimension two and \(K\) a normal subgroup of its fundamental group \(P\). Attaching cells of dimension two kills \(K\), but it changes the homology. If \(K\) is perfect, the change can be undone by cells of dimension three. The result is a complex \(X\) of dimension three with the fundamental group \(P/K\) and with the same homology as \(B\), for all coefficients that come from \(P/K\). This is Quillen's plus construction, in a finite form. This lesson proves that \(X\) satisfies the condition D(2).

  • Perfect subgroups and the cover that belongs to \(K\) (Lemma 1.1).
  • Cycles that pass once through the new cells (Lemma 2.1).
  • The construction and its three properties (Proposition 3.1).

The lesson follows [OpenAI-W, Section 3]. It uses chains of covers, coefficients in a module and the standard theorems TOP1 to TOP4 from Wall's D(2) problem and twisted homology.

1. The setting

A group \(K\) is perfect if it is generated by its commutators \(ghg^{-1}h^{-1}\), that is, if its abelianization \(K_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is trivial. The normal closure of elements \(t_1,\dots,t_r\) of a group \(P\) is the smallest normal subgroup of \(P\) that contains them; it consists of the products of conjugates of the \(t_j\) and their inverses.

Throughout the lesson:

  • \(B\) is a finite connected CW complex of dimension at most two, and \(P=\pi_1(B)\);
  • \(t_1,\dots,t_r\in P\), \(K\) is their normal closure, and \(K\) is perfect;
  • \(G=P/K\) and \(R=\mathbb Z[G]\).

Let \(B'\) be the complex \(B\) with \(r\) further cells \(d_1,\dots,d_r\) of dimension two, attached along loops that represent \(t_1,\dots,t_r\). By TOP1, \(\pi_1(B')=G\), and \(\pi_1(B)\to\pi_1(B')\) is the quotient map \(P\to G\). Let \(p:\widetilde{B'}\to B'\) be the universal cover and \[ \overline B=p^{-1}(B)\subseteq\widetilde{B'} . \]

Lemma 1.1. \(\overline B\) is a connected subcomplex, the restriction \(\overline B\to B\) is the covering \(B_K\) of \(B\) that belongs to \(K\), and \(H_1(\overline B;\mathbb Z)=0\).

Proof. By TOP2, \(\overline B\) is a subcomplex, and the group \(G\) permutes the lifts of each cell of \(B\) freely and transitively. All cells of \(\widetilde{B'}\) outside \(\overline B\) have the dimension two. So \(\overline B\) contains all vertices and edges of the connected complex \(\widetilde{B'}\), and it is connected. A loop of \(B\) lifts to a closed path in \(\widetilde{B'}\) exactly if its class in \(\pi_1(B')=G\) is trivial, that is, if its class in \(P\) lies in \(K\). So \(\overline B\to B\) is the connected covering with the group \(K\). By TOP3, \(H_1(\overline B;\mathbb Z)=K_{\mathrm{ab}}=0\). \(\blacksquare\)

By Section 3 of the first lesson, the cellular chain groups of \(\overline B\) and \(\widetilde{B'}\) are free left \(R\)-modules. Choose lifts \(\tilde d_1,\dots,\tilde d_r\) of the new cells. Then \[ C_2(\widetilde{B'})=C_2(\overline B)\oplus R^r, \] where \(R^r\) has the basis \(\tilde d_1,\dots,\tilde d_r\); in the other degrees \(C_j(\widetilde{B'})=C_j(\overline B)\). Let \[ q:C_2(\widetilde{B'})\to R^r \] be the projection onto the second summand. It is \(R\)-linear.

2. Cycles through the new cells

Lemma 2.1. For every \(j\) there is a chain \(c_j\in C_2(\overline B)\) such that \[ \alpha_j=\tilde d_j-c_j \] is a cycle of \(C_2(\widetilde{B'})\). It satisfies \(q(\alpha_j)=\tilde d_j\).

Proof. The chain \(\partial\tilde d_j\) lies in \(C_1(\overline B)\), and it is a cycle, because \(\partial\partial=0\). Since \(H_1(\overline B;\mathbb Z)=0\) and the homology of \(\overline B\) is computed by its cellular chains (TOP3), there is \(c_j\in C_2(\overline B)\) with \(\partial c_j=\partial\tilde d_j\). \(\blacksquare\)

This is the place where the perfectness of \(K\) is used. The cycle \(\alpha_j\) consists of one new cell and of a chain in \(\overline B\) that closes it up.

3. The construction

Proposition 3.1 ([OpenAI-W]; Quillen, Mannan). Assume TOP1 to TOP4. In the setting of Section 1 there is a finite complex \(X\) that contains \(B\) and has, besides the cells of \(B\), exactly \(r\) cells of dimension two and \(r\) cells of dimension three, such that:

  1. the inclusion \(B\to X\) induces the quotient map \(P\to G\) on the fundamental groups;
  2. \(H_i(\widetilde X;\mathbb Z)=0\) for all \(i>2\), and \(H^3(X;M)=0\) for every left \(R\)-module \(M\): the complex \(X\) satisfies the condition D(2);
  3. for every right \(R\)-module \(N\) and every \(i\), the inclusion induces an isomorphism \[ H_i(B;N)\cong H_i(X;N), \] where on the left \(P\) acts on \(N\) through \(P\to G\).

Proof. The cells of dimension three. The complex \(\widetilde{B'}\) is simply connected and has the dimension two, so \(H_2(\widetilde{B'};\mathbb Z)\) is the group of the cycles of \(C_2(\widetilde{B'})\). By TOP4 there are maps \(\varphi_j:S^2\to\widetilde{B'}\) such that the image of the fundamental class of \(S^2\) is the cycle \(\alpha_j\) of Lemma 2.1. Attach to \(B'\) one cell \(e_j\) of dimension three along \(p\varphi_j:S^2\to B'\), for \(j=1,\dots,r\). This is \(X\).

Property 1. By TOP1 the cells of dimension three do not change the fundamental group, and \(\pi_1(B)\to\pi_1(B')\) is \(P\to G\).

The universal cover of \(X\). Since \(\pi_1(B')\to\pi_1(X)\) is an isomorphism, the preimage of \(B'\) in \(\widetilde X\) is the universal cover \(\widetilde{B'}\). By TOP2 the cells of dimension three of \(\widetilde X\) are the lifts of the \(e_j\); let \(\tilde e_j\) be the lift that is attached along the lift \(\varphi_j\) of \(p\varphi_j\). By TOP3, \[ \partial\tilde e_j=\alpha_j . \] So \(C_3(\widetilde X)=R^r\) with the basis \(\tilde e_1,\dots,\tilde e_r\), \(C_2(\widetilde X)=C_2(\overline B)\oplus R^r\), and \(C_j(\widetilde X)=C_j(\overline B)\) for \(j\le1\). With the projection \(q\) and the identification of the two modules \(R^r\) by \(\tilde e_j\mapsto\tilde d_j\), Lemma 2.1 says \[ q\,\partial_3=\mathrm{id}. \] We refer to this equation as (3.1).

Property 2. By (3.1), \(\partial_3\) is injective, so \(H_3(\widetilde X;\mathbb Z)=0\); there are no cells of a dimension above three. Let \(M\) be a left \(R\)-module and \(\psi:C_3(\widetilde X)\to M\) an \(R\)-linear map. Then \(\psi q:C_2(\widetilde X)\to M\) is \(R\)-linear, and \((\psi q)\partial_3=\psi\) by (3.1). So every element of \(C^3(X;M)\) is a coboundary, and \(H^3(X;M)=0\).

Property 3. Let \(N\) be a right \(R\)-module. Write \(C=C_*(\widetilde X)\) and \(C'=C_*(\overline B)\), a subcomplex. By the definition, \(C_*(B;N)=N\otimes_{\mathbb Z[P]}C_*(\widetilde B)\). By the formula for covers of the first lesson, \(C_*(\overline B)=\mathbb Z[G]\otimes_{\mathbb Z[P]}C_*(\widetilde B)\), because \(\overline B\) is the cover \(B_K\) of \(B\) (Lemma 1.1). So \[ \begin{gathered} C_*(B;N)=N\otimes_RC',\\ C_*(X;N)=N\otimes_RC , \end{gathered} \] and we must compare the homology of these two complexes. An element of \(N\otimes_RC_2\) is a pair \((a,\nu)\) with \(a\in N\otimes_RC'_2\) and \(\nu\in N^r\), and an element of \(N\otimes_RC_3\) is a \(\nu\in N^r\). Write \(c(\nu)\) for the image of \(\nu\) under the map \(N^r\to N\otimes_RC'_2\) that the chains \(c_j\) define. Then \[ \begin{gathered} \partial_3\nu=(-c(\nu),\ \nu),\\ \partial_2(a,\nu)=\partial a+\partial c(\nu), \end{gathered} \] the second because \(\partial\tilde d_j=\partial c_j\).

  • Degree \(3\): \(\partial_3\nu=0\) forces \(\nu=0\). So \(H_3(N\otimes C)=0=H_3(N\otimes C')\).
  • Degree \(2\): let \((a,\nu)\) be a cycle. Then \((a,\nu)-\partial_3\nu=(a+c(\nu),0)\) is a cycle of \(N\otimes C'\). So every class of \(H_2(N\otimes C)\) comes from a cycle of \(N\otimes C'\). If a cycle \((a,0)\) is a boundary \(\partial_3\nu\), then \(\nu=0\) and \(a=0\). Since \(N\otimes C'\) has no chains of degree three, \(H_2(N\otimes C')\to H_2(N\otimes C)\) is an isomorphism.
  • Degree \(1\): the two complexes have the same chains and the same cycles of degree one. The boundaries are the same too, because \(\partial_2(a,\nu)=\partial(a+c(\nu))\).
  • Degree \(0\): the two complexes agree in the degrees \(0\) and \(1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remarks. The equation (3.1) says more than \(H_3(\widetilde X)=0\): the boundary \(\partial_3\) is an isomorphism onto a direct summand of \(C_2(\widetilde X)\). This is why the cohomology vanishes for every module \(M\), and why no condition on \(M\) or \(N\) is needed.

The complex \(X\) depends on choices: the chains \(c_j\) and the maps \(\varphi_j\). The proposition holds for every choice.

For a character \(\rho\) of \(G\), property 3 with \(N=\mathbb C_\rho\) gives \[ H_i(B;\rho\pi)\cong H_i(X;\rho),\qquad\pi:P\to G . \] So the twisted homology of \(X\) is computed on the complex \(B\) of dimension two, by Fox derivatives. But \(X\) has a different fundamental group than \(B\): elements of \(P\) of infinite order may have finite order in \(G\). The obstruction of the lesson on the tower applies to complexes of dimension two with the fundamental group \(G\), and \(B\) is not one of them.

4. Exercises

4.1. Let \(B\) be a single vertex with one loop, \(P=\mathbb Z\) with the generator \(x\), and \(t_1=x\). Is \(K\) perfect? What goes wrong in Lemma 2.1?

4.2. Show that a normal subgroup \(K\) of \(P\) acts trivially on \(K_{\mathrm{ab}}\) by conjugation, and that \(K_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is a left module over \(\mathbb Z[P/K]\), generated by the classes of \(t_1,\dots,t_r\) if \(K\) is their normal closure.

4.3. Let \(X\) be as in the proposition. Compute \(\chi(X)\) from \(\chi(B)\), where the Euler characteristic of a complex of dimension three is \(c_0-c_1+c_2-c_3\).

4.4. Where does the proof of property 3 use that the chain groups are tensored over \(R\), and where would it fail for a module over \(\mathbb Z[P]\) on which \(K\) acts nontrivially?

4.5. Derive from (3.1) that \(C_2(\widetilde X)=\ker q\oplus\operatorname{im}\partial_3\) and that \(\ker q=C_2(\overline B)\).

5. Solutions

4.1. \(K=P=\mathbb Z\) is not perfect. \(B'\) is a disc, \(\overline B=B\) is the circle with \(H_1=\mathbb Z\), and \(\partial\tilde d_1\) is a generator of \(H_1(\overline B)\): it is not the boundary of a chain of \(\overline B\). Indeed \(B'\) is contractible and has no cycle of dimension two.

4.2. For \(k,k'\in K\) the class of \(kk'k^{-1}\) in \(K_{\mathrm{ab}}\) is the class of \(k'\) times the class of the commutator \(kk'k^{-1}k'^{-1}\), which is trivial. So the action of \(P\) on \(K_{\mathrm{ab}}\) by conjugation factors through \(P/K\). Every element of \(K\) is a product of conjugates \(gt_j^{\pm1}g^{-1}\), whose classes are \(\pm g\cdot[t_j]\).

4.3. \(X\) has \(r\) more cells of dimension two and \(r\) cells of dimension three: \(\chi(X)=\chi(B)+r-r=\chi(B)\).

4.4. The identification \(C_*(B;N)=N\otimes_RC_*(\overline B)\) needs that \(K\) acts trivially on \(N\): it uses \[ \begin{aligned} &N\otimes_{\mathbb Z[P]}C_*(\widetilde B)\\ &\quad=N\otimes_R\bigl(R\otimes_{\mathbb Z[P]}C_*(\widetilde B)\bigr). \end{aligned} \] The complex \(X\) has the fundamental group \(G\), so modules over \(\mathbb Z[P]\) that do not come from \(G\) are not coefficients on \(X\) at all.

4.5. For \(c\in C_2(\widetilde X)\): \(c=(c-\partial_3q(c))+\partial_3q(c)\), and \(q(c-\partial_3q(c))=q(c)-q(c)=0\). If \(c=\partial_3\nu\) and \(q(c)=0\), then \(\nu=q\partial_3\nu=0\). The kernel of the projection \(q\) is the first summand \(C_2(\overline B)\).
