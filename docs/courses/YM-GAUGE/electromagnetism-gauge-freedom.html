Electromagnetism and gauge freedom

Lesson YM-F02 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

An electromagnetic field assigns an electric field and a magnetic field to each spacetime point. We can also describe these fields using potentials. The potentials contain freedom: different potential functions can produce exactly the same electric and magnetic fields.

This lesson constructs that freedom, proves what it preserves, and tests its limits on explicit domains. Maxwell’s equations provide the physical model. The calculus identities and every consequence used here are derived from that model and the preparation in Lesson 1.

1. Fields, sources and units

Fix a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system x=(x1,x2,x3)x=(x^1,x^2,x^3), an open time interval II, and an open spatial set Ω⊆ℝ3\Omega\subseteq\mathbb R^3. Time remains tt; it is not replaced by a length coordinate. Unless a smaller regularity class is explicitly stated, all functions in this lesson are smooth on I×ΩI\times\Omega. Smooth means that partial derivatives of every finite order exist and are continuous.

The unknown fields and prescribed sources are

E,B:I×Ω→ℝ3,ρ:I×Ω→ℝ,j:I×Ω→ℝ3. E,B:I\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R^3, \qquad \rho:I\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R, \qquad j:I\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R^3. \tag{F2.1}
(F2.1)

Here ρ\rho is electric charge per volume, and jj is electric current per area, with direction. The symbol ρ\rho has this meaning in the present lesson; it is not the quadratic transport density used in Lesson 1.

For vectors a,ba,b, our products are defined by

a⋅b=a1b1+a2b2+a3b3,a×b=(a2b3−a3b2,a3b1−a1b3,a1b2−a2b1),|a|2=(a1)2+(a2)2+(a3)2. \begin{aligned} a\cdot b&=a^1b^1+a^2b^2+a^3b^3,\\ a\times b&=(a^2b^3-a^3b^2,\ a^3b^1-a^1b^3,\ a^1b^2-a^2b^1),\\ |a|^2&=(a^1)^2+(a^2)^2+(a^3)^2. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.2}
(F2.2)

In SI units, a test particle with charge qq and velocity vv experiences electromagnetic force

F=q(E+v×B). F=q(E+v\times B). \tag{F2.3}
(F2.3)

This is a physical law specifying the role of the fields, not a theorem of vector arithmetic. A test particle means that its effect on the given field is neglected in this description. The formula is evaluated at the particle’s time and position.

We retain the vacuum constants ϵ0>0\epsilon_0>0 and μ0>0\mu_0>0, with

c2=1ϵ0μ0,c>0. c^2=\frac{1}{\epsilon_0\mu_0},\qquad c>0. \tag{F2.4}
(F2.4)

The following table uses metres (m), seconds (s), coulombs (C) and newtons (N). The coulomb measures electric charge, and the newton measures force. Each vector component has the unit displayed for its vector.

Quantity SI unit
EE N/C\mathrm{N}/\mathrm{C}

ρ\rho C/m3\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{m}^3
jj C/(m2s)\mathrm{C}/(\mathrm{m}^2\mathrm{\,s})

For instance, v×Bv\times B has unit (m/s)(Ns/(Cm))=N/C(\mathrm m/\mathrm s)(\mathrm{N\,s}/(\mathrm{C\,m})) =\mathrm N/\mathrm C, matching EE. Also, ϵ0μ0\epsilon_0\mu_0 has unit s2/m2\mathrm s^2/\mathrm m^2, as (F2.4) requires. The potentials and gauge function will be defined below; their units already specify which derivatives can be added.

2. The vector calculus we need

Write ∂i=∂/∂xi\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i. For a scalar ff and a vector VV, define

∇f=(∂1f,∂2f,∂3f),div⁡V=∂1V1+∂2V2+∂3V3,curl⁡V=(∂2V3−∂3V2,∂3V1−∂1V3,∂1V2−∂2V1),Δf=∂12f+∂22f+∂32f,(ΔV)i=ΔVi(i=1,2,3). \begin{aligned} \nabla f&=(\partial_1f,\partial_2f,\partial_3f),\\ \operatorname{div}V&=\partial_1V^1+\partial_2V^2+\partial_3V^3,\\ \operatorname{curl}V &=(\partial_2V^3-\partial_3V^2,\ \partial_3V^1-\partial_1V^3,\ \partial_1V^2-\partial_2V^1),\\ \Delta f&=\partial_1^2f+\partial_2^2f+\partial_3^2f,\qquad (\Delta V)^i=\Delta V^i\quad(i=1,2,3). \end{aligned} \tag{F2.5}
(F2.5)

The notation ∇⋅V\nabla\cdot V also means divergence, and ∇×V\nabla\times V means curl. These are differential operators; the displayed component formulas define them.

Lemma 2.1. Mixed derivatives commute

For a C2C^2 function, any two coordinate derivatives commute, including a time and a spatial derivative.

Proof. Let yy denote all coordinates, and let ea,ebe_a,e_b be two different coordinate vectors. For a sufficiently small rectangle in the domain, apply the fundamental theorem of calculus twice to the four-corner difference

D=f(y+hea+keb)−f(y+hea)−f(y+keb)+f(y). D=f(y+he_a+ke_b)-f(y+he_a)-f(y+ke_b)+f(y). \tag{F2.6}
(F2.6)

Differencing first along the aa-direction gives D=∫0h∫0k∂b∂af(y+sea+reb)drdsD=\int_0^h\int_0^k \partial_b\partial_af(y+se_a+re_b)\,dr\,ds. Differencing first along the bb-direction gives D=∫0k∫0h∂a∂bf(y+sea+reb)dsdrD=\int_0^k\int_0^h \partial_a\partial_bf(y+se_a+re_b)\,ds\,dr. Take h,k>0h,k>0, divide both expressions by hkhk, and let both lengths tend to zero. Continuity makes the limits the corresponding mixed derivatives at yy. They are equal because both expressions equal D/(hk)D/(hk). Equal-coordinate derivatives require no interchange. ▫\square

Proposition 2.2. Three differential identities

For C2C^2 fields,

curl⁡(∇f)=0,div⁡(curl⁡V)=0,curl⁡(curl⁡V)=∇(div⁡V)−ΔV. \operatorname{curl}(\nabla f)=0,\qquad \operatorname{div}(\operatorname{curl}V)=0,\qquad \operatorname{curl}(\operatorname{curl}V) =\nabla(\operatorname{div}V)-\Delta V. \tag{F2.7}
(F2.7)

Proof. The three components of the first expression are

(∂2∂3f−∂3∂2f,∂3∂1f−∂1∂3f,∂1∂2f−∂2∂1f). (\partial_2\partial_3f-\partial_3\partial_2f,\ \partial_3\partial_1f-\partial_1\partial_3f,\ \partial_1\partial_2f-\partial_2\partial_1f). \tag{F2.8}
(F2.8)

Each is zero by Lemma 2.1. Expanding the second expression gives all six terms

∂1∂2V3−∂1∂3V2+∂2∂3V1−∂2∂1V3+∂3∂1V2−∂3∂2V1=0. \partial_1\partial_2V^3-\partial_1\partial_3V^2 +\partial_2\partial_3V^1-\partial_2\partial_1V^3 +\partial_3\partial_1V^2-\partial_3\partial_2V^1=0. \tag{F2.9}
(F2.9)

For the third identity, the three components of its left side are

∂2∂1V2−∂22V1−∂32V1+∂3∂1V3,∂3∂2V3−∂32V2−∂12V2+∂1∂2V1,∂1∂3V1−∂12V3−∂22V3+∂2∂3V2. \begin{aligned} &\partial_2\partial_1V^2-\partial_2^2V^1 -\partial_3^2V^1+\partial_3\partial_1V^3,\\ &\partial_3\partial_2V^3-\partial_3^2V^2 -\partial_1^2V^2+\partial_1\partial_2V^1,\\ &\partial_1\partial_3V^1-\partial_1^2V^3 -\partial_2^2V^3+\partial_2\partial_3V^2. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.10}
(F2.10)

For component ii, expand

∂i(∂1V1+∂2V2+∂3V3)−(∂12Vi+∂22Vi+∂32Vi). \partial_i(\partial_1V^1+\partial_2V^2+\partial_3V^3) -(\partial_1^2V^i+\partial_2^2V^i+\partial_3^2V^i).

The ∂i2Vi\partial_i^2V^i terms cancel, and Lemma 2.1 identifies the remaining four terms with the corresponding line of (F2.10). This proves all three components. ▫\square

We will also use, for C1C^1 vectors E,BE,B,

div⁡(E×B)=B⋅curl⁡E−E⋅curl⁡B. \operatorname{div}(E\times B) =B\cdot\operatorname{curl}E-E\cdot\operatorname{curl}B. \tag{F2.11}
(F2.11)

To prove it, expand ∂1(E2B3−E3B2)\partial_1(E^2B^3-E^3B^2), ∂2(E3B1−E1B3)\partial_2(E^3B^1-E^1B^3), and ∂3(E1B2−E2B1)\partial_3(E^1B^2-E^2B^1) with the product rule. The terms differentiating EE, grouped by their undifferentiated factor, are

B1(∂2E3−∂3E2)+B2(∂3E1−∂1E3)+B3(∂1E2−∂2E1). B^1(\partial_2E^3-\partial_3E^2) +B^2(\partial_3E^1-\partial_1E^3) +B^3(\partial_1E^2-\partial_2E^1). \tag{F2.12}
(F2.12)

The other six terms are

−E1(∂2B3−∂3B2)−E2(∂3B1−∂1B3)−E3(∂1B2−∂2B1). -E^1(\partial_2B^3-\partial_3B^2) -E^2(\partial_3B^1-\partial_1B^3) -E^3(\partial_1B^2-\partial_2B^1). \tag{F2.13}
(F2.13)

Their sum is (F2.11).

3. Maxwell’s equations and compatible sources

In vacuum, with the charge and current sources of (F2.1), Maxwell’s equations in SI units are

div⁡E=ρ/ϵ0,div⁡B=0,curl⁡E=−∂tB,curl⁡B=μ0j+μ0ϵ0∂tE. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{div}E&=\rho/\epsilon_0, &\operatorname{div}B&=0,\\ \operatorname{curl}E&=-\partial_tB, &\operatorname{curl}B&=\mu_0j+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_tE. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.14}
(F2.14)

The first two are Gauss constraints. The last two describe time evolution. The word vacuum specifies the constitutive setting: we have not introduced a material’s polarization or magnetization. It does not require the prescribed ρ\rho and jj to vanish.

These physical equations are input to the model. Once adopted, they have mathematical consequences that can be proved.

Proposition 3.1. Charge continuity and constraint propagation

Every smooth solution of (F2.14) obeys

∂tρ+div⁡j=0. \partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}j=0. \tag{F2.15}
(F2.15)

Conversely, suppose the two curl equations in (F2.14) hold and the sources satisfy (F2.15). Then the two constraint defects

div⁡B,div⁡E−ρ/ϵ0 \operatorname{div}B,\qquad \operatorname{div}E-\rho/\epsilon_0 \tag{F2.16}
(F2.16)

are constant in time at every fixed point. In particular, constraints that hold at one time hold throughout II.

Proof. Take divergence of the last equation in (F2.14), use (F2.9), and then use electric Gauss:

0=μ0div⁡j+μ0ϵ0∂t(div⁡E)=μ0(div⁡j+∂tρ). 0=\mu_0\operatorname{div}j +\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t(\operatorname{div}E) =\mu_0(\operatorname{div}j+\partial_t\rho). \tag{F2.17}
(F2.17)

Since μ0>0\mu_0>0, this proves (F2.15). For the converse, divergence of Faraday’s equation gives ∂tdiv⁡B=0\partial_t\operatorname{div}B=0. Solving the last equation in (F2.14) for ∂tE\partial_tE gives

∂t(div⁡E−ρ/ϵ0)=−div⁡j+∂tρϵ0=0. \partial_t(\operatorname{div}E-\rho/\epsilon_0) =-\frac{\operatorname{div}j+\partial_t\rho}{\epsilon_0}=0. \tag{F2.18}
(F2.18)

Integration in time proves the assertions. ▫\square

For a finite box, (F2.15) says that the change in charge is minus outgoing current through its six faces. To state every boundary term, put K=∏i=13[ai,bi]K=\prod_{i=1}^3[a_i,b_i], with ai<bia_i<b_i. Denote by xi,+x_{i,+} the point with coordinate ii set to bib_i, and by xi,−x_{i,-} the point with it set to aia_i. The other two coordinates are integrated over their stated intervals; write their product measure as d2xîd^2x_{\widehat i}. Then

ddt∫Kρd3x=−∑i=13∫∏r≠i[ar,br](ji(t,xi,+)−ji(t,xi,−))d2xî. \frac d{dt}\int_K\rho\,d^3x =-\sum_{i=1}^3\int_{\prod_{r\ne i}[a_r,b_r]} \bigl(j^i(t,x_{i,+})-j^i(t,x_{i,-})\bigr) \,d^2x_{\widehat i}. \tag{F2.19}
(F2.19)

Differentiating the finite-box integral is justified by uniform continuity on compact sets, exactly as in Lesson 1, Proposition 7.1. Integrating each ∂iji\partial_i j^i in its own coordinate and using the fundamental theorem of calculus proves (F2.19).

4. Potentials and the equations they satisfy

A scalar potential ϕ\phi and a vector potential AA give fields by

E1=−∂1ϕ−∂tA1,B1=∂2A3−∂3A2,E2=−∂2ϕ−∂tA2,B2=∂3A1−∂1A3,E3=−∂3ϕ−∂tA3,B3=∂1A2−∂2A1. \begin{aligned} E^1&=-\partial_1\phi-\partial_tA^1,& B^1&=\partial_2A^3-\partial_3A^2,\\ E^2&=-\partial_2\phi-\partial_tA^2,& B^2&=\partial_3A^1-\partial_1A^3,\\ E^3&=-\partial_3\phi-\partial_tA^3,& B^3&=\partial_1A^2-\partial_2A^1. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.20}
(F2.20)

Thus E=−∇ϕ−∂tAE=-\nabla\phi-\partial_tA and B=curl⁡AB=\operatorname{curl}A. Both terms in EE have unit N/C\mathrm N/\mathrm C, and curl AA has the unit of BB.

Proposition 4.1. Exact passage from potentials to fields

The fields (F2.20) satisfy magnetic Gauss and Faraday’s equation identically. They satisfy the other two Maxwell equations with given ρ,j\rho,j exactly when

Δϕ+∂t(div⁡A)=−ρ/ϵ0 \Delta\phi+\partial_t(\operatorname{div}A) =-\rho/\epsilon_0 \tag{F2.21}
(F2.21)

and, for every i=1,2,3i=1,2,3,

ΔAi−μ0ϵ0∂t2Ai−∂i(div⁡A+μ0ϵ0∂tϕ)=−μ0ji. \Delta A^i-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2 A^i -\partial_i\bigl(\operatorname{div}A +\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\phi\bigr) =-\mu_0j^i. \tag{F2.22}
(F2.22)

Proof. Magnetic Gauss is div⁡curl⁡A=0\operatorname{div}\operatorname{curl}A=0. For Faraday, commuting the time derivative and the spatial derivatives gives

curl⁡E+∂tB=−curl⁡∇ϕ−∂tcurl⁡A+∂tcurl⁡A=0. \operatorname{curl}E+\partial_tB =-\operatorname{curl}\nabla\phi -\partial_t\operatorname{curl}A +\partial_t\operatorname{curl}A=0. \tag{F2.23}
(F2.23)

Electric Gauss follows by substituting div⁡E=−Δϕ−∂tdiv⁡A\operatorname{div}E=-\Delta\phi-\partial_t\operatorname{div}A. For the remaining equation, Proposition 2.2 gives its left side as ∇div⁡A−ΔA\nabla\operatorname{div}A-\Delta A, and its right side as μ0j−μ0ϵ0∇∂tϕ−μ0ϵ0∂t2A\mu_0j-\mu_0\epsilon_0\nabla\partial_t\phi -\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2A. Bringing all terms to the indicated sides gives (F2.22). Every step is reversible, so these conditions are also sufficient. ▫\square

Arbitrary potentials therefore produce the two homogeneous identities. The source equations still impose (F2.21) and (F2.22). Introducing potentials does not remove those equations.

5. Gauge transformations, with two complete examples

For a smooth real function χ:I×Ω→ℝ\chi:I\times\Omega\to\mathbb R, define

ϕ′=ϕ−∂tχ,(A′)i=Ai+∂iχ(i=1,2,3). \phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi,\qquad (A')^i=A^i+\partial_i\chi\quad(i=1,2,3). \tag{F2.24}
(F2.24)

The units listed in Section 1 ensure that both additions have the required dimensions. This is a gauge transformation by a globally defined real function on the stated domain.

Theorem 5.1. Gauge invariance

Transformation (F2.24) preserves EE and BB pointwise. It therefore preserves Maxwell’s equations with the same sources, and the test-particle force for the same charge and velocity.

Proof. For every spatial component,

(E′)i=−∂iϕ+∂i∂tχ−∂tAi−∂t∂iχ=Ei. (E')^i=-\partial_i\phi+\partial_i\partial_t\chi -\partial_tA^i-\partial_t\partial_i\chi=E^i. \tag{F2.25}
(F2.25)

Lemma 2.1 cancels the two added terms. Also B′=curl⁡A+curl⁡∇χ=BB'=\operatorname{curl}A+\operatorname{curl}\nabla\chi=B by (F2.8). Substitution into (F2.14) and (F2.3) proves the other statements. ▫\square

Successive transformations by χ1\chi_1 and χ2\chi_2 give the transformation by χ1+χ2\chi_1+\chi_2. The zero function acts as the identity, and −χ-\chi reverses the transformation, directly from (F2.24). A constant χ\chi acts trivially.

Example 5.2. A uniform magnetic field

Let B0B_0 be a real constant with the unit of magnetic field. On all ℝ×ℝ3\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^3, take

ϕ1=0,A1=(−B0x22,B0x12,0),χ=B0x1x22. \phi_1=0,\qquad A_1=\left(-\frac{B_0x^2}{2},\frac{B_0x^1}{2},0\right),\qquad \chi=\frac{B_0x^1x^2}{2}. \tag{F2.26}
(F2.26)

Here ∇χ=(B0x2/2,B0x1/2,0)\nabla\chi=(B_0x^2/2,B_0x^1/2,0), so the second potential is

ϕ2=0,A2=(0,B0x1,0). \phi_2=0,\qquad A_2=(0,B_0x^1,0). \tag{F2.27}
(F2.27)

Both give E=(0,0,0)E=(0,0,0). For A1A_1, the third curl component is B0/2−(−B0/2)=B0B_0/2-(-B_0/2)=B_0; the other two are zero. For A2A_2, the third component is B0−0=B0B_0-0=B_0, and the other two are zero. Thus both give B=(0,0,B0)B=(0,0,B_0). These constant fields satisfy all four Maxwell equations with ρ=0\rho=0, j=(0,0,0)j=(0,0,0).

Example 5.3. A uniform electric field in temporal gauge

Let E0E_0 be constant with the unit of electric field. Start from ϕ=−E0x1\phi=-E_0x^1, A=(0,0,0)A=(0,0,0), giving E=(E0,0,0)E=(E_0,0,0) and B=(0,0,0)B=(0,0,0). Choose χ=−E0tx1\chi=-E_0tx^1. Then

ϕ′=0,A′=(−E0t,0,0),−∇ϕ′−∂tA′=(E0,0,0). \phi'=0,\qquad A'=(-E_0t,0,0),\qquad -\nabla\phi'-\partial_tA'=(E_0,0,0). \tag{F2.28}
(F2.28)

The same electric field now comes entirely from the time derivative of the vector potential. The potentials change while the fields remain constant. Both field descriptions satisfy source-free Maxwell equations.

6. When are all equivalent potentials gauge related?

Theorem 5.1 starts from a gauge function and proves equal fields. The reverse question begins with equal fields and asks for a gauge function. A spatial domain has to be specified to answer it.

Theorem 6.1. The converse on a star-shaped domain

Suppose Ω\Omega is star-shaped about x0∈Ωx_0\in\Omega: every segment x0+s(x−x0)x_0+s(x-x_0), 0≤s≤10\le s\le1, lies in Ω\Omega. Two smooth potential pairs (ϕ,A)(\phi,A) and (ϕ̃,Ã)(\widetilde\phi,\widetilde A) on I×ΩI\times\Omega give equal fields if and only if they are related by (F2.24) for a smooth real χ\chi on that domain.

Proof. One direction is Theorem 5.1. For the other, put a=Ã−Aa=\widetilde A-A and ψ=ϕ̃−ϕ\psi=\widetilde\phi-\phi. Equality of fields says

curl⁡a=0,∇ψ+∂ta=0. \operatorname{curl}a=0,\qquad \nabla\psi+\partial_ta=0. \tag{F2.29}
(F2.29)

Writing y=x−x0y=x-x_0, define the actual candidate

h(t,x)=∫01∑i=13yiai(t,x0+sy)ds. h(t,x)=\int_0^1\sum_{i=1}^3 y^i a^i(t,x_0+sy)\,ds. \tag{F2.30}
(F2.30)

All segments lie in the domain. For a fixed segment, compactness and openness give a neighbourhood in which the integrand and every derivative are defined. Differentiation under this finite integral is justified by the same uniform-continuity argument as in Lesson 1. Consequently hh is smooth. Vanishing curl means ∂jai=∂iaj\partial_j a^i=\partial_i a^j for all i,ji,j. Hence

∂jh(t,x)=∫01(aj(t,x0+sy)+s∑i=13yi∂jai(t,x0+sy))ds=∫01dds(saj(t,x0+sy))ds=aj(t,x). \begin{aligned} \partial_jh(t,x) &=\int_0^1\left(a^j(t,x_0+sy) +s\sum_{i=1}^3 y^i\partial_j a^i(t,x_0+sy)\right)ds\\ &=\int_0^1\frac d{ds}\bigl(s a^j(t,x_0+sy)\bigr)ds =a^j(t,x). \end{aligned} \tag{F2.31}
(F2.31)

The term at s=0s=0 is zero. Equations (F2.29) and (F2.31) give ∇(ψ+∂th)=0\nabla(\psi+\partial_th)=0. Integrating the gradient along the segment from x0x_0 to xx shows that this function is spatially constant. Since h(t,x0)=0h(t,x_0)=0, its value is ψ(t,x0)\psi(t,x_0). Fix any t0∈It_0\in I and set

χ(t,x)=h(t,x)−∫t0tψ(s,x0)ds. \chi(t,x)=h(t,x)-\int_{t_0}^{t}\psi(s,x_0)\,ds. \tag{F2.32}
(F2.32)

Then ∇χ=a\nabla\chi=a and ∂tχ=−ψ\partial_t\chi=-\psi. These are precisely the two relations (F2.24). ▫\square

Theorem 6.2. Constructing potentials from the homogeneous equations

On the same star-shaped domain, every smooth pair E,BE,B satisfying div⁡B=0\operatorname{div}B=0 and curl⁡E=−∂tB\operatorname{curl}E=-\partial_tB has smooth potentials. Consequently the map (F2.20), with potential pairs identified when they differ by (F2.24), is a bijection onto these homogeneous-field pairs.

Proof. Continue to write y=x−x0y=x-x_0. Define all three vector components by

A1(t,x)=∫01s(y3B2(t,x0+sy)−y2B3(t,x0+sy))ds,A2(t,x)=∫01s(y1B3(t,x0+sy)−y3B1(t,x0+sy))ds,A3(t,x)=∫01s(y2B1(t,x0+sy)−y1B2(t,x0+sy))ds. \begin{aligned} A^1(t,x)&=\int_0^1s\bigl(y^3B^2(t,x_0+sy)-y^2B^3(t,x_0+sy)\bigr)ds,\\ A^2(t,x)&=\int_0^1s\bigl(y^1B^3(t,x_0+sy)-y^3B^1(t,x_0+sy)\bigr)ds,\\ A^3(t,x)&=\int_0^1s\bigl(y^2B^1(t,x_0+sy)-y^1B^2(t,x_0+sy)\bigr)ds. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.32a}
(F2.32a)

These formulas retain the segment parameter and all coordinate factors. They define smooth functions by the compact-segment argument in Theorem 6.1. 7. Choosing a gauge and tracking the freedom that remains

Three frequently used conditions on potentials are

Coulomb:div⁡A=0,Lorenz:div⁡A+μ0ϵ0∂tϕ=0,temporal:ϕ=0. \begin{array}{ll} \text{Coulomb:}&\operatorname{div}A=0,\\ \text{Lorenz:}&\operatorname{div}A+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\phi=0,\\ \text{temporal:}&\phi=0. \end{array} \tag{F2.38}
(F2.38)

For the Lorenz condition, μ0ϵ0=1/c2\mu_0\epsilon_0=1/c^2 is retained. A gauge condition is a restriction on potential descriptions. It need not leave a unique description.

Let G=div⁡A+μ0ϵ0∂tϕG=\operatorname{div}A+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\phi. Substituting (F2.24), with every derivative term included, gives

div⁡A′=div⁡A+Δχ,G′=G+Δχ−μ0ϵ0∂t2χ,ϕ′=ϕ−∂tχ. \begin{aligned} \operatorname{div}A'&=\operatorname{div}A+\Delta\chi,\\ G'&=G+\Delta\chi-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\chi,\\ \phi'&=\phi-\partial_t\chi. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.39}
(F2.39)

Consequently a transformation preserves Coulomb gauge exactly when Δχ=0\Delta\chi=0, preserves Lorenz gauge exactly when Δχ−μ0ϵ0∂t2χ=0\Delta\chi-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\chi=0, and preserves temporal gauge exactly when ∂tχ=0\partial_t\chi=0. These equivalences follow directly from (F2.39).

Temporal gauge can be reached explicitly on the product domain:

χ(t,x)=∫t0tϕ(s,x)ds⇒ϕ′=0. \chi(t,x)=\int_{t_0}^{t}\phi(s,x)\,ds \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi'=0. \tag{F2.40}
(F2.40)

Reaching Coulomb gauge requires solving Δχ=−div⁡A\Delta\chi=-\operatorname{div}A; reaching Lorenz gauge requires solving Δχ−μ0ϵ0∂t2χ=−G\Delta\chi-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\chi=-G. These are the exact equations obtained from the transformation. Their boundary and initial data are part of any particular gauge selection problem. We have constructed the temporal transformation and the explicit examples above; we do not infer global solvability of an unspecified boundary problem from these formulas.

For example, when smooth boundary values have been specified, if one additionally requires n⋅A=0n\cdot A=0 on a given spatial boundary with unit normal nn, a transformation preserves that requirement exactly when n⋅∇χ=0n\cdot\nabla\chi=0 there. If a boundary value of ϕ\phi is fixed, preservation requires ∂tχ=0\partial_t\chi=0 there. These statements follow by substitution and show why extra conditions must be retained.

In Lorenz gauge, (F2.21) and (F2.22) become

Δϕ−μ0ϵ0∂t2ϕ=−ρ/ϵ0,ΔAi−μ0ϵ0∂t2Ai=−μ0ji(i=1,2,3). \Delta\phi-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0, \qquad \Delta A^i-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2A^i=-\mu_0j^i \quad(i=1,2,3). \tag{F2.41}
(F2.41)

For the scalar equation, substitute div⁡A=−μ0ϵ0∂tϕ\operatorname{div}A=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\phi into (F2.21). For the vector equations, the gradient of G=0G=0 in (F2.22) vanishes. No coupling constant has been discarded.

8. Waves and the energy they carry

Proposition 8.1. Field wave equations with their source terms

Every smooth Maxwell solution satisfies

ΔE−μ0ϵ0∂t2E=1ϵ0∇ρ+μ0∂tj,ΔB−μ0ϵ0∂t2B=−μ0curl⁡j. \begin{aligned} \Delta E-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2E &=\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\nabla\rho+\mu_0\partial_tj,\\ \Delta B-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2B &=-\mu_0\operatorname{curl}j. \end{aligned} \tag{F2.42}
(F2.42)

Proof. Curl of Faraday gives ∇div⁡E−ΔE=−μ0∂tj−μ0ϵ0∂t2E\nabla\operatorname{div}E-\Delta E =-\mu_0\partial_tj-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2E. Substitute electric Gauss and rearrange to obtain the first line. Curl of the last Maxwell equation gives −ΔB=μ0curl⁡j−μ0ϵ0∂t2B-\Delta B=\mu_0\operatorname{curl}j -\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2B, using magnetic Gauss and Faraday. Rearrangement gives the second line. These vector equalities hold in each of the three components by (F2.10). ▫\square

The Maxwell constraints remain required; the wave equations alone are not a replacement for the full system.

Example 8.2. An exact plane wave

Let k>0k>0 have unit inverse length, let ω=ck\omega=ck, and let A0A_0 be a real constant with the unit of AA. Put θ=kx1−ωt\theta=kx^1-\omega t, and choose

ϕ=0,A=(0,A0sin⁡θ,0). \phi=0,\qquad A=(0,A_0\sin\theta,0). \tag{F2.43}
(F2.43)

Direct differentiation gives

E=(0,A0ωcos⁡θ,0),B=(0,0,A0kcos⁡θ). E=(0,A_0\omega\cos\theta,0),\qquad B=(0,0,A_0k\cos\theta). \tag{F2.44}
(F2.44)

Their divergences are zero, since the nonzero electric component has no x2x^2 dependence and the nonzero magnetic component has no x3x^3 dependence. The two curl equations are verified by

curl⁡E=(0,0,−A0ωksin⁡θ)=−∂tB,curl⁡B=(0,A0k2sin⁡θ,0),μ0ϵ0∂tE=(0,μ0ϵ0A0ω2sin⁡θ,0)=(0,A0k2sin⁡θ,0). \begin{aligned} \operatorname{curl}E&=(0,0,-A_0\omega k\sin\theta) =-\partial_tB,\\ \operatorname{curl}B&=(0,A_0k^2\sin\theta,0),\\ \mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_tE &=(0,\mu_0\epsilon_0 A_0\omega^2\sin\theta,0) =(0,A_0k^2\sin\theta,0). \end{aligned} \tag{F2.45}
(F2.45)

Thus all four Maxwell equations hold with zero sources. The phase is constant along x1=ct+ξx^1=ct+\xi; this exact wave travels in the positive first direction at speed cc. It is an infinite plane wave, not a claim about a finite-energy pulse or a general existence theorem.

Proposition 8.3. Local electromagnetic energy balance

Define the field energy density and flux by

w=ϵ02((E1)2+(E2)2+(E3)2)+12μ0((B1)2+(B2)2+(B3)2),S=1μ0E×B. w=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\bigl((E^1)^2+(E^2)^2+(E^3)^2\bigr) +\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\bigl((B^1)^2+(B^2)^2+(B^3)^2\bigr), \qquad S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}E\times B. \tag{F2.46}
(F2.46)

For every smooth Maxwell solution,

∂tw+div⁡S=−j⋅E. \partial_tw+\operatorname{div}S=-j\cdot E. \tag{F2.47}
(F2.47)

Proof. The product rule and the evolution equations give

∂tw=ϵ0E⋅∂tE+1μ0B⋅∂tB=1μ0E⋅curl⁡B−j⋅E−1μ0B⋅curl⁡E=−1μ0div⁡(E×B)−j⋅E, \begin{aligned} \partial_tw &=\epsilon_0 E\cdot\partial_tE +\frac1{\mu_0}B\cdot\partial_tB\\ &=\frac1{\mu_0}E\cdot\operatorname{curl}B-j\cdot E -\frac1{\mu_0}B\cdot\operatorname{curl}E\\ &=-\frac1{\mu_0}\operatorname{div}(E\times B)-j\cdot E, \end{aligned} \tag{F2.48}
(F2.48)

where the last line is the proved product identity (F2.11). This is (F2.47). ▫\square

9. Exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 1. Compute all vector derivatives

For constants α,γ\alpha,\gamma, take V=(−αx2,αx1,γx3)V=(-\alpha x^2,\alpha x^1,\gamma x^3). Calculate its divergence and all components of its curl.

Solution. The divergence is 0+0+γ=γ0+0+\gamma=\gamma. The first curl component is 0−0=00-0=0, the second is 0−0=00-0=0, and the third is α−(−α)=2α\alpha-(-\alpha)=2\alpha. Therefore curl⁡V=(0,0,2α)\operatorname{curl}V=(0,0,2\alpha). Divergence and curl are different operations even when applied to the same vector.

Exercise 2. A potential with a prescribed charge density

Take constant ρ0\rho_0, A=0A=0, and ϕ=−ρ0((x1)2+(x2)2+(x3)2)/(6ϵ0)\phi=-\rho_0((x^1)^2+(x^2)^2+(x^3)^2)/(6\epsilon_0) on all space. Find the fields and the sources for which they solve Maxwell’s equations.

Solution. For each ii, differentiation gives Ei=ρ0xi/(3ϵ0)E^i=\rho_0x^i/(3\epsilon_0), and B=0B=0. The electric divergence is the sum of three terms ρ0/(3ϵ0)\rho_0/(3\epsilon_0), hence ρ0/ϵ0\rho_0/\epsilon_0. Its curl is zero because each component depends only on its own coordinate. All time derivatives and the magnetic curl vanish. Thus ρ=ρ0\rho=\rho_0, j=0j=0 give all four equations. The same result follows from Δϕ=−ρ0(2+2+2)/(6ϵ0)=−ρ0/ϵ0\Delta\phi=-\rho_0(2+2+2)/(6\epsilon_0)=-\rho_0/\epsilon_0. For ρ0≠0\rho_0\ne0, this is a spatially uniform, nonlocalized source; no assumption of finite total charge is being made.

Exercise 3. Both time and space derivatives matter

Start with ϕ=0,A=0\phi=0,A=0, and choose χ=βtx1x2\chi=\beta t x^1x^2, where β\beta is a constant with unit N/(Cm)\mathrm N/(\mathrm{C\,m}). Calculate the transformed potentials and their fields directly.

Solution. They are

ϕ′=−βx1x2,A′=(βtx2,βtx1,0). \phi'=-\beta x^1x^2,\qquad A'=(\beta t x^2,\beta t x^1,0). \tag{F2.50}
(F2.50)

The two contributions to the electric field are −∇ϕ′=(βx2,βx1,0)-\nabla\phi'=(\beta x^2,\beta x^1,0) and −∂tA′=(−βx2,−βx1,0)-\partial_tA'=(-\beta x^2,-\beta x^1,0); their sum is zero. The first two magnetic components vanish, and the third is βt−βt=0\beta t-\beta t=0. Thus the two nonconstant potentials still describe zero fields. Omitting either electric contribution would produce an incorrect answer.

Exercise 4. Incompatible prescribed sources

Let ρ(t,x)=ρ0t/T0\rho(t,x)=\rho_0t/T_0, with T0>0T_0>0 a fixed time and ρ0≠0\rho_0\ne0 a constant charge density. Take j=0j=0. Can these be the sources of a smooth Maxwell solution on a nonempty open time interval and spatial domain?

Solution. Their continuity expression is ∂tρ+div⁡j=ρ0/T0≠0\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}j=\rho_0/T_0\ne0. Proposition 3.1 proves that every Maxwell solution has zero continuity expression. These specified sources therefore admit no such solution. The obstruction is the explicit scalar source defect ρ0/T0\rho_0/T_0, not a failure of the gauge description.

Exercise 5. Evolution does not impose the initial constraints

For λ≠0\lambda\ne0, set E=(λx1,0,0)E=(\lambda x^1,0,0), B=0B=0, ρ=0\rho=0, and j=0j=0. Test all four equations.

Solution. All time derivatives vanish. Both curls vanish, so the two evolution equations hold. Magnetic Gauss also holds. But div⁡E=λ≠0=ρ/ϵ0\operatorname{div}E=\lambda\ne0=\rho/\epsilon_0. The electric constraint fails at every time. Its defect is constant in time, as (F2.18) predicts. This also gives a source-free solution of the field wave equations that is not a Maxwell solution: ΔE=0\Delta E=0, ∂t2E=0\partial_t^2E=0, and both terms for BB vanish.

Exercise 6. Gauge conditions can leave many representatives

Show that both magnetic potentials in Example 5.2 satisfy Coulomb, Lorenz and temporal gauge. Explain why their difference does not contradict any of those conditions.

Solution. Their divergences are respectively ∂1(−B0x2/2)+∂2(B0x1/2)+0=0\partial_1(-B_0x^2/2)+\partial_2(B_0x^1/2)+0=0 and ∂2(B0x1)=0\partial_2(B_0x^1)=0. Their scalar potentials are zero. Thus all three conditions in (F2.38) hold. Their gauge function χ=B0x1x2/2\chi=B_0x^1x^2/2 obeys Δχ=0\Delta\chi=0 and ∂tχ=∂t2χ=0\partial_t\chi=\partial_t^2\chi=0, exactly the three residual conditions in (F2.39). These gauge conditions alone do not choose a unique representative.

Exercise 7. Classify the missing-axis family

Allow any real κ\kappa, including zero, in (F2.34). For which κ1,κ2\kappa_1,\kappa_2 are the two resulting potential pairs related by a globally defined real gauge function on I×ΩI\times\Omega?

Solution. Their difference is (F2.34) with coefficient κ2−κ1\kappa_2-\kappa_1. Its integral around the specified circle is 2π(κ2−κ1)2\pi(\kappa_2-\kappa_1). If the difference is a gradient, that integral must be zero, so κ2=κ1\kappa_2=\kappa_1. If the coefficients are equal, the potentials are identical and χ=0\chi=0 supplies a gauge transformation. Thus equality of coefficients is both necessary and sufficient within this family and the declared class of real gauge functions.

Exercise 8. Energy and direction of the plane wave
