Yang–Mills theory on a lattice

A lattice replaces spacetime by vertices and edges. A gauge field assigns a transport matrix to each edge. Products around loops record curvature, and a finite product of compact group measures makes expectation values actual integrals. This unit constructs those objects, proves their relation to the original classical action, and obtains a Hilbert space and time evolution from the finite Euclidean model.

The route has two parts. Part I builds links, plaquettes, the Wilson action and its finite probability measure. It proves a quantitative comparison with a fixed smooth continuum connection. Part II constructs the transfer operator, its physical subspace, trace, Hamiltonian domain and reflection form. These constructions provide the starting point for simulations and the later study of continuum limits.

Read transport and holonomy, action and field equations, Euclidean theory, and quantum gauge symmetry first. The compact group measure and averaging projection are proved in Unit 12. This unit proves the additional approximation and compact-operator facts it uses. Matrix products follow the course’s active gauge convention: the first edge traversed supplies the rightmost factor.

Keep the physical speed cc, Planck constant ℏ\hbar, coupling gYMg_{\mathrm{YM}}, edge lengths and temporal step Δτ\Delta\tau visible. A lattice spacing is a length. An energy extracted from a transfer eigenvalue has the factor ℏ/Δτ\hbar/\Delta\tau. A finite number of links with a continuous compact group still gives an infinite-dimensional space of wavefunctions.

By the end, you can write the exact lattice action in unequal spacings, control its error on a fixed smooth field, evaluate a compact plaquette integral, construct the gauge-projected transfer operator and determine what its spectrum proves at that fixed regulator.

1. Links are maps between vertex fibres

Let G⊂U(N)G\subset U(N) be a closed matrix Lie subgroup, with its given faithful defining representation and κ(X,Y)=−tr⁡(XY)\kappa(X,Y)=-\operatorname{tr}(XY). Let a finite graph have vertex set VV, a set E+E_+ of chosen directed edges, and formal reversed edges e‾\bar e, with s(e‾)=t(e)s(\bar e)=t(e), t(e‾)=s(e)t(\bar e)=s(e). Different edges may have the same endpoints. A configuration is

U∈𝒬=GE+,Ue‾=Ue−1. U\in\mathcal Q=G^{E_+},\qquad U_{\bar e}=U_e^{-1}. \tag{L.1}
(L.1)

It represents a linear isometry from the copy of ℂN\mathbb C^N at s(e)s(e) to the copy at t(e)t(e). For a path γ=(e1,…,em)\gamma=(e_1,\ldots,e_m), traversed in that order, define

Uγ=Uem⋯Ue1:ℂs(e1)N→ℂt(em)N. U_\gamma=U_{e_m}\cdots U_{e_1}: \mathbb C^N_{s(e_1)}\longrightarrow\mathbb C^N_{t(e_m)}. \tag{L.2}
(L.2)

The empty path has the identity matrix. Concatenating paths multiplies their transport matrices in the same composition order; reversing a path gives the inverse matrix. Both assertions follow by writing the finite products, with adjacent inverse factors cancelling in a backtrack.

The vertex gauge group 𝒢=GV\mathcal G=G^V acts by

(h⋅U)e=ht(e)Uehs(e)−1. (h\cdot U)_e=h_{t(e)}U_eh_{s(e)}^{-1}. \tag{L.3}
(L.3)

This preserves the reversal rule. Direct multiplication proves h⋅(k⋅U)=(hk)⋅Uh\cdot(k\cdot U)=(hk)\cdot U, with pointwise product in 𝒢\mathcal G. Intermediate vertex factors cancel in (L.2), giving

Uγh=ht(γ)Uγhs(γ)−1. U_\gamma^{\,h}=h_{t(\gamma)}U_\gamma h_{s(\gamma)}^{-1}. \tag{L.4}
(L.4)

For a closed path, tr⁡Uγ\operatorname{tr}U_\gamma is gauge invariant. We will distinguish the full trace, its real part, and the optionally divided trace N−1tr⁡UγN^{-1}\operatorname{tr}U_\gamma; they are different specified observables. Reversal conjugates the complex trace because Uγ−1=Uγ*U_\gamma^{-1}=U_\gamma^*. Changing the starting vertex conjugates the loop matrix, hence preserves its trace. A loop that winds around a periodic direction is included; it need not bound plaquettes.

5. Finite-volume probability is an actual measure

Use Haar probability μ\mu on GG. A complete construction for this class of groups is Q.6–Q.7 of the F12 proof: the metric mg(v,w)=−tr⁡(g−1vg−1w)m_g(v,w)=-\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}v\,g^{-1}w) is bi-invariant; its coordinate volume densities glue by change of variables; compactness makes the total volume finite and positive; dividing by that number gives μ\mu. Solution 6
