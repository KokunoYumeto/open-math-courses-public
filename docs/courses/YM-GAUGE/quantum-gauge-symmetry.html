Quantum gauge symmetry

Lesson YM-F12 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

The preceding lesson constructed quantum states, observables and dynamics for a free scalar field. A gauge theory adds a constraint: different field descriptions can refer to the same physical configuration. We now calculate how this affects the state space and the integrals used in quantum calculations.

Our first quantum model has only three links and finitely many Fourier states. Its gauge transformations, constraints, Hamiltonian and observables are actual finite matrices. A second model is a rotating point in the plane. It makes the orbit, gauge slice, Jacobian and repeated intersections visible. These constructions give a direct route to Grassmann variables, BRST symmetry and Ward identities.

Read the original connection and action in Lesson 7, the Gauss constraint in Lesson 8, and quantum states and domains in Lesson 11. The full elliptic operator used in background gauge was calculated in the Unit 10 operator chapter. All new algebraic and finite-integral constructions are given below.

0. What a quantum gauge constraint specifies

Let a gauge group act by unitary operators U(g)U(g) on a Hilbert space ℋ\mathcal H. In the charge-free constraint model studied here, the physical Hilbert space is

ℋphys={ψ∈ℋ:U(g)ψ=ψ for every g∈G}. \mathcal H_{\rm phys} =\{\psi\in\mathcal H:U(g)\psi=\psi\text{ for every }g\in G\}. \tag{F12.1}
(F12.1)

This is a closed linear space: each condition is the kernel of the bounded operator U(g)−IU(g)-I, and an intersection of closed sets is closed. Its unit vectors define physical pure states with the same constant-phase identification as in Lesson 11. Equation (F12.1) states which constraint sector this lesson chooses. A different charge sector would need its own definition.

An operator preserving this space can act on physical states. A bounded self-adjoint operator commuting with every U(g)U(g) restricts to a self-adjoint physical observable. To prove the last statement directly, its restriction is bounded and symmetric on all of the physical Hilbert space; the inner-product definition of the adjoint gives the same operator on that entire space. The averaging projection constructed in Section 3 makes this restriction explicit.

For a differentiable one-parameter group written U(etX)=e−itCX/ℏU(e^{tX})=e^{-itC_X/\hbar}, differentiating an invariant vector gives CXψ=0C_X\psi=0 on the generator domain. The converse for the identity component, and the additional condition for disconnected groups, will be proved below. The continuum Gauss formula in Section 1 first identifies the original classical generator. The finite triangle then supplies fully defined quantum matrices for the corresponding constraint calculation.

Gauge fixing chooses a representative of each group orbit on a specified domain. A Jacobian records how the integration density changes under that choice. A Grassmann algebra supplies anticommuting variables whose coefficient integrals represent signed determinants. BRST names an odd, square-zero operation that encodes the gauge transformation in that algebra. A Ward identity is a relation among integrals obtained from a symmetry and its actual boundary behaviour. Each definition is followed by a construction and calculation, including cases where a proposed global gauge slice has multiple intersections.

The ghost symbol cc denotes an odd algebra element in the BRST sections. Where the physical speed appears there, it is written cphysc_{\rm phys}, with the exact identification stated. The connection AA is the original connection Γ\Gamma of the earlier lessons; its active rule remains Au=uAu−1−duu−1A^u=uAu^{-1}-du\,u^{-1}.

The field-level BRST formulas are identities in the defined algebra of fields and their derivatives. The finite models provide the actual integrals and proved Ward identities. The final Gaussian calculation shows precisely what a finite-order perturbative expansion computes, including a numerical coefficient and an explicit error bound.

1. The original Gauss generator and its signs

Retain F07–F08’s closed matrix Lie group G⊂U(N)G\subset U(N), skew-Hermitian Lie algebra, and κ(X,Y)=−tr⁡(XY)\kappa(X,Y)=-\operatorname{tr}(XY). 2. A normalized invariant measure for the compact groups used here

Since GG is a closed matrix Lie subgroup of the compact group U(N)U(N), it is a compact smooth manifold. For v,w∈TgGv,w\in T_gG, define a Riemannian metric

mg(v,w)=κ(g−1v,g−1w). m_g(v,w)=\kappa(g^{-1}v,g^{-1}w). \tag{Q.6}
(Q.6)

Left translation preserves g−1vg^{-1}v. Under right translation by hh, it becomes h−1(g−1v)hh^{-1}(g^{-1}v)h; trace cyclicity proves that (Q.6) is unchanged. Hence both translations are isometries. In a chart its Riemannian density is det⁡(mij)|dx1⋯dxd|\sqrt{\det(m_{ij})}\,|dx^1\cdots dx^d|. The determinant transformation law and ordinary finite-dimensional change of variables show that these chart densities agree. A partition of unity therefore defines a global positive measure vol⁡m\operatorname{vol}_m. The partition construction uses finitely many charts on this compact manifold; each weight can be supported in a chart with compact closure, where its smooth density is bounded. This proves vol⁡m(G)<∞\operatorname{vol}_m(G)<\infty. A smaller coordinate ball has a strictly positive lower density bound, proving vol⁡m(G)>0\operatorname{vol}_m(G)>0.

For every point of the compact smooth group, choose a chart containing concentric coordinate balls whose closed outer ball stays inside that chart. The inverse images of the smaller balls cover the group; compactness selects finitely many. In each outer ball, a smooth bump is obtained by scaling and translating b(z)=exp⁡[−1/(1−|z|2)]b(z)=\exp[-1/(1-|z|^2)] for |z|<1|z|<1, with value zero outside. Every derivative extends continuously as zero across the boundary: successive derivatives are finite sums of powers of (1−|z|2)−1(1-|z|^2)^{-1} and coordinate polynomials times the same exponential, and uke−u→0u^k e^{-u}\to0 as u→∞u\to\infty for every integer k≥0. These bumps are strictly positive on the smaller balls. Their finite sum is therefore a positive smooth function on the whole group. Divide each bump by that sum. The resulting smooth weights sum to one and have compact support inside their charts. This is the partition used to integrate the metric density. Independence of the chosen partition follows by inserting a second partition, expanding the finite double sum, and applying the density change-of-variables formula on each overlap. Consequently the measure used by Q.7 is defined without an unintroduced global construction.

Thus

dμ(g)=vol⁡m(G)−1dvol⁡m(g) d\mu(g)=\operatorname{vol}_m(G)^{-1}d\operatorname{vol}_m(g) \tag{Q.7}
(Q.7)

is a probability measure invariant under left and right translation. Inversion is an isometry too: at gg, its differential is v↦−g−1vg−1v\mapsto-g^{-1}vg^{-1}, and the left-trivialized vector at g−1g^{-1} is −vg−1=−g(g−1v)g−1-vg^{-1}=-g(g^{-1}v)g^{-1}. Trace invariance again preserves its norm and all pairings. Therefore inversion preserves μ\mu.

For a zero-dimensional compact Lie group there are finitely many points, since its discrete open point-cover has a finite subcover; take normalized counting measure instead. For a disconnected positive-dimensional group the same density construction applies to every component and translations permute components with their volume. There are finitely many components: a finite collection of connected chart neighbourhoods covers the compact manifold and each lies in one component. This construction supplies all measure properties used below, without an unproved appeal to an invariant averaging symbol.

For U(1)={eiθ}U(1)=\{e^{i\theta}\} the metric induced by κ(i,i)=1\kappa(i,i)=1 is dθ2d\theta^2, with period 2π2\pi. Its normalized measure is exactly dθ/(2π)d\theta/(2\pi). Product groups use the product measures; repeated change of variables proves their invariance. This is the measure in the finite model below.

3. Averaging is an orthogonal projection, with its domain specified

Let ℋ\mathcal H be a complex Hilbert space and U:G→𝒰(ℋ)U:G\to\mathcal U(\mathcal H) a strongly continuous unitary representation. For every ψ∈ℋ\psi\in\mathcal H, the map g↦U(g)ψg\mapsto U(g)\psi is continuous on compact GG. Define its vector integral by uniformly approximating it with finite-valued measurable functions: cover GG by finitely many sets on which its oscillation is at most ϵ\epsilon, disjointize that cover, choose a value on each nonempty set, and sum that value times its measure. Any two such approximations differ in integral norm by at most the sum of their uniform errors. Completeness gives a limit independent of the approximation. Scalar products with a fixed vector pass through these finite sums and their norm limit. Thus the bounded linear operator

Pψ=∫GU(g)ψdμ(g),Dom⁡P=ℋ,∥Pψ∥≤∥ψ∥ P\psi=\int_GU(g)\psi\,d\mu(g),\qquad \operatorname{Dom}P=\mathcal H,\qquad \|P\psi\|\leq\|\psi\| \tag{Q.8}
(Q.8)

is well defined. Left invariance gives U(h)Pψ=PψU(h)P\psi=P\psi for every hh. Conversely an invariant vector has Pψ=ψP\psi=\psi. Hence the range is exactly the closed invariant subspace ℋG\mathcal H^G, and P2=PP^2=P. Finally unitarity and inversion invariance give P*=PP^*=P, by moving U(g)U(g) across the Hilbert inner product and replacing gg by g−1g^{-1}. Therefore PP is the orthogonal projection onto ℋG\mathcal H^G.

A bounded observable OO commuting with every U(g)U(g) commutes with the finite sums defining PP, and hence with PP. Its physical action is its restriction to ℋG\mathcal H^G. For an unbounded operator this argument alone is insufficient: its domain must also be invariant and appropriate limits must belong to that domain. The finite model below has bounded operators and an explicit full domain, so this issue does not arise there.

For a strongly differentiable vector, vanishing infinitesimal generators imply invariance under the identity component: if (d/dt)0U(etX)ψ=0(d/dt)_0U(e^{tX})\psi=0, the representation property gives zero derivative at every tt, so U(etX)ψU(e^{tX})\psi is constant. Products of these exponentials generate the identity component. To see the latter, the exponential map has derivative the identity at zero, hence contains an identity neighbourhood by the finite-dimensional inverse function theorem; products of that neighbourhood form an open subgroup, whose cosets are open, so its intersection with the connected identity component is the whole component. For disconnected groups, invariance under the other components remains an additional condition. Group averaging imposes it automatically.

6. Grassmann algebra, integration order, and the determinant

For n≥1n\geq1, form the complex exterior algebra on 2n2n ordered generators c1,c‾1,c2,c‾2,…,cn,c‾nc_1,\bar c_1,c_2,\bar c_2,\ldots,c_n,\bar c_n. Products are associative and each pair of odd generators anticommutes; each generator squares to zero. Every element has a unique finite expansion in increasing products of distinct generators, since these products define a basis. A left derivative ∂θ\partial_\theta is the odd derivation with ∂θθ=1\partial_\theta\theta=1, derivative zero on the other generators, and ∂θ(ab)=(∂θa)b+(−1)|a|a∂θb\partial_\theta(ab)=(\partial_\theta a)b+(-1)^{|a|}a\partial_\theta b for homogeneous aa.

Define the Berezin integral to be the coefficient of Ω=c1c‾1c2c‾2⋯cnc‾n\Omega=c_1\bar c_1c_2\bar c_2\cdots c_n\bar c_n. Our measure notation is
