Lie groups and Lie algebras

Lesson YM-F04 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

In Lesson 3, matrices described finite transformations. Now we will describe transformations that vary smoothly and calculate their derivatives. A finite transformation belongs to a Lie group. Its possible velocities at the identity form a Lie algebra. The exponential connects these two objects by an actual curve of matrices.

We will construct that connection, including the convergence and inverse maps it needs. The main examples are U(1)U(1), SU(2)SU(2) and SU(3)SU(3). Their matrices, real dimensions, generator conventions and commutators will all be explicit. The rotation example will also show why an infinitesimal description does not by itself specify every global identification in a group.

Preparation is Lesson 3 and elementary real calculus: differentiation, integration, the real exponential and logarithm, sine and cosine, and completeness of the real numbers. Matrix power series and the local coordinate arguments used below are proved here. All parameter spaces and Lie algebras in this lesson are real unless a different ground field is explicitly given. Matrix entries may still be complex.

1. Estimates and determinants we will need

Fix a positive integer nn. For an nn-by-nn complex matrix define the Frobenius norm and trace by

∥A∥F=(∑j=1n∑k=1n|Ajk|2)1/2,tr⁡A=∑j=1nAjj. \|A\|_F=\left(\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^n|A_{jk}|^2\right)^{1/2}, \qquad \operatorname{tr}A=\sum_{j=1}^n A_{jj}. \tag{F4.1}
(F4.1)

In particular, ∥In∥F=n\|I_n\|_F=\sqrt n. We retain that value; this norm does not assign norm one to the identity when n>1n>1.

Lemma 1.1. Finite-dimensional estimates

For compatible square matrices,

∥A+B∥F≤∥A∥F+∥B∥F,∥AB∥F≤∥A∥F∥B∥F,∥A†∥F=∥A∥F,|tr⁡A|≤n∥A∥F. \begin{aligned} \|A+B\|_F&\leq\|A\|_F+\|B\|_F,& \|AB\|_F&\leq\|A\|_F\|B\|_F,\\ \|A^\dagger\|_F&=\|A\|_F,& |\operatorname{tr}A|&\leq\sqrt n\,\|A\|_F. \end{aligned} \tag{F4.2}
(F4.2)

Proof. First, the inner product of Lesson 3 obeys |⟨v,w⟩|≤∥v∥∥w∥|\langle v,w\rangle|\leq\|v\|\|w\|. For v≠0v\ne0, expand the nonnegative quantity

(F4.3)

For v=0v=0, the assertion is immediate. Apply this inequality to arrays of entries to expand ∥A+B∥F2\|A+B\|_F^2 and bound its two cross terms by 2∥A∥F∥B∥F2\|A\|_F\|B\|_F. Taking square roots gives the first inequality. For the product, apply it to each row and column, giving

(F4.4)

Conjugation and transposition preserve the sum of squared absolute values. Finally apply the same inner-product inequality to the nn diagonal entries and the vector of nn ones; the sum of squared diagonal entries is at most the sum over all entries. ▫\square

Every matrix entry has absolute value at most ∥A∥F\|A\|_F. Thus an absolutely convergent matrix series converges entry by entry. It converges in this norm because there are finitely many entries.

The determinant as a full finite sum

Let SnS_n be the set of permutations of 1,…,n1,\ldots,n. The sign sgn⁡σ\operatorname{sgn}\sigma is (−1)r(-1)^r, where rr is the number of inversions in that permutation. Define

det⁡A=∑σ∈Sn(sgn⁡σ)∏j=1nAj,σ(j). \det A=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}(\operatorname{sgn}\sigma) \prod_{j=1}^n A_{j,\sigma(j)}. \tag{F4.5}
(F4.5)

This agrees with ad−bcad-bc for a two-by-two matrix. Reindexing the sum by σ−1\sigma^{-1} proves det⁡AT=det⁡A\det A^T=\det A. It also expresses the determinant as a multilinear function of its columns. Interchanging two adjacent columns reverses its sign: reindex the permutations by that interchange, which reverses the parity of the inversion count. Any column interchange is a product of an odd number of adjacent interchanges. A matrix with repeated columns consequently has determinant zero. Finally, det⁡In=1\det I_n=1.

These facts prove multiplicativity without omitting any terms. If ff is any alternating multilinear function of nn columns, expand each column in the coordinate vectors. Terms with a repeated coordinate vector vanish. In a remaining term the coordinate vectors are a permutation of the standard basis, so column interchanges give

f(v1,…,vn)=det⁡(v1,…,vn)f(e1,…,en). f(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=\det(v_1,\ldots,v_n)f(e_1,\ldots,e_n). \tag{F4.6}
(F4.6)

Here the coefficient sum is exactly (F4.5), in its column form. Apply (F4.6) to f(v1,…,vn)=det⁡(Av1,…,Avn)f(v_1,\ldots,v_n)=\det(Av_1,\ldots,Av_n). Its value on the standard basis is det⁡A\det A, proving

det⁡(AB)=det⁡Adet⁡B,det⁡A†=det⁡A¯. \det(AB)=\det A\det B,\qquad \det A^\dagger=\overline{\det A}. \tag{F4.7}
(F4.7)

The second identity also follows directly from the real signs in (F4.5) and the transpose identity.

For a subset S⊆{1,…,n}S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}, let X[S,S]X[S,S] be the submatrix with its rows and columns both in SS, in their original increasing order. Give the empty determinant the value one. Multilinearity in all columns yields the exact polynomial identity

(F4.8)

To verify the coefficient for a chosen SS, use columns from XX at those positions and identity columns elsewhere. The identity columns force the complementary rows. Moving the remaining rows and columns to the same end gives the two identical permutation signs, whose product is one, and leaves precisely the principal minor in (F4.8). In particular the derivative of determinant at the identity is trace.

The adjugate has entries (adj⁡A)jk=(−1)j+kdet⁡A[k̂,ĵ](\operatorname{adj}A)_{jk}=(-1)^{j+k}\det A[\widehat k,\widehat j], where the hats mean omission of the indicated row and column. Expansion of (F4.5) along a row gives

Aadj⁡A=(adj⁡A)A=(det⁡A)In. A\operatorname{adj}A=(\operatorname{adj}A)A=(\det A)I_n. \tag{F4.9}
(F4.9)

For an off-diagonal entry of the first product, that expansion is the determinant of a matrix with one row repeated, hence zero. The diagonal entry is the determinant of AA. The column expansion proves the other product. The convention for the empty minor includes n=1n=1. Consequently AA is invertible exactly when det⁡A≠0\det A\ne0: (F4.9) supplies its inverse in that case, and (F4.7) rules out zero determinant for an invertible matrix.

The entries of the inverse are therefore quotients of polynomials with nonzero denominator. They are smooth on the open set of invertible matrices. Here smooth means that every order of partial derivative exists and is continuous in the real and imaginary matrix entries.

For a differentiable curve A(t)A(t) of invertible matrices, the chain rule, (F4.7), and the derivative in (F4.8) give

ddtdet⁡A(t)=det⁡A(t)tr⁡(A(t)−1A′(t)). \frac d{dt}\det A(t) =\det A(t)\operatorname{tr}\big(A(t)^{-1}A'(t)\big). \tag{F4.10}
(F4.10)

Indeed, at a fixed time t0t_0, factor A(t)=A(t0)(A(t0)−1A(t))A(t)=A(t_0)(A(t_0)^{-1}A(t)). The second factor equals the identity at t0t_0 and has derivative A(t0)−1A′(t0)A(t_0)^{-1}A'(t_0). Applying the ordinary derivative of the finite polynomial (F4.8) proves (F4.10).

2. Constructing the matrix exponential

For every complex square matrix XX, define

exp⁡X=In+∑k=1∞Xkk!. \exp X=I_n+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{X^k}{k!}. \tag{F4.11}
(F4.11)

The series is absolutely convergent. To see this, put r=∥X∥Fr=\|X\|_F. Lemma 1.1 gives ∥Xk∥F≤rk\|X^k\|_F\leq r^k for every k≥1k\geq1. The scalar series ∑k≥0rk/k!\sum_{k\geq0}r^k/k! converges, since successive terms have ratio r/(k+1)r/(k+1), at most 1/21/2 for all sufficiently large kk. Its sum is the usual real exponential ere^r: on any bounded real interval the series and its differentiated series converge uniformly by the same bound, so its derivative equals itself and its value at zero is one. Multiplication by the ordinary function e−re^{-r} then shows that its sum is ere^r.

The corresponding matrix estimates retain the identity contribution:

∥exp⁡X∥F≤n+er−1,∥exp⁡X−In∥F≤er−1,∥expX−∑k=0mXkk!∥F≤∑k=m+1∞rkk!≤rm+1er(m+1)!(m≥0). \begin{aligned} \|\exp X\|_F&\leq\sqrt n+e^r-1,\\ \|\exp X-I_n\|_F&\leq e^r-1,\\ \left\|\exp X-\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{X^k}{k!}\right\|_F &\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{r^k}{k!} \leq\frac{r^{m+1}e^r}{(m+1)!}\quad(m\geq0). \end{aligned} \tag{F4.12}
(F4.12)

For the last inequality, write k=m+1+jk=m+1+j and use (m+1+j)!≥(m+1)!j!(m+1+j)!\geq(m+1)!j!. These are bounds on the full series, which remains (F4.11).

Lemma 2.1. Differentiation of the exponential

The exponential is smooth in all real matrix coordinates, and

ddtexp⁡(tX)=Xexp⁡(tX)=exp⁡(tX)X. \frac d{dt}\exp(tX)=X\exp(tX)=\exp(tX)X. \tag{F4.13}
(F4.13)

Proof. For a fixed order p≥1p\geq1, differentiating XkX^k in pp specified directions inserts those pp direction matrices in distinct positions among its kk factors, in every order. There are k!/(k−p)!k!/(k-p)! terms when k≥pk\geq p, and zero otherwise. On ∥X∥F≤R\|X\|_F\leq R, their norms are bounded in sum by

k!(k−p)!Rk−p∏j=1p∥Hj∥F. \frac{k!}{(k-p)!}R^{k-p} \prod_{j=1}^{p}\|H_j\|_F. \tag{F4.14}
(F4.14)

Zero powers here mean that there are no remaining XX factors, not an extra identity factor with a silently changed norm. After division by k!k!, the scalar majorants sum to eR∏j∥Hj∥Fe^R\prod_j\|H_j\|_F. Thus every differentiated series converges uniformly on a bounded matrix ball.

For completeness, the elementary justification for differentiation of these limits is the fundamental theorem of calculus on a coordinate line: write the difference of each finite partial sum at two endpoints as the integral of its derivative. Uniform convergence lets both the endpoint values and that integral pass to the limit, since the integral of an error is bounded by interval length times its uniform bound. The resulting identity proves the claimed derivative. Repeating this argument for each derivative order proves smoothness. Differentiating (F4.11) with XX fixed and tt varying now gives (F4.13), with the index shift from k/k!k/k! to 1/(k−1)!1/(k-1)!. ▫\square

Smooth groups in matrix coordinates

An open set in a real coordinate space contains a ball about each of its points. A local coordinate chart on a subset of matrix space is a continuous bijection from an open set in ℝd\mathbb R^d onto a relatively open part of that subset, with continuous inverse. A smooth manifold is a Hausdorff space with a countable base of open sets and a covering by such charts whose coordinate transition maps are smooth. The charts we construct inherit the first two properties from finite-dimensional matrix space: distinct matrices have disjoint small balls, and balls with rational centres and radii give a countable base before restriction to the subset.

A real Lie group is a smooth manifold with a group operation such that multiplication and inversion are smooth in its charts. The next proof constructs those charts for the groups used here.

Define

U(n)={U∈Mn(ℂ):U†U=In},SU(n)={U∈U(n):det⁡U=1},O(n)={R∈Mn(ℝ):RTR=In},SO(n)={R∈O(n):det⁡R=1}. \begin{aligned} U(n)&=\{U\in M_n(\mathbb C):U^\dagger U=I_n\},\\ SU(n)&=\{U\in U(n):\det U=1\},\\ O(n)&=\{R\in M_n(\mathbb R):R^TR=I_n\},\\ SO(n)&=\{R\in O(n):\det R=1\}. \end{aligned} \tag{F4.23}
(F4.23)

These are groups: Lesson 3 proves the unitary and orthogonal assertions, and (F4.7) shows that determinant one is preserved by products and inverses. Their candidate coordinate spaces are the following real vector spaces:

𝔲(n)={X:X†=−X},𝔰𝔲(n)={X:X†=−X,tr⁡X=0},𝔰𝔬(n)={X∈Mn(ℝ):XT=−X}. \begin{aligned} \mathfrak u(n)&=\{X:X^\dagger=-X\},\\ \mathfrak{su}(n)&=\{X:X^\dagger=-X,\ \operatorname{tr}X=0\},\\ \mathfrak{so}(n)&=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb R):X^T=-X\}. \end{aligned} \tag{F4.24}
(F4.24)

The coefficients in their linear combinations are real. Multiplication by ii generally changes a nonzero skew-Hermitian matrix into a Hermitian matrix, so 𝔲(n)\mathfrak u(n) and 𝔰𝔲(n)\mathfrak{su}(n) are not being described as complex vector subspaces.

It remains to count real coordinates. In a skew-Hermitian matrix, each diagonal entry is one imaginary parameter and each pair of off-diagonal entries is determined by one arbitrary complex number.
