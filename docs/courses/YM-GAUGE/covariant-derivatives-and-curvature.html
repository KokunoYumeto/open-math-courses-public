Covariant derivatives and curvature

Lesson YM-F05 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

An ordinary derivative compares the entries of a field at nearby points. If the basis used for those entries changes from point to point, its derivative contributes too. A connection records the additional matrix needed to make differentiation respect the chosen change of basis.

We will construct this derivative, calculate its curvature, and prove the identities satisfied by that curvature. The calculation begins with matrices and the product rule. Differential forms will then organize the same complete expressions, including every sign and factor. The electromagnetic formulas of Lessons 2–3 will appear as an exact special case.

Preparation is Lesson 4, together with the field and coordinate calculus in Lessons 1–3. All functions in this lesson are smooth. Unless physical units are specified, UU is an open subset of ℝm\mathbb R^m with coordinates x1,…,xmx^1,\ldots,x^m. Fields take values in ℂN\mathbb C^N, and matrices act on column vectors from the left. Every sum below is written explicitly.

1. What a connection adds to a derivative

Choose mm smooth matrix functions Γμ:U→MN(ℂ)\Gamma_\mu:U\to M_N(\mathbb C). For a field ψ:U→ℂN\psi:U\to\mathbb C^N, define

Dμψ=∂μψ+Γμψ,DXψ=∑μ=1mXμDμψ,X=∑μ=1mXμ∂μ. D_\mu\psi=\partial_\mu\psi+\Gamma_\mu\psi, \qquad D_X\psi=\sum_{\mu=1}^m X^\mu D_\mu\psi, \quad X=\sum_{\mu=1}^m X^\mu\partial_\mu. \tag{F5.1}
(F5.1)

A vector field XX has real smooth coefficients. The operators are complex linear in ψ\psi, real linear in XX, and satisfy

DfXψ=fDXψ,DX(hψ)=(Xh)ψ+hDXψ. D_{fX}\psi=fD_X\psi,\qquad D_X(h\psi)=(Xh)\psi+hD_X\psi. \tag{F5.2}
(F5.2)

Here ff is real smooth and hh is complex smooth. The second identity is the Leibniz rule. It follows by differentiating the product and retaining the multiplication term Γμhψ\Gamma_\mu h\psi. A covariant derivative on this product field space means operators with precisely these linearity and Leibniz properties.

Conversely these properties determine matrices as in (F5.1). Let e1,…,eNe_1,\ldots,e_N be the constant coordinate columns. Make the jj-th column of Γμ\Gamma_\mu equal to D∂μejD_{\partial_\mu}e_j. Writing ψ=∑jψjej\psi=\sum_j\psi^j e_j, the Leibniz rule gives

D∂μψ=∑j=1N(∂μψj)ej+∑j=1NψjD∂μej=∂μψ+Γμψ. D_{\partial_\mu}\psi =\sum_{j=1}^N(\partial_\mu\psi^j)e_j +\sum_{j=1}^N\psi^jD_{\partial_\mu}e_j =\partial_\mu\psi+\Gamma_\mu\psi. \tag{F5.3}
(F5.3)

Linearity over smooth real functions in the direction gives (F5.1) for every XX. Thus the matrix description loses none of the stated operator data. The array Γμ\Gamma_\mu is the connection in this chosen frame.

Changing the field coordinates

Let g:U→GL⁡N(ℂ)g:U\to\operatorname{GL}_N(\mathbb C) be smooth, and declare the new field coordinates to be

ψ′=gψ. \psi'=g\psi. \tag{F5.4}
(F5.4)

The inverse is smooth by Lesson 4’s adjugate formula. We require the new derivative to describe the same operation in these coordinates: Dμ′(gψ)=gDμψD'_\mu(g\psi)=gD_\mu\psi for every smooth ψ\psi.

Theorem 1.1. The full connection transformation

The required covariance holds exactly when

Γμ′=gΓμg−1−(∂μg)g−1. \Gamma'_\mu =g\Gamma_\mu g^{-1}-(\partial_\mu g)g^{-1}. \tag{F5.5}
(F5.5)

Proof. Apply the product rule without combining its terms:

Dμ′(gψ)=(∂μg)ψ+g∂μψ+Γμ′gψ,gDμψ=g∂μψ+gΓμψ. \begin{aligned} D'_\mu(g\psi) &=(\partial_\mu g)\psi+g\partial_\mu\psi +\Gamma'_\mu g\psi,\\ gD_\mu\psi&=g\partial_\mu\psi+g\Gamma_\mu\psi. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.6}
(F5.6)

Equality for every constant test column ψ=ej\psi=e_j, at each point, is equivalent to ∂μg+Γμ′g=gΓμ\partial_\mu g+\Gamma'_\mu g=g\Gamma_\mu. Multiplication by g−1g^{-1} gives (F5.5). Substitution into (F5.6) proves sufficiency, for every field. ▫\square

This convention also specifies a passive frame change. If a geometric vector is written e(x)ψ(x)e(x)\psi(x), where e(x)e(x) is a row of basis vectors, the same vector in the frame e′=ehe'=eh has coordinates ψ′=h−1ψ\psi'=h^{-1}\psi. Set g=h−1g=h^{-1} in (F5.5). Differentiating h−1h=INh^{-1}h=I_N gives

∂μ(h−1)=−h−1(∂μh)h−1,Γμ′=h−1Γμh+h−1∂μh. \partial_\mu(h^{-1}) =-h^{-1}(\partial_\mu h)h^{-1},\qquad \Gamma'_\mu=h^{-1}\Gamma_\mu h+h^{-1}\partial_\mu h. \tag{F5.7}
(F5.7)

Thus the two commonly used signs describe the same map with inverse coordinate conventions. Hereafter (F5.4) fixes our convention.

These transformations form a group action. Write Γg\Gamma^g for (F5.5). A subsequent field change by hh is multiplication by hghg, and

(Γg)μh=hgΓμg−1h−1−h(∂μg)g−1h−1−(∂μh)h−1=hgΓμ(hg)−1−((∂μh)g+h(∂μg))(hg)−1=Γμhg. \begin{aligned} (\Gamma^g)^h_\mu &=hg\Gamma_\mu g^{-1}h^{-1} -h(\partial_\mu g)g^{-1}h^{-1} -(\partial_\mu h)h^{-1}\\ &=hg\Gamma_\mu(hg)^{-1} -\big((\partial_\mu h)g+h(\partial_\mu g)\big)(hg)^{-1} =\Gamma^{hg}_\mu. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.8}
(F5.8)

The identity acts trivially and g−1g^{-1} reverses the change. This also proves exactly which order of transformations is being used.

Preserving a fixed inner product

With ⟨u,v⟩=u†v\langle u,v\rangle=u^\dagger v, the derivative is compatible with that fixed inner product when

∂μ⟨ψ,η⟩=⟨Dμψ,η⟩+⟨ψ,Dμη⟩for all ψ,η. \partial_\mu\langle\psi,\eta\rangle =\langle D_\mu\psi,\eta\rangle+ \langle\psi,D_\mu\eta\rangle \quad\hbox{for all }\psi,\eta. \tag{F5.9}
(F5.9)

Expansion of the right side adds ψ†(Γμ†+Γμ)η\psi^\dagger(\Gamma_\mu^\dagger+\Gamma_\mu)\eta to the left side. Testing every pair of constant coordinate columns shows that (F5.9) holds exactly when Γμ†=−Γμ\Gamma_\mu^\dagger=-\Gamma_\mu.

If gg is unitary, its conjugation preserves skew-Hermitian matrices. Also differentiation of gg†=INgg^\dagger=I_N gives

((∂μg)g−1)†=g(∂μg†)=−(∂μg)g−1. \big((\partial_\mu g)g^{-1}\big)^\dagger =g(\partial_\mu g^\dagger) =-(\partial_\mu g)g^{-1}. \tag{F5.10}
(F5.10)

Thus (F5.5) preserves compatible connections. If g:U→SU(N)g:U\to SU(N), the determinant derivative proved in Lesson 4 gives tr⁡(g−1∂μg)=0\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}\partial_\mu g)=0. Cyclicity of trace shows that trace-zero connection matrices remain trace zero as well. We may therefore work with Γμ∈𝔲(N)\Gamma_\mu\in\mathfrak u(N) or 𝔰𝔲(N)\mathfrak{su}(N) and the corresponding group of frame changes. Real skew-symmetric matrices and orthogonal changes satisfy the same argument with transpose in place of adjoint.

2. Curvature is an actual commutator

Two coordinate covariant derivatives need not commute. Expand their composition on an arbitrary field:

DμDνψ=∂μ∂νψ+(∂μΓν)ψ+Γν∂μψ+Γμ∂νψ+ΓμΓνψ,DνDμψ=∂ν∂μψ+(∂νΓμ)ψ+Γμ∂νψ+Γν∂μψ+ΓνΓμψ. \begin{aligned} D_\mu D_\nu\psi &=\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\psi +(\partial_\mu\Gamma_\nu)\psi +\Gamma_\nu\partial_\mu\psi +\Gamma_\mu\partial_\nu\psi +\Gamma_\mu\Gamma_\nu\psi,\\ D_\nu D_\mu\psi &=\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\psi +(\partial_\nu\Gamma_\mu)\psi +\Gamma_\mu\partial_\nu\psi +\Gamma_\nu\partial_\mu\psi +\Gamma_\nu\Gamma_\mu\psi. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.11}
(F5.11)

Mixed partial derivatives agree for smooth functions, and the two first-derivative terms cancel in pairs. The result is multiplication by a matrix:

[Dμ,Dν]ψ=Fμνψ,Fμν=∂μΓν−∂νΓμ+[Γμ,Γν]. [D_\mu,D_\nu]\psi=F_{\mu\nu}\psi,\qquad F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma_\nu-\partial_\nu\Gamma_\mu +[\Gamma_\mu,\Gamma_\nu]. \tag{F5.12}
(F5.12)

This matrix is the curvature component. In particular Fνμ=−FμνF_{\nu\mu}=-F_{\mu\nu} and Fμμ=0F_{\mu\mu}=0. When the connection matrices lie in one of Lesson 4’s real matrix Lie algebras, derivatives and brackets remain in that same algebra. So does FμνF_{\mu\nu}.

Theorem 2.1. Curvature covariance

Under (F5.4)–(F5.5),

Fμν′=gFμνg−1. F'_{\mu\nu}=gF_{\mu\nu}g^{-1}. \tag{F5.13}
(F5.13)

Proof. Theorem 1.1 holds for every field, so it also holds when the input field is DνψD_\nu\psi. Apply it twice and subtract:

[Dμ′,Dν′](gψ)=Dμ′(gDνψ)−Dν′(gDμψ)=gDμDνψ−gDνDμψ=gFμνψ. \begin{aligned} [D'_\mu,D'_\nu](g\psi) &=D'_\mu(gD_\nu\psi)-D'_\nu(gD_\mu\psi)\\ &=gD_\mu D_\nu\psi-gD_\nu D_\mu\psi =gF_{\mu\nu}\psi. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.14}
(F5.14)

The left side equals Fμν′gψF'_{\mu\nu}g\psi by the full expansion (F5.11), now for the primed connection. Constant coordinate columns determine a matrix; hence Fμν′g=gFμνF'_{\mu\nu}g=gF_{\mu\nu}, proving (F5.13). No derivatives of the varying matrix gg have been discarded: their cancellations are precisely Theorem 1.1. ▫\square

For general directions, ordinary vector fields themselves need not commute. Their bracket is

[X,Y]ν=∑μ=1m(Xμ∂μYν−Yμ∂μXν). [X,Y]^\nu=\sum_{\mu=1}^m (X^\mu\partial_\mu Y^\nu-Y^\mu\partial_\mu X^\nu). \tag{F5.15}
(F5.15)

Expanding the two compositions using (F5.2) gives

DXDYψ=∑μ,ν=1mXμ(∂μYν)Dνψ+∑μ,ν=1mXμYνDμDνψ,DYDXψ=∑μ,ν=1mYμ(∂μXν)Dνψ+∑μ,ν=1mYμXνDμDνψ. \begin{aligned} D_XD_Y\psi &=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^m X^\mu(\partial_\mu Y^\nu)D_\nu\psi +\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^m X^\mu Y^\nu D_\mu D_\nu\psi,\\ D_YD_X\psi &=\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^m Y^\mu(\partial_\mu X^\nu)D_\nu\psi +\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^m Y^\mu X^\nu D_\mu D_\nu\psi. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.16}
(F5.16)

Subtract the first sums using (F5.15), and interchange the dummy indices in the second composition’s last sum. This proves

(DXDY−DYDX−D[X,Y])ψ=(∑μ,ν=1mXμYνFμν)ψ. (D_XD_Y-D_YD_X-D_{[X,Y]})\psi =\left(\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^m X^\mu Y^\nu F_{\mu\nu}\right)\psi. \tag{F5.17}
(F5.17)

The subtraction of D[X,Y]D_{[X,Y]} is essential: it removes the variation of the directions. The remaining expression is linear over smooth real functions in both directions and over smooth complex functions in ψ\psi.

3. Differential forms, with their coefficients retained

At x∈Ux\in U, a scalar pp-form is an alternating pp-linear function on (ℝm)p(\mathbb R^m)^p, varying smoothly with xx. A zero-form is a smooth function. The coordinate one-form dxμdx^\mu sends a vector vv to its component vμv^\mu. For a list of coordinate covectors, define their exterior product by

(dxi1∧⋯∧dxip)(v1,…,vp)=det⁡(vbia)a,b=1p. (dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p})(v_1,\ldots,v_p) =\det\big(v_b^{\,i_a}\big)_{a,b=1}^p. \tag{F5.18}
(F5.18)

For p=0p=0 the empty product is the scalar one. If αi1…ip=α(∂i1,…,∂ip)\alpha_{i_1\ldots i_p}=\alpha(\partial_{i_1},\ldots,\partial_{i_p}), multilinearity and alternation give both exact coordinate formulas

α=1p!∑i1,…,ip=1mαi1…ipdxi1∧⋯∧dxip=∑i1<⋯<ipαi1…ipdxi1∧⋯∧dxip. \alpha =\frac1{p!}\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_p=1}^m \alpha_{i_1\ldots i_p}\, dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p} =\sum_{i_1<\cdots<i_p} \alpha_{i_1\ldots i_p}\, dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p}. \tag{F5.19}
(F5.19)

To prove the first equality, evaluate on coordinate vectors. Repeated indices vanish by alternation. For any ordered list of distinct indices, the p!p! permutations each contribute the same coefficient: the sign of the coefficient permutation cancels the determinant sign. The factor 1/p!1/p! removes exactly these repetitions. Equality on the coordinate basis proves equality on all vectors. This argument also proves the second formula and uniqueness of its coefficients. For p>mp>m every form is zero.

For forms of degrees p,qp,q, define

(α∧β)(v1,…,vp+q)=1p!q!∑σ∈Sp+q(sgn⁡σ)α(vσ(1),…,vσ(p))β(vσ(p+1),…,vσ(p+q)). \begin{aligned} (\alpha\wedge\beta)(v_1,\ldots,v_{p+q}) =\frac1{p!q!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{p+q}} (\operatorname{sgn}\sigma)\, \alpha(v_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(p)}) \beta(v_{\sigma(p+1)},\ldots,v_{\sigma(p+q)}). \end{aligned} \tag{F5.20}
(F5.20)

This is the same as the sum over permutations that preserve order inside each of the two blocks: reordering within them gives p!q!p!q! identical signed contributions. This is the shuffle description of the product.

The product is associative. For three forms, either parenthesization of the shuffle sum chooses the same division of the input list into three ordered blocks, of lengths p,q,rp,q,r. Its sign is the sign of the full permutation, because successive permutations multiply their signs. Equivalently both parenthesizations equal the full permutation sum with coefficient 1/(p!q!r!)1/(p!q!r!). In degree-one coordinate products this is also the determinant expansion in (F5.18). Exchanging the two blocks in (F5.20) requires pqpq crossings, so scalar-valued forms satisfy β∧α=(−1)pqα∧β\beta\wedge\alpha=(-1)^{pq}\alpha\wedge\beta.

Matrix and vector coefficients

For matrix-valued forms use (F5.20) with matrix multiplication in the displayed order. Equivalently (α∧β)ab=∑c=1Nαac∧βcb(\alpha\wedge\beta)_{ab}=\sum_{c=1}^N \alpha_{ac}\wedge\beta_{cb}. For a matrix-valued form and a column-valued form the same rule uses matrix action on the column. Associativity follows from the same three-block argument and associativity of matrix multiplication. Matrix-valued forms generally do not satisfy scalar graded commutativity.

Their graded commutator is

[α,β]∧=α∧β−(−1)pqβ∧α. [\alpha,\beta]_\wedge =\alpha\wedge\beta-(-1)^{pq}\beta\wedge\alpha. \tag{F5.21}
(F5.21)

For scalar forms a,ba,b and constant matrices X,YX,Y,

[aX,bY]∧=(a∧b)(XY−YX). [aX,bY]_\wedge =(a\wedge b)(XY-YX). \tag{F5.22}
(F5.22)

Indeed the sign from moving bb past aa cancels the (−1)pq(-1)^{pq} in (F5.21). Expanding in a fixed real basis therefore shows that the bracket of Lie-algebra-valued forms is again Lie-algebra-valued. This also proves that the definition does not depend on the chosen basis: it is already an operation on the actual matrices.

The exterior derivative

Define exterior differentiation entry by entry:

dα=1p!∑μ,i1,…,ip=1m(∂μαi1…ip)dxμ∧dxi1∧⋯∧dxip. d\alpha =\frac1{p!}\sum_{\mu,i_1,\ldots,i_p=1}^m (\partial_\mu\alpha_{i_1\ldots i_p})\, dx^\mu\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_p}. \tag{F5.23}
(F5.23)

The full coefficient formula is

(dα)i0…ip=∑r=0p(−1)r∂irαi0…ir̂…ip. (d\alpha)_{i_0\ldots i_p} =\sum_{r=0}^p(-1)^r \partial_{i_r}\alpha_{i_0\ldots\widehat{i_r}\ldots i_p}. \tag{F5.24}
(F5.24)

The hat omits exactly that argument. To verify the formula, evaluate (F5.23) on the coordinate list. The derivative covector can occupy each of its p+1p+1 positions. Moving it to the front gives (−1)r(-1)^r; the remaining p!p! permutations cancel the factor in (F5.23). This proves every coefficient, including the cases with repeated indices.

The two identities used below are

d2α=0,d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)pα∧dβ. d^2\alpha=0,\qquad d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+ (-1)^p\alpha\wedge d\beta. \tag{F5.25}
(F5.25)

For the first, applying (F5.23) twice gives all terms (∂ν∂μαI)dxν∧dxμ∧dxI/p!(\partial_\nu\partial_\mu\alpha_I) dx^\nu\wedge dx^\mu\wedge dx^I/p!. Terms with μ=ν\mu=\nu vanish. The two terms indexed by (ν,μ)(\nu,\mu) and (μ,ν)(\mu,\nu) cancel because their partial derivatives agree and their first two covectors reverse sign. For the second, expand both forms in (F5.19), retaining their factors, and apply the ordinary coefficient product rule. The terms differentiating αI\alpha_I give dα∧βd\alpha\wedge\beta. For the others, moving the new dxμdx^\mu past the pp covectors of α\alpha contributes (−1)p(-1)^p, giving the second term. No matrix factors change order in either argument, so (F5.25) also holds for the matrix and vector products just defined.

4. The same connection and curvature as forms

Package the original connection matrices in a one-form and the original curvature matrices in a two-form:

Γ=∑μ=1mΓμdxμ,F=12∑μ,ν=1mFμνdxμ∧dxν=∑μ<νFμνdxμ∧dxν. \Gamma=\sum_{\mu=1}^m\Gamma_\mu dx^\mu,\qquad F=\frac12\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^mF_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu =\sum_{\mu<\nu}F_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu. \tag{F5.26}
(F5.26)

Both presentations of FF retain the same coefficients: the antisymmetry of FμνF_{\mu\nu} and of the coordinate wedge makes the two ordered terms equal. The diagonal terms are zero. From (F5.20),

(Γ∧Γ)(∂μ,∂ν)=ΓμΓν−ΓνΓμ,dΓ(∂μ,∂ν)=∂μΓν−∂νΓμ. \begin{aligned} (\Gamma\wedge\Gamma)(\partial_\mu,\partial_\nu) &=\Gamma_\mu\Gamma_\nu-\Gamma_\nu\Gamma_\mu,\\ d\Gamma(\partial_\mu,\partial_\nu) &=\partial_\mu\Gamma_\nu-\partial_\nu\Gamma_\mu. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.27}
(F5.27)

Consequently the exact form of (F5.12) is

F=dΓ+Γ∧Γ=dΓ+12[Γ,Γ]∧. F=d\Gamma+\Gamma\wedge\Gamma =d\Gamma+\frac12[\Gamma,\Gamma]_\wedge. \tag{F5.28}
(F5.28)

The last factor is essential: a one-form has odd degree, so [Γ,Γ]∧=2Γ∧Γ[\Gamma,\Gamma]_\wedge=2\Gamma\wedge\Gamma. The transformation laws become

Γg=gΓg−1−(dg)g−1,Fg=gFg−1. \Gamma^g=g\Gamma g^{-1}-(dg)g^{-1},\qquad F^g=gFg^{-1}. \tag{F5.29}
(F5.29)

Multiplication by a matrix-valued zero-form has no permutation sign. Evaluating these equalities on coordinate vectors recovers exactly (F5.5) and (F5.13).

Smooth maps of the coordinate domain

Let f:V→Uf:V\to U be smooth, with coordinates y1,…,yly^1,\ldots,y^l on the open set V⊂ℝlV\subset\mathbb R^l. Pullback of a form means

(f*α)y(v1,…,vp)=αf(y)(dfyv1,…,dfyvp). (f^*\alpha)_y(v_1,\ldots,v_p) =\alpha_{f(y)}(df_yv_1,\ldots,df_yv_p). \tag{F5.30}
(F5.30)

In coordinates this replaces every coefficient by its composition with ff, and every dxμdx^\mu by dfμ=∑a(∂afμ)dyadf^\mu=\sum_a(\partial_a f^\mu)dy^a. These rules follow directly from multilinearity in (F5.19), with the factor 1/p!1/p! unchanged. They imply

f*(α∧β)=f*α∧f*β,d(f*α)=f*(dα). f^*(\alpha\wedge\beta)=f^*\alpha\wedge f^*\beta,\qquad d(f^*\alpha)=f^*(d\alpha). \tag{F5.31}
(F5.31)

The first identity follows by inserting dfvjdf\,v_j in the full sum (F5.20). For the second, expand the coordinate rule just given. Differentiating a coefficient uses the chain rule d(a∘f)=∑μ(∂μa)∘fdfμd(a\circ f)=\sum_\mu(\partial_\mu a)\circ f\,df^\mu. Differentiating any factor dfμdf^\mu gives zero: its second partial derivatives cancel in antisymmetric pairs, the degree-zero case of (F5.25). The product rule now proves the second identity for each coordinate term and hence for their sum.

The pulled-back connection has coefficients

Γaf(y)=∑μ=1m(∂afμ)(y)Γμ(f(y)). \Gamma^f_a(y)=\sum_{\mu=1}^m (\partial_a f^\mu)(y)\Gamma_\mu(f(y)). \tag{F5.32}
(F5.32)

The chain rule verifies

Daf(ψ∘f)=∑μ=1m(∂afμ)(Dμψ)∘f. D^f_a(\psi\circ f) =\sum_{\mu=1}^m(\partial_a f^\mu)(D_\mu\psi)\circ f. \tag{F5.33}
(F5.33)

Applying (F5.28) and (F5.31) proves Ff=f*FF^f=f^*F, including the complete coefficient formula

Fabf=∑μ,ν=1m(∂afμ)(∂bfν)Fμν∘f. F^f_{ab} =\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^m (\partial_a f^\mu)(\partial_b f^\nu) F_{\mu\nu}\circ f. \tag{F5.34}
(F5.34)

No invertibility of ff was assumed. A coordinate diffeomorphism is one special case; an arbitrary smooth pullback has the same formula.

5. Covariant exterior derivatives and the Bianchi identity

For a column-valued pp-form η\eta, extend (F5.1) by

DΓη=dη+Γ∧η. D_\Gamma\eta=d\eta+\Gamma\wedge\eta. \tag{F5.35}
(F5.35)

The output is a column-valued (p+1)(p+1)-form. For a matrix-valued pp-form α\alpha, the corresponding derivative of endomorphisms is

𝒟Γα=dα+Γ∧α−(−1)pα∧Γ=dα+[Γ,α]∧. \mathcal D_\Gamma\alpha =d\alpha+\Gamma\wedge\alpha-(-1)^p\alpha\wedge\Gamma =d\alpha+[\Gamma,\alpha]_\wedge. \tag{F5.36}
(F5.36)

These are different actions of the same connection on specified spaces. For example, at degree zero, η\eta is a column field, while a matrix field KK acts on such columns. Direct expansion proves the exact compatibility

Dμ(Kψ)=(∂μK+[Γμ,K])ψ+KDμψ. D_\mu(K\psi) =\big(\partial_\mu K+[\Gamma_\mu,K]\big)\psi+KD_\mu\psi. \tag{F5.37}
(F5.37)

Indeed its right side is (∂μK)ψ+ΓμKψ−KΓμψ+K∂μψ+KΓμψ(\partial_\mu K)\psi+\Gamma_\mu K\psi-K\Gamma_\mu\psi+ K\partial_\mu\psi+K\Gamma_\mu\psi, with the last two connection terms of opposite signs cancelling. Thus the endomorphism derivative is forced by the action on the original fields.

The full graded versions follow from (F5.25):

DΓ(α∧η)=(𝒟Γα)∧η+(−1)pα∧DΓη,𝒟Γ(α∧β)=(𝒟Γα)∧β+(−1)pα∧𝒟Γβ. \begin{aligned} D_\Gamma(\alpha\wedge\eta) &=(\mathcal D_\Gamma\alpha)\wedge\eta +(-1)^p\alpha\wedge D_\Gamma\eta,\\ \mathcal D_\Gamma(\alpha\wedge\beta) &=(\mathcal D_\Gamma\alpha)\wedge\beta +(-1)^p\alpha\wedge\mathcal D_\Gamma\beta. \end{aligned} \tag{F5.38}
(F5.38)

Here β\beta is another matrix-valued form. To check the second formula, substitute (F5.36). The two inserted middle products −(−1)pα∧Γ∧β-(-1)^p\alpha\wedge\Gamma\wedge\beta and (−1)pα∧Γ∧β( -1)^p\alpha\wedge\Gamma\wedge\beta cancel.
