The Yang–Mills action and field equations

Lesson YM-F07 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

A connection tells us how to differentiate a field. An action assigns a real number to a specified field configuration on a specified region. Its first variation tests whether changing that configuration changes the action to first order. The Yang–Mills equations are the resulting stationarity equations.

We will derive them, including the boundary contribution. We will also derive the equations for a complex scalar field coupled to the connection. The preparation is Lesson 5 and Lesson 6, together with their earlier calculus and matrix material.

1. The data in an action

Use four coordinates x0,x1,x2,x3x^0,x^1,x^2,x^3 on an open region U⊂ℝ4U\subset\mathbb R^4. A metric is a smooth real symmetric invertible matrix gμν(x)g_{\mu\nu}(x) specifying g(v,w)=∑μ,ν=03gμνvμwνg(v,w)=\sum_{\mu,\nu=0}^3g_{\mu\nu}v^\mu w^\nu. Its inverse has entries gμνg^{\mu\nu}. We will use either a positive definite metric, called Euclidean signature, or a metric with one negative and three positive directions, called Lorentzian signature. Here and below a repeated coordinate index is summed from 00 to 33. The sums in formulas containing repeated indices are complete ordered sums.

Define

w=|det⁡(gμν)|,Fμν=gμρgνσFρσ,σ={+1,Euclidean signature,−1,Lorentzian signature. w=\sqrt{|\det(g_{\mu\nu})|},\qquad F^{\mu\nu}=g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma},\qquad \sigma= \begin{cases} +1,&\text{Euclidean signature},\\ -1,&\text{Lorentzian signature}. \end{cases} \tag{F7.1}
(F7.1)

Smoothness of the inverse follows from its cofactor formula and the nonzero determinant. The positive function ww is smooth for the same reason. In particular, differentiating a raised tensor must differentiate the inverse metric too.

Take a specified matrix Lie group G⊂U(N)G\subset U(N), such as U(N)U(N), SU(N)SU(N), or the real orthogonal groups regarded inside U(N)U(N). Its real Lie algebra 𝔤\mathfrak g consists of the corresponding skew-Hermitian matrices. We use the original defining trace pairing

κ(X,Y)=−tr⁡(XY),X,Y∈𝔤. \kappa(X,Y)=-\operatorname{tr}(XY),\qquad X,Y\in\mathfrak g. \tag{F7.2}
(F7.2)

It is real: complex conjugation of tr⁡(XY)\operatorname{tr}(XY) gives tr⁡(Y†X†)=tr⁡(YX)\operatorname{tr}(Y^\dagger X^\dagger)=\operatorname{tr}(YX). It is symmetric by trace cyclicity, and κ(X,X)=∑j,k|Xjk|2\kappa(X,X)=\sum_{j,k}|X_{jk}|^2, positive unless X=0X=0. The same cyclicity proves both

κ(hXh−1,hYh−1)=κ(X,Y),κ([Z,X],Y)+κ(X,[Z,Y])=0. \kappa(hXh^{-1},hYh^{-1})=\kappa(X,Y),\qquad \kappa([Z,X],Y)+\kappa(X,[Z,Y])=0. \tag{F7.3}
(F7.3)

For the second formula the two expanded traces contain ZXYZXY and XYZXYZ with opposite signs after cyclic permutation; the remaining XZYXZY terms cancel directly. These are the precise invariance properties needed in the variation.

A connection is the one from the earlier lessons:

Dμψ=∂μψ+Γμψ,Fμν=∂μΓν−∂νΓμ+[Γμ,Γν],𝒟μX=∂μX+[Γμ,X]. D_\mu\psi=\partial_\mu\psi+\Gamma_\mu\psi,\qquad F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma_\nu-\partial_\nu\Gamma_\mu +[\Gamma_\mu,\Gamma_\nu],\qquad \mathcal D_\mu X=\partial_\mu X+[\Gamma_\mu,X]. \tag{F7.4}
(F7.4)

Here Γμ,Fμν,X\Gamma_\mu,F_{\mu\nu},X take values in 𝔤\mathfrak g. The full curvature form is F=12Fμνdxμ∧dxνF=\tfrac12F_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu. The factor 1/21/2 removes the double counting in that ordered sum; it does not remove either occurrence from later tensor contractions.

Let gYM≠0g_{\mathrm{YM}}\ne0 be a fixed real coupling and ℏ>0\hbar>0 the action unit. On a closed coordinate box Q=∏μ=03[aμ,bμ]Q=\prod_{\mu=0}^3[a_\mu,b_\mu] inside UU, define

SYM,Q[Γ]=σℏ2gYM2∫Qwκ(Fμν,Fμν)d4x. S_{\mathrm{YM},Q}[\Gamma] =\frac{\sigma\hbar}{2g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \int_Q w\,\kappa(F_{\mu\nu},F^{\mu\nu})\,d^4x. \tag{F7.5}
(F7.5)

Fields and metrics are smooth on a neighbourhood of the box. Thus this integral and every variation used below exist. On an unbounded region we use this same action when its integral converges. Local field equations also make sense without a finite total action: for a compactly supported variation the difference of actions is an integral over a compact set, even when the separate infinite-volume actions have no finite value. No subtraction of two infinite numbers is involved.

If all four coordinates have length units, gμνg_{\mu\nu} is dimensionless, Γμ\Gamma_\mu has unit length−1^{-1}, and FμνF_{\mu\nu} has unit length−2^{-2}. In four dimensions a dimensionless gYMg_{\mathrm{YM}} makes (F7.5) have the unit of ℏ\hbar. Physical time will be kept explicitly in Section 7.

2. Coordinate changes and the integration measure

Let x=f(y)x=f(y) be a smooth coordinate change with smooth inverse. Put Jμa=∂xμ/∂yaJ^\mu{}_a=\partial x^\mu/\partial y^a and Kaμ=∂ya/∂xμK^a{}_\mu=\partial y^a/\partial x^\mu, so KJ=JK=I4KJ=JK=I_4. Pullback, already proved for forms in F05, gives the exact data

(F7.6)

The inverse-metric identity follows by multiplying the first two matrices. The determinant identity follows from det⁡(JTgJ)=(det⁡J)2det⁡g\det(J^TgJ)=(\det J)^2\det g. Contracting both curvature indices cancels the four inverse-Jacobian factors in pairs:

w̃κ(F̃ab,F̃ab)=|det⁡J|[wκ(Fμν,Fμν)]∘f. \widetilde w\,\kappa(\widetilde F_{ab},\widetilde F^{ab}) =|\det J|\,\bigl[w\kappa(F_{\mu\nu},F^{\mu\nu})\bigr]\circ f. \tag{F7.7}
(F7.7)

This identifies the actual integration density, including an orientation-reversing coordinate change. We use positive coordinate volume, hence the absolute determinant.

The change-of-variables fact used here

For completeness, if ff is a C1C^1 diffeomorphism on a neighbourhood of a compact box B⊂ℝnB\subset\mathbb R^n, and hh is continuous on f(B)f(B), then

∫f(B)h(x)dnx=∫Bh(f(y))|det⁡Df(y)|dny. \int_{f(B)}h(x)\,d^nx =\int_B h(f(y))\,|\det Df(y)|\,d^ny. \tag{F7.8}
(F7.8)

Here integrals over bounded regions are Riemann integrals, obtained by upper and lower box sums; boundary sets of zero box volume do not change them. The following argument supplies the needed fact, rather than assuming that a nonlinear map has a constant Jacobian.

Subdivide BB into boxes with side lengths proportional to a mesh size dd, using fixed coordinate proportions. On a small box CC with centre zz, write f(y)=f(z)+A(y−z)+r(y)f(y)=f(z)+A(y-z)+r(y), A=Df(z)A=Df(z). The fundamental theorem along line segments gives ∥r(y)−r(y′)∥≤ω(d)∥y−y′∥\|r(y)-r(y')\|\leq\omega(d)\|y-y'\|, where ω(d)→0\omega(d)\to0 uniformly over all boxes, by uniform continuity of DfDf. Inverses A−1A^{-1} have a uniform bound on BB, by continuity and compactness.

In the original box coordinates, A−1rA^{-1}r therefore moves each point by at most ϵ(d)d\epsilon(d)d, with ϵ(d)→0\epsilon(d)\to0. Consequently f(C)f(C) is contained in the affine image of the box enlarged by that amount in each coordinate. It contains the affine image of the correspondingly shrunken box. For this last inclusion, if y0y_0 lies in the shrunken box, solve y=y0−A−1r(y)y=y_0-A^{-1}r(y). The right side maps CC into itself and has Lipschitz constant less than 1/21/2 for small enough dd. Iteration converges: successive differences are bounded by a geometric series, the closed box contains the limit, and continuity makes it a fixed point. Its image under ff is f(z)+A(y0−z)f(z)+A(y_0-z).

An invertible linear map multiplies box volume by |det⁡A||\det A|. This follows by decomposing it through Gaussian elimination into coordinate interchanges, nonzero coordinate dilations and shears. Their factors are respectively 11, the absolute dilation factor, and 11. A shear preserves volume by integrating the translated one-dimensional slices; repeated integration gives the result in dimension nn. The same slicing proves the volume statement for the intervening parallelepipeds. The enlarged and shrunken boxes thus bound the volume of f(C)f(C) by |det⁡A|vol⁡(C)|\det A|\operatorname{vol}(C) with uniform error at most C0ϵ(d)dnC_0\epsilon(d)d^n.

The regions used here have Riemann volume. A C1C^1 map on the compact neighbourhood is Lipschitz. Cover a box face by O(δ−(n−1))O(\delta^{-(n-1)}) face boxes of diameter O(δ)O(\delta). Their images fit in ordinary boxes of total volume O(δ)O(\delta). Hence the image of every face has zero box volume. A homeomorphism takes the boundary of CC to that of f(C)f(C); its interior maps to the interior. The image region’s boundary therefore has zero volume, as required for upper and lower Riemann sums.

The images of the small-box interiors are disjoint and their union misses only those zero-volume boundaries. Uniform continuity of hh makes its oscillation on each image tend uniformly to zero. The integral over each image equals h(f(z))|det⁡Df(z)|vol⁡(C)h(f(z))|\det Df(z)|\operatorname{vol}(C), up to an error whose sum tends to zero: the volume error above has O(d−n)O(d^{-n}) terms, and the oscillation error is bounded by the total image volume times the maximum oscillation. Their sum is the Riemann sum on the right of (F7.8). This proves the formula.

Applying (F7.8) to (F7.7) proves invariance of the action under the specified coordinate change and corresponding change of region. For a compactly supported smooth density on a manifold, cover its support by finitely many charts and use the smooth weights constructed in F06. The densities agree on overlaps, and inserting those weights gives compactly supported chart integrands, which can be integrated on containing coordinate boxes. Independence of the weights follows by inserting their pairwise products and using (F7.8) on the overlaps. The same construction works on compact regions whose chart boundaries have zero coordinate volume, including piecewise smooth boundaries. Thus no global bundle frame is required; no Riemann-integrability claim is made for an arbitrary irregular compact region.

3. Varying a connection

A difference of connections is an adjoint-bundle-valued one-form, as proved in F06. Thus a valid variation is Γ(s)=Γ+sα\Gamma(s)=\Gamma+s\alpha, with the same homogeneous overlap law for α\alpha. The metric, coupling, region and ℏ\hbar are held fixed. Direct expansion, keeping the quadratic term, gives

Fμν(s)=Fμν+sBμν+s2Cμν,Bμν=𝒟μαν−𝒟ναμ,Cμν=[αμ,αν],Fμν′(0)=Bμν,Fμν″(0)=2Cμν. \begin{aligned} F_{\mu\nu}(s)&=F_{\mu\nu}+sB_{\mu\nu}+s^2C_{\mu\nu},\\ B_{\mu\nu}&=\mathcal D_\mu\alpha_\nu-\mathcal D_\nu\alpha_\mu,\\ C_{\mu\nu}&=[\alpha_\mu,\alpha_\nu],\\ F'_{\mu\nu}(0)&=B_{\mu\nu},\qquad F''_{\mu\nu}(0)=2C_{\mu\nu}. \end{aligned} \tag{F7.10}
(F7.10)

The contraction κ(Xμν,Yμν)\kappa(X_{\mu\nu},Y^{\mu\nu}) is symmetric in X,YX,Y: exchange the two pairs of summed indices, using the symmetry of gμνg^{\mu\nu} and of κ\kappa. Therefore

SYM,Q′(0)=σℏgYM2∫Qwκ(Bμν,Fμν)d4x=2σℏgYM2∫Qwκ(𝒟μαν,Fμν)d4x. \begin{aligned} S'_{\mathrm{YM},Q}(0) &=\frac{\sigma\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \int_Q w\,\kappa(B_{\mu\nu},F^{\mu\nu})\,d^4x\\ &=\frac{2\sigma\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \int_Q w\,\kappa(\mathcal D_\mu\alpha_\nu,F^{\mu\nu})\,d^4x. \end{aligned} \tag{F7.11}
(F7.11)

The second line relabels μ,ν\mu,\nu in the second term of BB and uses Fνμ=−FμνF^{\nu\mu}=-F^{\mu\nu}. Its factor 22 is essential. Differentiation under the integral is justified here without a limiting argument: the integrand is a polynomial of degree at most four in ss, with smooth coefficients on a compact set.

Trace invariance (F7.3) also gives the exact product rule

∂μ[wκ(αν,Fμν)]=wκ(𝒟μαν,Fμν)+κ(αν,𝒟μ(wFμν)). \partial_\mu\!\left[w\,\kappa(\alpha_\nu,F^{\mu\nu})\right] =w\,\kappa(\mathcal D_\mu\alpha_\nu,F^{\mu\nu}) +\kappa\!\left(\alpha_\nu, \mathcal D_\mu(wF^{\mu\nu})\right). \tag{F7.12}
(F7.12)

Indeed the commutator terms on the right sum to zero, leaving the ordinary product derivative, including ∂μw\partial_\mu w and the derivatives of both inverse-metric factors in FμνF^{\mu\nu}.

4. Why stationarity gives the field equations

We first prove the required fundamental lemma. Suppose YνY^\nu is a continuous 𝔤\mathfrak g-valued field and

∫Qwκ(αν,Yν)d4x=0for every smooth α supported in the interior. \int_Qw\,\kappa(\alpha_\nu,Y^\nu)\,d^4x=0 \quad\text{for every smooth \(\alpha\) supported in the interior.} \tag{F7.16}
(F7.16)

If Yν(p)≠0Y^\nu(p)\ne0 for one index and interior point, put X=Yν(p)X=Y^\nu(p). Continuity makes κ(X,Yν(x))>0\kappa(X,Y^\nu(x))>0 in a sufficiently small ball about pp. The smooth bump β(x)=exp⁡[−1/(r2−|x−p|2)2]\beta(x)=\exp[-1/(r^2-|x-p|^2)^2] inside that ball and zero outside is nonnegative and positive inside. Its smooth zero extension was proved in F06. Set that one component αν=βX\alpha_\nu=\beta X, and all others zero. Then the integral is strictly positive: w>0w>0, and on a smaller closed ball the continuous integrand has a positive lower bound. This contradicts (F7.16). Hence every Yν=0Y^\nu=0 in the interior. The converse is immediate.

Theorem 4.1. The Yang–Mills variational equation

A smooth connection is stationary for every compactly supported interior variation if and only if

𝒟μ(wFμν)=0(ν=0,1,2,3),equivalentlyℰν=0. \mathcal D_\mu(wF^{\mu\nu})=0\quad(\nu=0,1,2,3), \quad\text{equivalently}\quad \mathcal E^\nu=0. \tag{F7.17}
(F7.17)

Proof. Such variations have zero boundary term in (F7.15). The coefficient 2σℏ/gYM22\sigma\hbar/g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2 is nonzero. The fundamental lemma gives ℰν=0\mathcal E^\nu=0. Conversely this equation and the vanishing boundary term give zero first variation. ▫\square

For a constant metric ww is constant, so this becomes 𝒟μFμν=0\mathcal D_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0. For a varying metric the derivatives in (F7.14) remain. One cannot replace (F7.17) by an equation that discards them.

At the boundary there are several actual variational problems.

  • Fixed connection on the boundary. Require αν=0\alpha_\nu=0 there. Then solutions of (F7.17) are stationary for all those variations.
  • Fixed tangential connection on each face. On a face xμ=constantx^\mu=\text{constant}, require αν=0\alpha_\nu=0 for ν≠μ\nu\ne\mu. The remaining term is zero because Fμμ=0F^{\mu\mu}=0. Thus fixing the pullback of the connection suffices.
  • Free connection on the boundary. Arbitrary variations on the relative interior of a face require Fμν=0F^{\mu\nu}=0 there for every ν\nu, where μ\mu is that face’s fixed index.

For necessity of the last condition, after imposing the interior equations use a bump supported near one face, away from its edges, with arbitrary smooth tangential boundary values. A product of a tangential bump and a normal function equal to one at the face extends those values smoothly into the box. The same positivity argument proves each face coefficient wFμν=0wF^{\mu\nu}=0. Continuity extends it to the face edges. Conversely these face conditions kill (F7.15) for every variation. Corners have zero face measure and create no additional term in (F7.13).

Mixed problems fix selected boundary components and require the coefficient of each remaining freely varied component to vanish. This follows component by component from the displayed pairing. Choosing a boundary condition is part of defining the problem, not a term to erase during differentiation.

Covariance of the equation

For an adjoint field Xu=uXu−1X^u=uXu^{-1}, the full product rule in F05 gives 𝒟μuXu=u(𝒟μX)u−1\mathcal D^u_\mu X^u=u(\mathcal D_\mu X)u^{-1}. Consequently

The fundamental lemma proves (F7.19). The coordinate and gauge maps thus identify the same equation on actual overlaps of a general bundle.

5. Coupling constants and complete component equations

Keep F05’s exact basis TaT_a of 𝔤\mathfrak g, with [Ta,Tb]=fabcTc[T_a,T_b]=f_{ab}{}^cT_c, and write κab=−tr⁡(TaTb)\kappa_{ab}=-\operatorname{tr}(T_aT_b).
