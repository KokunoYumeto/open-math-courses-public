# Yang–Mills theory: a route from the foundations

This course starts with fields, electromagnetism and symmetry, then develops
the geometry, dynamics and quantum questions of Yang–Mills theory. The aim is
to understand the subject well enough to read its literature critically.
The lesson sequence follows prerequisites and established subject coverage.

Start with basic algebra, elementary calculus and matrix arithmetic. The
opening unit includes a preparatory review; later geometry, analytical and
quantum prerequisites enter when their purpose is visible. A student does
not need to arrive knowing fibre bundles or quantum field theory.

The principal setting is ordinary Yang–Mills theory for compact gauge groups.
U(1), SU(2) and SU(3) provide concrete examples. Pure Yang–Mills theory is
distinguished from theories with matter, and Lorentzian evolution is
distinguished from Euclidean field theory. Every detailed calculation will
state its group, dimension, metric, coupling conventions and boundaries.

## The lesson sequence

The sequence has eighteen units. [Fields, coordinates and physical
quantities](../fields-coordinates-quantities.html),
[Electromagnetism and gauge freedom](../electromagnetism-gauge-freedom.html),
[Symmetry through matrices](../symmetry-through-matrices.html),
[Lie groups and Lie algebras](../lie-groups-and-lie-algebras.html),
[Covariant derivatives and curvature](../covariant-derivatives-and-curvature.html),
[Bundles, parallel transport and holonomy](../bundles-transport-holonomy.html),
[The Yang–Mills action and field equations](../action-and-field-equations.html), and
[Constraints, initial data and energy](../constraints-initial-data-energy.html)
are complete, each with eight solved exercises. The remaining ten
units are being written.
The four earlier standalone texts remain accessible separately, with their
original proofs and dependencies.


### Starting points

**1. Fields, coordinates and physical quantities.** Scalar and vector fields; derivatives and integrals; spacetime; units; differential equations and initial data.

After this lesson: Read a field equation and identify its unknowns, coordinates, dimensions and initial data.

**2. Electromagnetism and gauge freedom.** Electric and magnetic fields; scalar and vector potentials; Maxwell equations; local changes of potential; observables.

After this lesson: Calculate a gauge change and identify the quantities it preserves.

**3. Symmetry through matrices.** Complex numbers and phases; rotations; matrix multiplication; unitary matrices; representations; global and local symmetry.

After this lesson: Use explicit matrices to distinguish a symmetry action from a choice of local description.

### Gauge geometry

**4. Lie groups and Lie algebras.** U(1), SU(2) and SU(3); tangent generators; commutators; exponential maps; invariant inner products.

After this lesson: Calculate the Lie bracket in concrete examples and identify the group acting on a field.

**5. Covariant derivatives and curvature.** Differentiating fields under local changes of frame; connection matrices; curvature; gauge covariance; Bianchi identity.

After this lesson: Derive local transformation laws and calculate the entire curvature of a specified connection.

**6. Bundles, parallel transport and holonomy.** Local trivializations and transition functions; principal and associated bundles; transport along paths; holonomy; Wilson loops.

After this lesson: Relate local formulas to a global connection and construct gauge-invariant loop observables.

### Classical dynamics

**7. The Yang-Mills action and field equations.** Variational principles; invariant metrics; coupling constants; Euler-Lagrange equations; boundary terms; pure theory and coupling to matter.

After this lesson: Derive the field equations from the stated action while retaining the group, signature, units and boundary assumptions.

**8. Constraints, initial data and energy.** Temporal and other gauges; Gauss constraint; admissible Cauchy data; stress-energy; conservation and flux.

After this lesson: Identify valid initial data and derive the corresponding energy and constraint identities.

### Classical analysis and topology

**9. Local and global classical evolution.** Fourier and Sobolev preparation; local well-posedness; gauge control; finite propagation; finite-energy global results in 3+1 dimensions.

After this lesson: State a classical existence theorem with its exact hypotheses and understand the estimates that enter its proof.

**10. Euclidean Yang-Mills and instantons.** Euclidean signature; self-duality; topological charge; energy bounds; instantons and moduli; the relation to four-manifold geometry.

After this lesson: Recognize the different analytical questions posed by elliptic Euclidean equations and Lorentzian time evolution.

### Quantum foundations

**11. From oscillators to quantum fields.** Hilbert spaces; observables; self-adjoint Hamiltonians; oscillators; free fields; distributions; vacuum and correlation functions.

After this lesson: Distinguish a classical field configuration from a quantum state and calculate basic spectral and correlation examples.

**12. Gauge symmetry in the quantum theory.** Physical states and constraints; gauge fixing; perturbative path integrals; Faddeev-Popov ghosts; Ward identities; an introduction to BRST.

After this lesson: Identify the physical quantities a quantization procedure must preserve and the scope of a perturbative calculation.

### Quantum scales

**13. Renormalization and asymptotic freedom.** Regularization; renormalized parameters; scale dependence; beta functions; asymptotic freedom; the limits of perturbation theory.

After this lesson: Follow the relation between regulator parameters and physical scales and state what a perturbative result establishes.

### Lattice theory and observables

**14. Yang-Mills theory on a lattice.** Links and plaquettes; compact-group Haar integration; Wilson action; gauge-invariant observables; transfer matrices and Hamiltonian descriptions.

After this lesson: Construct a finite regulator model and track the relation between its variables and continuum geometry.

**15. Strong coupling, simulations and their errors.** Strong-coupling methods; Monte Carlo sampling; lattice spacing; finite volume; statistical and systematic errors; continuum extrapolation.

After this lesson: Read a lattice result with its regulator, error analysis and applicable regime intact.

**16. Spectra, mass gaps and confinement.** Vacuum subtraction; spectral measures; correlation decay; mass extraction; Wilson-loop criteria; the differences between gap and confinement questions.

After this lesson: State precisely which observable and limiting procedure a spectral or confinement claim concerns.

### Continuum theory and the literature

**17. Continuum limits and reconstruction.** Infinite volume and removal of the ultraviolet regulator; correlation distributions; positivity; locality; Euclidean and Wightman frameworks; reconstruction.

After this lesson: List the data and estimates needed to turn a regulated calculation into a continuum quantum theory.

**18. The established research landscape and open problems.** Classical PDE, geometric gauge theory, perturbative results, lattice evidence and constructive approaches; lower-dimensional comparisons; the four-dimensional existence and mass-gap problem.

After this lesson: Place a paper in the literature by its exact model, dimension, assumptions, proof status and contribution.

## How this relates to the research literature

The classical, geometric, perturbative, lattice and constructive questions
each receive attention because they are parts of the subject. A particular
research project does not set the destination or supply a preferred answer.
The course will identify proved theorems, controlled approximations,
computational evidence and conjectures at the point where each appears.

The four-dimensional quantum existence and mass-gap problem remains listed
as unresolved by the [Clay Mathematics Institute](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/yang-mills-the-maths-gap/).
The final units teach its precise formulation and the relation of existing
results to it. Classical smooth solutions, finite lattice spectra and a
continuum quantum theory require different statements and arguments; the
course will construct their actual mathematical connections.

Every source is assessed by the same questions: Is its claim reliable at the
stated scope? Is it relevant here? Does it teach something the existing
components do not already teach as well? What preparation does it require?
How much space does its contribution justify? A result may merit a short
example rather than a lesson, or no inclusion at all.

No workbench result has a reserved place, including in optional reading.
The previously written treatments are retained for access and possible
component reuse. Their availability is not a curriculum-selection decision.

## Existing components to examine first

The course library already contains **RT-LIE**, on Lie algebras, and
**DG-FND**, on bundles, connections and holonomy. These are concrete starting
points for matching the prerequisites of Units 3–6. Compact-group
representation theory and the analysis courses are further routes for the
later units. Exact statements, hypotheses, proofs and current editions must
be checked before a component is adopted. This plan does not label an
unread provider as a completed prerequisite.

The next writing task is Unit 9: local and global classical evolution.
Units 1–8 provide its calculus, symmetry, geometry, field equations and energy estimates.
The general sequence remains independent of the previous workbench family.
The later lessons will build from suitable existing components and complete
the missing connections, preserving full mathematical statements and proofs.

## Representative reading routes

This initial map locates established treatments and the official open
problem; it is not an exhaustive survey. A teaching plan does not certify
every theorem in a cited work.


- Andriy Haydys, [Introduction to gauge theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10436v1). Geometric background: bundles, connections, gauge transformations and characteristic classes.

- Sung-Jin Oh, [Finite energy global well-posedness of the Yang-Mills equations on R^(1+3): An approach using the Yang-Mills heat flow](https://arxiv.org/abs/1210.1557v2). Classical PDE route, alongside the earlier Klainerman-Machedon result identified by Oh.

- Rajan Gupta, [Introduction to Lattice QCD](https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-lat/9807028v1). Established lattice methods and their error analysis. QCD with matter is distinguished from pure Yang-Mills theory.

- David J. Gross and Frank Wilczek, [Ultraviolet Behavior of Non-Abelian Gauge Theories](https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.30.1343). Primary historical route for asymptotic freedom; the independent Politzer route also belongs in the eventual detailed treatment.

- Clay Mathematics Institute; official problem description by Arthur Jaffe and Edward Witten, [Yang-Mills and the Mass Gap](https://www.claymath.org/millennium/yang-mills-the-maths-gap/). Official problem scope and current unresolved status.


- C. N. Yang and R. L. Mills, [Conservation of Isotopic Spin and Isotopic Gauge Invariance](https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.96.191). Historical primary source for non-Abelian gauge fields.
- Kenneth G. Wilson, [Confinement of quarks](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.10.2445). Historical primary route to lattice gauge theory and its strong-coupling regime.

The exact scope of the present source reading is recorded in [the literature map](../literature-map.json). No protected source exposition is reproduced in this plan.
