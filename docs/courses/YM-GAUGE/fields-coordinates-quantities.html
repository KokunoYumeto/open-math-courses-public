Fields, coordinates and physical quantities

Lesson YM-F01 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

A temperature map assigns a temperature to each place. A wind map assigns both a speed and a direction. If either map changes, time becomes one of its inputs. These are examples of fields.

The first task in reading a field theory is to identify its fields: what their inputs are, what kind of values they take, and what those values mean. The next task is to distinguish the field from the equation it is supposed to satisfy. We will do both with explicit examples.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to specify a scalar or vector field, keep track of its units, differentiate it, change its Cartesian coordinates, and solve a first evolution equation from its initial data. The final example also gives a precise instance of propagation and a local conservation law.

1. Preparation and notation

We use real numbers, arithmetic with triples, and elementary one-variable calculus: derivatives of polynomials, exponentials, sine and cosine, the one-variable chain rule, and substitution in definite integrals. In particular, we use the product rule and the fundamental theorem of calculus: if ff has a continuous derivative on an interval, then

f(b)−f(a)=∫abf′(s)ds. f(b)-f(a)=\int_a^b f'(s)\,ds. \tag{F1.1}
(F1.1)

We will derive the multivariable differentiation and transport formulas used below. You do not need prior knowledge of gauge theory, bundles, Hilbert spaces or quantum mechanics.

Write a point of Cartesian space as

x=(x1,x2,x3)∈ℝ3. x=(x^1,x^2,x^3)\in\mathbb R^3. \tag{F1.2}
(F1.2)

The superscripts label the three coordinates; they are not powers. We write x1x1=(x1)2x^1x^1=(x^1)^2 when a square is intended. A spacetime point in this lesson is a pair (t,x)(t,x). We keep time and the three spatial coordinates separate. This notation does not yet choose a spacetime metric or make any claim about relativistic dynamics.

Let II be an open interval of time and let Ω\Omega be an open subset of ℝ3\mathbb R^3. A real scalar field is a function

u:I×Ω→ℝ. u:I\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R. \tag{F1.3}
(F1.3)

A spatial vector field in these Cartesian coordinates is a function

V:I×Ω→ℝ3,V(t,x)=(V1(t,x),V2(t,x),V3(t,x)). V:I\times\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R^3,\qquad V(t,x)=\bigl(V^1(t,x),V^2(t,x),V^3(t,x)\bigr). \tag{F1.4}
(F1.4)

For physical quantities, the real numbers in these formulas are numerical values in specified units. A temperature and a mass density can both be represented by real-valued functions, but they are different physical quantities. Their units and meanings belong to the specification.

Not every triple in a field theory is a spatial vector. Three chemical concentrations, for example, form three scalar fields. A spatial rotation rotates the components of a velocity vector; it does not turn one chemical species into another. We will make this distinction concrete in Section 5.

2. Reading a field at a point

Choose constants ω\omega and vv, each independent of tt and xx. The field

V(t,x)=(−ωx2,ωx1,0) V(t,x)=(-\omega x^2,\ \omega x^1,\ 0) \tag{F1.5}
(F1.5)

assigns a vector to every point. It is independent of time, so it is stationary. It varies with position when ω≠0\omega\ne0; when ω=0\omega=0, it is the zero field. For any r>0r>0,

V(t,(r,0,0))=(0,ωr,0),V(t,(0,r,0))=(−ωr,0,0),V(t,(0,0,r))=(0,0,0). V(t,(r,0,0))=(0,\omega r,0),\qquad V(t,(0,r,0))=(-\omega r,0,0),\qquad V(t,(0,0,r))=(0,0,0). \tag{F1.6}
(F1.6)

These values describe rotation around the third coordinate axis when VV is interpreted as a velocity field.

That interpretation can be checked using a path. Fix r>0r>0 and z0∈ℝz_0\in\mathbb R, and define

x(t)=(rcos⁡(ωt),rsin⁡(ωt),z0). x(t)=\bigl(r\cos(\omega t),\ r\sin(\omega t),\ z_0\bigr). \tag{F1.7}
(F1.7)

Differentiation gives

dxdt=(−ωrsin⁡(ωt),ωrcos⁡(ωt),0)=V(t,x(t)). \frac{dx}{dt} =\bigl(-\omega r\sin(\omega t),\ \omega r\cos(\omega t),\ 0\bigr) =V(t,x(t)). \tag{F1.8}
(F1.8)

Thus this path is a trajectory of the velocity field; it travels around a circle when ω≠0\omega\ne0 and stays at one point when ω=0\omega=0. The field is the rule assigning velocities everywhere; the trajectory is one path that follows that rule.

Here is a scalar field that can change with time, defined on all of ℝ×ℝ3\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^3. Choose U0>0U_0>0, ℓ>0\ell>0 and v∈ℝv\in\mathbb R, and set

u(t,x)=U0exp⁡[−(x1−vt)2+(x2)2+(x3)2ℓ2]. u(t,x)=U_0 \exp\!\left[ -\frac{(x^1-vt)^2+(x^2)^2+(x^3)^2}{\ell^2} \right]. \tag{F1.9}
(F1.9)

Its maximum value is U0U_0, attained exactly at (vt,0,0)(vt,0,0), because the sum of the three squares is nonnegative and vanishes exactly there. The field is positive at every point. It becomes small far from its centre, but its support is all of space. Decay and compact support are different properties.

3. Units are part of the formula

Let 𝖫\mathsf L denote the dimension of length and 𝖳\mathsf T the dimension of time. Let 𝖴\mathsf U denote the dimension of the scalar quantity uu; its physical interpretation can be chosen separately. For (F1.9) to be meaningful, the dimensions are

[xi]=𝖫,[t]=𝖳,[v]=𝖫𝖳−1,[ℓ]=𝖫,[U0]=[u]=𝖴. [x^i]=\mathsf L,\quad [t]=\mathsf T,\quad [v]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1},\quad [\ell]=\mathsf L,\quad [U_0]=[u]=\mathsf U. \tag{F1.10}
(F1.10)

The two terms in x1−vtx^1-vt then have the same dimension. Its square and the other two squares have dimension 𝖫2\mathsf L^2. Division by ℓ2\ell^2 makes the exponent dimensionless, as required by the exponential function.

Likewise, the argument ωt\omega t of the sine and cosine in (F1.7) must be dimensionless. Consequently

[ω]=𝖳−1,[Vi]=𝖫𝖳−1. [\omega]=\mathsf T^{-1},\qquad [V^i]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1}. \tag{F1.11}
(F1.11)

Changing the length unit from metres to centimetres changes numerical values, not the physical quantity. For example, 0.50.5 metres per second is 5050 centimetres per second. In (F1.9) the numerical values of x1,x2,x3,ℓx^1,x^2,x^3,\ell and vv must all change consistently. Changing only ℓ\ell would define a different profile.

A derivative is a limit of a quotient. Its dimension therefore includes the dimension of the variable in the denominator:

[∂tu]=𝖴𝖳−1,[∂iu]=𝖴𝖫−1,[v∂1u]=𝖴𝖳−1. [\partial_tu]=\mathsf U\mathsf T^{-1},\qquad [\partial_i u]=\mathsf U\mathsf L^{-1},\qquad [v\partial_1u]=\mathsf U\mathsf T^{-1}. \tag{F1.12}
(F1.12)

The last two time-rate dimensions match, so an equation such as ∂tu+v∂1u=0\partial_tu+v\partial_1u=0 is dimensionally consistent. Dimensional consistency is a necessary check; it does not establish an equation. We will verify this particular equation by differentiation.

For comparison, ∂tu+∂1u=0\partial_tu+\partial_1u=0, as written in independently specified time and length units, adds unlike quantities. A coefficient with the dimension of speed is missing. We do not conceal it by setting a speed equal to the number one.

4. Differentiating a field

Write e1=(1,0,0)e_1=(1,0,0), e2=(0,1,0)e_2=(0,1,0), and e3=(0,0,1)e_3=(0,0,1). A spatial partial derivative changes one coordinate while holding the others and time fixed:

∂iu(t,x)=limh→0u(t,x+hei)−u(t,x)h,∂tu(t,x)=limh→0u(t+h,x)−u(t,x)h. \partial_i u(t,x)= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{u(t,x+he_i)-u(t,x)}{h},\qquad \partial_tu(t,x)= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{u(t+h,x)-u(t,x)}{h}. \tag{F1.13}
(F1.13)

The increments in the two limits have different units. The open-domain assumption ensures that sufficiently small increments remain in the domain. The notation C1C^1 means that these first partial derivatives exist and are continuous.

The spatial gradient is the vector of spatial derivatives in the specified Cartesian coordinates:

∇u=(∂1u,∂2u,∂3u). \nabla u=(\partial_1u,\partial_2u,\partial_3u). \tag{F1.14}
(F1.14)

To differentiate a vector field, differentiate each component. The matrix of its spatial derivatives has entries

(DxV)ij=∂jVi. (D_xV)^i{}_j=\partial_jV^i. \tag{F1.15}
(F1.15)

The output component is indexed by ii; the differentiated input coordinate is indexed by jj. For the rotating field (F1.5),

DxV=(0−ω0ω00000),∂tV=(0,0,0). D_xV= \begin{pmatrix} 0&-\omega&0\\ \omega&0&0\\ 0&0&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \partial_tV=(0,0,0). \tag{F1.16}
(F1.16)

Proposition 4.1. The derivative seen along a path

Let u∈C1(I×Ω)u\in C^1(I\times\Omega), and let x:I→Ωx:I\to\Omega be a C1C^1 path. Then

ddtu(t,x(t))=∂tu(t,x(t))+∑i=13dxidt(t)∂iu(t,x(t)). \frac{d}{dt}u(t,x(t)) =\partial_tu(t,x(t)) +\sum_{i=1}^{3}\frac{dx^i}{dt}(t)\, \partial_i u(t,x(t)). \tag{F1.17}
(F1.17)

Proof. We first justify the linear approximation needed in this formula. Set y=(t,x1,x2,x3)y=(t,x^1,x^2,x^3) and h=(h0,h1,h2,h3)h=(h^0,h^1,h^2,h^3). For sufficiently small hh, all coordinate segments below lie in the domain. Change one coordinate at a time. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives the exact identity

u(y+h)−u(y)=∑μ=03hμ∫01∂μu(y+∑ν<μhνeν+shμeμ)ds,∂0=∂t. u(y+h)-u(y) =\sum_{\mu=0}^{3}h^\mu \int_0^1 \partial_\mu u\!\left( y+\sum_{\nu<\mu}h^\nu e_\nu+s h^\mu e_\mu \right)\,ds, \qquad \partial_0=\partial_t. \tag{F1.18}
(F1.18)

Here the eμe_\mu are the four coordinate basis vectors, and the empty sum is zero. Continuity of the four derivatives shows that each integrand approaches ∂μu(y)\partial_\mu u(y), uniformly for 0≤s≤10\le s\le1, as h→0h\to0. Subtracting ∑μhμ∂μu(y)\sum_\mu h^\mu\partial_\mu u(y), the absolute remainder is at most ∑μ|hμ|\sum_\mu|h^\mu| times a quantity tending to zero. Thus the remainder divided by ∑μ|hμ|\sum_\mu|h^\mu| tends to zero. This assertion concerns coordinate numbers in fixed units.

Now take the increments

h0=δ,hi=xi(t+δ)−xi(t)=δdxidt(t)+o(δ). h^0=\delta,\qquad h^i=x^i(t+\delta)-x^i(t) =\delta\,\frac{dx^i}{dt}(t)+o(\delta). \tag{F1.19}
(F1.19)

Here o(δ)/δ→0o(\delta)/\delta\to0. The sum of the absolute increments is bounded by a constant times |δ||\delta| near zero, so the preceding remainder divided by δ\delta tends to zero. Divide (F1.18) by δ\delta, let δ→0\delta\to0, and obtain (F1.17). ▫\square

At a fixed point, the path has zero velocity and (F1.17) becomes ∂tu\partial_tu. A moving observer has the additional three spatial terms. These derivatives answer different questions.

Worked example: the moving profile

For (F1.9), the one-variable exponential rule and its polynomial argument give

∂tu=2v(x1−vt)ℓ2u,∂1u=−2(x1−vt)ℓ2u,∂2u=−2x2ℓ2u,∂3u=−2x3ℓ2u. \begin{aligned} \partial_tu&=\frac{2v(x^1-vt)}{\ell^2}\,u,\\ \partial_1u&=-\frac{2(x^1-vt)}{\ell^2}\,u,\\ \partial_2u&=-\frac{2x^2}{\ell^2}\,u,\qquad \partial_3u=-\frac{2x^3}{\ell^2}\,u. \end{aligned} \tag{F1.20}
(F1.20)

Every parameter in the original field is retained in these expressions. Adding the first line to vv times the second proves

∂tu+v∂1u=0. \partial_tu+v\partial_1u=0. \tag{F1.21}
(F1.21)

On the path x(t)=(ξ1+vt,ξ2,ξ3)x(t)=(\xi^1+vt,\xi^2,\xi^3), Proposition 4.1 says that the derivative of u(t,x(t))u(t,x(t)) is zero. Direct substitution also gives its value:

The field generally changes at a fixed spatial point while remaining constant along these moving paths. When v=0v=0, the entire profile is stationary.

5. A coordinate change changes the description

Consider a new Cartesian coordinate system given by

x′1=−x2,x′2=x1,x′3=x3. x'^1=-x^2,\qquad x'^2=x^1,\qquad x'^3=x^3. \tag{F1.23}
(F1.23)

The inverse relations are x1=x′2,x2=−x′1,x3=x′3x^1=x'^2,\ x^2=-x'^1,\ x^3=x'^3. This is a rotation of the coordinate values by a right angle in the first two coordinates. Time is unchanged.

A scalar assigns the same value to the same point in either coordinate system. Its new coordinate function is therefore

u′(t,x′)=u(t,x′2,−x′1,x′3). u'(t,x')=u(t,x'^2,-x'^1,x'^3). \tag{F1.24}
(F1.24)

For a spatial vector, the components change too:

V′1(t,x′)=−V2(t,x′2,−x′1,x′3),V′2(t,x′)=V1(t,x′2,−x′1,x′3),V′3(t,x′)=V3(t,x′2,−x′1,x′3). \begin{aligned} V'^1(t,x')&=-V^2(t,x'^2,-x'^1,x'^3),\\ V'^2(t,x')&= V^1(t,x'^2,-x'^1,x'^3),\\ V'^3(t,x')&= V^3(t,x'^2,-x'^1,x'^3). \end{aligned} \tag{F1.25}
(F1.25)

One way to establish this law is to use a vector tangent to a path. Differentiate all three equations in (F1.23). If dxi/dt=Vidx^i/dt=V^i, the resulting derivatives of x′ix'^i are exactly (F1.25). Because the coordinate change is linear, the same component rule applies to every vector.

For example, a spatially constant velocity (V0,0,0)(V_0,0,0) has new components (0,V0,0)(0,V_0,0). Its squared speed is unchanged: V02+0+0=0+V02+0V_0^2+0+0=0+V_0^2+0. More generally, (F1.25) gives the identity

(V′1)2+(V′2)2+(V′3)2=(V1)2+(V2)2+(V3)2 (V'^1)^2+(V'^2)^2+(V'^3)^2 =(V^1)^2+(V^2)^2+(V^3)^2 \tag{F1.26}
(F1.26)

when the two sides are evaluated at the corresponding coordinates.

Applying (F1.17), or the coordinate difference quotients directly, to (F1.24) yields

∂1′u′=−∂2u,∂2′u′=∂1u,∂3′u′=∂3u. \partial'_1u'=-\partial_2u,\qquad \partial'_2u'=\partial_1u,\qquad \partial'_3u'=\partial_3u. \tag{F1.27}
(F1.27)

Thus the gradient transforms by the same component rule as a spatial vector under this Cartesian rotation. For a general coordinate change, one must distinguish differentials, metrics and gradients; that geometrical development comes later.

If c1,c2,c3c_1,c_2,c_3 are three scalar concentrations, each instead follows (F1.24) separately. There is no minus sign or interchange of the species labels:

ca′(t,x′)=ca(t,x′2,−x′1,x′3),a=1,2,3. c'_a(t,x')=c_a(t,x'^2,-x'^1,x'^3),\qquad a=1,2,3. \tag{F1.28}
(F1.28)

This is why the number of components does not determine their meaning. The transformation rule is part of the field’s definition.

6. An evolution equation and its initial data

An equation imposes a relation on a field and its derivatives. An initial condition specifies the field at a chosen starting time. They are separate pieces of information.

For example, fixing v∈ℝv\in\mathbb R, the transport problem is

∂tu+v∂1u=0on ℝ×ℝ3,u(0,x)=f(x). \partial_tu+v\partial_1u=0 \quad\hbox{on }\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^3, \qquad u(0,x)=f(x). \tag{F1.29}
(F1.29)

The function f:ℝ3→ℝf:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R is prescribed. We can solve the entire problem, including uniqueness, using the path derivative already proved.

Theorem 6.1. The complete C1C^1 transport solution

For each f∈C1(ℝ3)f\in C^1(\mathbb R^3), the unique C1C^1 solution of (F1.29) is

u(t,x1,x2,x3)=f(x1−vt,x2,x3). u(t,x^1,x^2,x^3) =f(x^1-vt,x^2,x^3). \tag{F1.30}
(F1.30)

Proof. This formula defines a C1C^1 function for every real time. Differentiation gives ∂tu=−v(∂1f)(x1−vt,x2,x3)\partial_tu=-v(\partial_1 f)(x^1-vt,x^2,x^3) and ∂1u=(∂1f)(x1−vt,x2,x3)\partial_1u=(\partial_1 f)(x^1-vt,x^2,x^3); their stated sum vanishes. At t=0t=0 the formula equals f(x)f(x). Thus a solution exists.

For uniqueness, let ww be any C1C^1 solution, and fix (t,x)(t,x). Follow the path, parametrized by ss,

γ(s)=(x1−vt+vs,x2,x3). \gamma(s)=\bigl(x^1-vt+vs,\ x^2,\ x^3\bigr). \tag{F1.31}
(F1.31)

For each ss, Proposition 4.1 and the equation for ww give

ddsw(s,γ(s))=(∂tw+v∂1w)(s,γ(s))=0. \frac{d}{ds}w(s,\gamma(s)) =(\partial_tw+v\partial_1w)(s,\gamma(s))=0. \tag{F1.32}
(F1.32)

Integrating from s=0s=0 to s=ts=t, with the same identity valid for negative tt, gives

w(t,x)=w(0,x1−vt,x2,x3)=f(x1−vt,x2,x3). w(t,x)=w(0,x^1-vt,x^2,x^3)=f(x^1-vt,x^2,x^3).

This is (F1.30). ▫\square

The value at (t,x)(t,x) is determined by the single initial point (x1−vt,x2,x3)(x^1-vt,x^2,x^3). No other initial point enters this formula. That is the domain-of-dependence statement for this equation. Other evolution equations have different domains of dependence. We have not derived a universal propagation speed for physical fields.

The moving profile (F1.9) is exactly this solution with

f(x)=U0exp⁡[−(x1)2+(x2)2+(x3)2ℓ2]. f(x)=U_0\exp\!\left[ -\frac{(x^1)^2+(x^2)^2+(x^3)^2}{\ell^2} \right]. \tag{F1.33}
(F1.33)

It is one choice of initial data, not the only possible solution.

Corollary 6.2. Support and dependence on initial data

Define the support of a continuous function as the closure of the set where it is nonzero. Then the solution in (F1.30) satisfies

supp⁡u(t,⋅)={y+(vt,0,0):y∈supp⁡f}. \operatorname{supp}u(t,\cdot) =\{\,y+(vt,0,0):y\in\operatorname{supp}f\,\}. \tag{F1.34}
(F1.34)

For two bounded initial functions f1,f2f_1,f_2 of class C1C^1, their solutions satisfy the exact equality

supx∈ℝ3|u1(t,x)−u2(t,x)|=supy∈ℝ3|f1(y)−f2(y)|. \sup_{x\in\mathbb R^3}|u_1(t,x)-u_2(t,x)| =\sup_{y\in\mathbb R^3}|f_1(y)-f_2(y)|. \tag{F1.35}
(F1.35)

Proof. The map x↦x−(vt,0,0)x\mapsto x-(vt,0,0) is a bijection with a continuous inverse. Formula (F1.30) identifies the nonzero set with the translated nonzero set of ff; the bijection takes closures to closures, proving (F1.34). For (F1.35), substitute y=x−(vt,0,0)y=x-(vt,0,0). As xx ranges over all of ℝ3\mathbb R^3, so does yy; hence the two suprema are taken over the same set of values. ▫\square

Existence, uniqueness and continuous dependence are the three features usually meant by well-posedness once the function spaces and time interval have been specified. Here existence and uniqueness hold in C1C^1. For bounded C1C^1 data, the solution at each time depends continuously on the data in the supremum norm, with the exact estimate (F1.35), uniformly over all real times. Later equations require substantially more analysis.

7. A conservation law keeps its boundary flux

For a real C1C^1 solution of (F1.29), define

ρ(t,x)=u(t,x)22,J(t,x)=(vu(t,x)22,0,0). \rho(t,x)=\frac{u(t,x)^2}{2},\qquad J(t,x)=\left(\frac{v\,u(t,x)^2}{2},0,0\right). \tag{F1.36}
(F1.36)

These are a mathematical density and its flux for this transport equation. Calling them the physical energy of another theory would require that theory’s action and physical interpretation, which have not been specified here.

The product rule and (F1.29) prove

∂tρ+∂1J1+∂2J2+∂3J3=u(∂tu+v∂1u)=0. \partial_t\rho+\partial_1J^1+\partial_2J^2+\partial_3J^3 =u(\partial_tu+v\partial_1u)=0. \tag{F1.37}
(F1.37)

The divergence of JJ is the sum of its three indicated spatial derivatives. No spatial coordinate is omitted from this definition; the last two components happen to vanish in this example.

(F1.39)

Proof. On a compact time interval and the compact box, ∂tρ\partial_t\rho is continuous and uniformly continuous. Apply (F1.1) to the time difference quotient of ρ\rho. Its difference from ∂tρ(t,x)\partial_t\rho(t,x) tends to zero uniformly in xx. The integral of that difference is bounded by the box volume times this uniform bound. We may therefore differentiate QBQ_B under its integral.

Substitute (F1.37). For each ∂iJi\partial_iJ^i, integrate first in the ii-th coordinate and apply (F1.1). This gives the three face-pair integrals in (F1.39). The signs record outgoing minus incoming flux for each coordinate direction. Finally substitute J1=vu2/2J^1=vu^2/2, J2=0J^2=0 and J3=0J^3=0. The second and third face-pair terms are zero for that stated reason. ▫\square

The order of integration used here is valid for a continuous function on a finite box. To see why, choose a fine rectangular grid. Uniform continuity makes the variation of the integrand on each small box less than a number ε\varepsilon that tends to zero with the grid size. Every order of iterated integration differs from the same finite sum of sampled values times small-box volumes by at most ε\varepsilon times the large-box volume. Letting the grid size tend to zero shows that all orders have the same value.

A local conservation equation does not say that the amount inside every fixed box is constant. Material can cross its boundary. The boundary term in (F1.39) is part of the result.

For a continuous nonnegative function, its whole-space integral can be defined as the limit of its integrals over expanding cubes [−R,R]3[-R,R]^3 as R→∞R\to\infty. These integrals increase with RR; the limit may be infinite. When the whole-space integral of f2f^2 is finite, the amount over the whole space is constant:

∫ℝ3u(t,x)22d3x=∫ℝ3f(y)22d3y. \int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{u(t,x)^2}{2}\,d^3x =\int_{\mathbb R^3}\frac{f(y)^2}{2}\,d^3y. \tag{F1.40}
(F1.40)

To prove this, first integrate over the finite cube [−R,R]3[-R,R]^3. Substitute y1=x1−vt,y2=x2,y3=x3y^1=x^1-vt,\ y^2=x^2,\ y^3=x^3. Each one-dimensional substitution has derivative one. The transformed domain is [−R−vt,R−vt]×[−R,R]×[−R,R][-R-vt,R-vt]\times[-R,R]\times[-R,R], and the integrand is exactly f(y)2/2f(y)^2/2. If a=|vt|a=|vt| and R>aR>a, this domain contains [−R+a,R−a]3[-R+a,R-a]^3 and is contained in [−R−a,R+a]3[-R-a,R+a]^3. Nonnegativity therefore bounds its integral between the integrals over those two cubes. Both bounds tend to the same whole-space integral as R→∞R\to\infty. Taking the limit proves (F1.40); the assumed integrability makes it finite.

For example, compactly supported continuous ff is bounded on its compact support and vanishes outside a finite box, so f2f^2 is integrable. Formula (F1.40) therefore applies to such initial data.

Exercise 2. Restore the units

Suppose a field is written as u=U0e−qu=U_0 e^{-q}. Find the dimensional defects in the proposed expression q=((x1−t)2+(x2)2+(x3)2)/ℓq=((x^1-t)^2+(x^2)^2+(x^3)^2)/\ell, and give a dimensionally consistent expression using a speed vv and a length ℓ>0\ell>0.

Exercise 6. The support moves; it need not spread

Suppose f∈C1(ℝ3)f\in C^1(\mathbb R^3) has support contained in [a1,b1]×[a2,b2]×[a3,b3][a_1,b_1]\times[a_2,b_2]\times[a_3,b_3]. Give the corresponding support containment at time tt for (F1.29). Does the Gaussian in (F1.33) satisfy this hypothesis for any finite box?
