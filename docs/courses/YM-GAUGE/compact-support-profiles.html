Compact-support profiles, curvature and source

Lesson 2 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

The three quaternionic colour maps from Lesson 1 now become coefficients of spatial fields. We will construct those fields, calculate their curvature and source throughout the cutoff region, and find their complete energy. The calculation will also tell us what the static source prescribes as an initial acceleration.

On a narrow screen, wide equations and diagrams can be scrolled horizontally.

The starting algebra is Lesson 1. We use its bracket [v,w]=vw−wv=2v×w[v,w]=vw-wv=2v\times w on Im⁡ℍ\operatorname{Im}\mathbb H, its Euclidean inner product, and its exact matrix map

φ(𝒊)=2T1,φ(𝒋)=2T2,φ(𝒌)=2T3,Ta=−iℂσa/2. \varphi(\mathbf i)=2T_1,\qquad \varphi(\mathbf j)=2T_2,\qquad \varphi(\mathbf k)=2T_3,\qquad T_a=-i_{\mathbb C}\sigma_a/2. \tag{2.1}
(2.1)

In particular,

φ([v,w])=[φ(v),φ(w)],|φ(v)|mat2:=−2tr⁡(φ(v)2)=4|v|2. \varphi([v,w])=[\varphi(v),\varphi(w)],\qquad |\varphi(v)|_{\mathrm{mat}}^2 :=-2\operatorname{tr}(\varphi(v)^2)=4|v|^2. \tag{2.2}
(2.2)

The factor 44 will remain in the energy calculation. Spatial differentiation uses ordinary smooth real functions, the product rule, the chain rule and integration by parts. All constructions specific to this lesson are proved below. No evolution or quantum-state theorem is needed for these calculations.

1. A cutoff with its support specified

Fix two real numbers 0<r<R0<r<R. Write Br(0)={x∈ℝ3:|x|<r}B_r(0)=\{x\in\mathbb R^3:|x|<r\}, with the Euclidean metric and the orientation dx1∧dx2∧dx3dx_1\wedge dx_2\wedge dx_3. Choose

χ∈Cc∞(ℝ3),χ=1 on Br(0),supp⁡χ⊂BR(0). \chi\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^3),\qquad \chi=1\text{ on }B_r(0),\qquad \operatorname{supp}\chi\subset B_R(0). \tag{2.3}
(2.3)

Here support is the closure of the set on which the function is nonzero. The subscript cc says that this closed set is compact. We impose no radial symmetry or parity condition on the chosen χ\chi.

There really are functions satisfying (2.3). The following example proves existence without changing the general choice used later. Set

η(u)={e−1/u,u>0,0,u≤0,ρ=(r+R)/2, \eta(u)= \begin{cases}e^{-1/u},&u>0,\\0,&u\le0,\end{cases} \qquad \rho=(r+R)/2, \tag{2.4}
(2.4)

and define

χex(x)=η(ρ2−|x|2)η(ρ2−|x|2)+η(|x|2−r2). \chi_{\mathrm{ex}}(x)= \frac{\eta(\rho^2-|x|^2)} {\eta(\rho^2-|x|^2)+\eta(|x|^2-r^2)}. \tag{2.5}
(2.5)

For u>0u>0, each derivative of η\eta is a polynomial in 1/u1/u times e−1/ue^{-1/u}. This follows by induction using the product and chain rules. For every nonnegative integer NN, u−Ne−1/u→0u^{-N}e^{-1/u}\to0 as u↓0u\downarrow0: put v=1/uv=1/u and use ev≥vN+1/(N+1)!e^v\ge v^{N+1}/(N+1)!. Thus the right-hand limits of every derivative are zero, matching the derivatives on u<0u<0. This proves smoothness of η\eta across zero.

The denominator in (2.5) is positive everywhere. If |x|2≤r2|x|^2\le r^2, its first term is positive. If |x|2≥ρ2|x|^2\ge\rho^2, its second term is positive. Between these values both terms are positive. Consequently (2.5) is smooth, equals one for |x|≤r|x|\le r, and vanishes for |x|≥ρ|x|\ge\rho. Its support lies in Bρ(0)¯⊂BR(0)\overline{B_\rho(0)}\subset B_R(0), as required. Using ρ<R\rho<R keeps the support strictly inside the original open ball.

For the rest of the lesson retain the arbitrary χ\chi in (2.3). We use

χi=∂iχ,χij=∂i∂jχ,χijk=∂i∂j∂kχ. \chi_i=\partial_i\chi,\quad \chi_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\chi,\quad \chi_{ijk}=\partial_i\partial_j\partial_k\chi. \tag{2.6}
(2.6)

These derivatives commute because χ\chi is smooth. One direct reason is that the integral of ∂i∂jχ\partial_i\partial_j\chi over a coordinate rectangle, computed by two applications of the fundamental theorem of calculus, is the same four-corner difference as the integral of ∂j∂iχ\partial_j\partial_i\chi. Divide by the area and shrink the rectangle; continuity gives equality at its centre. Repeat for higher derivatives.

2. Three curls and every coordinate

For a vector field v=(v1,v2,v3)v=(v_1,v_2,v_3), use

∇×v=(∂2v3−∂3v2,∂3v1−∂1v3,∂1v2−∂2v1). \nabla\times v= (\partial_2v_3-\partial_3v_2,\ \partial_3v_1-\partial_1v_3,\ \partial_1v_2-\partial_2v_1). \tag{2.7}
(2.7)

Define the original three fields by

w(1)=∇×(0,0,χx2),w(2)=∇×(0,0,−χx1),w(3)=∇×(0,χx1,0). \begin{aligned} w^{(1)}&=\nabla\times(0,0,\chi x_2),\\ w^{(2)}&=\nabla\times(0,0,-\chi x_1),\\ w^{(3)}&=\nabla\times(0,\chi x_1,0). \end{aligned} \tag{2.8}
(2.8)

The product rule gives all nine components:

w(1)=(χ+x2χ2,−x2χ1,0),w(2)=(−x1χ2,χ+x1χ1,0),w(3)=(−x1χ3,0,χ+x1χ1). \begin{aligned} w^{(1)}&=(\chi+x_2\chi_2,\ -x_2\chi_1,\ 0),\\ w^{(2)}&=(-x_1\chi_2,\ \chi+x_1\chi_1,\ 0),\\ w^{(3)}&=(-x_1\chi_3,\ 0,\ \chi+x_1\chi_1). \end{aligned} \tag{2.9}
(2.9)

Every term is smooth. Outside supp⁡χ\operatorname{supp}\chi, the function χ\chi vanishes on a neighbourhood, so every derivative there vanishes. It follows that supp⁡w(a)⊂supp⁡χ\operatorname{supp}w^{(a)}\subset\operatorname{supp}\chi. Differentiating (2.9) gives the divergence identities with their cancelling terms visible:

∑i∂iwi(1)=χ1+x2χ12−χ1−x2χ21+0=0,∑i∂iwi(2)=−χ2−x1χ12+χ2+x1χ21+0=0,∑i∂iwi(3)=−χ3−x1χ13+0+χ3+x1χ31=0. \begin{aligned} \sum_i\partial_iw_i^{(1)} &=\chi_1+x_2\chi_{12}-\chi_1-x_2\chi_{21}+0=0,\\ \sum_i\partial_iw_i^{(2)} &=-\chi_2-x_1\chi_{12}+\chi_2+x_1\chi_{21}+0=0,\\ \sum_i\partial_iw_i^{(3)} &=-\chi_3-x_1\chi_{13}+0+\chi_3+x_1\chi_{31}=0. \end{aligned} \tag{2.10}
(2.10)

On Br(0)B_r(0), all derivatives of χ\chi vanish. Thus

w(1)=(1,0,0),w(2)=(0,1,0),w(3)=(0,0,1)(|x|<r). w^{(1)}=(1,0,0),\qquad w^{(2)}=(0,1,0),\qquad w^{(3)}=(0,0,1)\qquad (|x|<r). \tag{2.11}
(2.11)

This also proves linear independence. If ∑ataw(a)=0\sum_a t_aw^{(a)}=0 as a field, evaluate at x=0x=0; its three components are t1,t2,t3t_1,t_2,t_3, hence all vanish.

The Euclidean metric identifies w(a)w^{(a)} with ∑iwi(a)dxi\sum_iw_i^{(a)}dx_i. This is the stated vector-to-one-form map; it does not change the components.

The drawing uses a coordinate projection of the balls. The formulas, rather than the drawn lengths, specify the radii and support. The core values are (2.11); every cutoff contribution is retained in (2.9).

3. The complete 24-dimensional input reaches spatial fields

Use the same ordered real representation as Lesson 1:

W=ℝtriv5⊕Im⁡ℍAd⊕ℍq,1⊕ℍq,2⊕ℍp,1⊕ℍp,2. W=\mathbb R^5_{\mathrm{triv}} \oplus\operatorname{Im}\mathbb H_{\mathrm{Ad}} \oplus\mathbb H_{q,1}\oplus\mathbb H_{q,2} \oplus\mathbb H_{p,1}\oplus\mathbb H_{p,2}. \tag{2.12}
(2.12)

An input is (z,v;α,β;γ,δ)(z,v;\alpha,\beta;\gamma,\delta), of real dimension 5+3+4+4+4+4=245+3+4+4+4+4=24. Put

c1=α𝒊α‾,c2=β𝒋β‾,c3=γ𝒌γ‾,Ci(x)=∑a=13cawi(a)(x). c_1=\alpha\mathbf i\bar\alpha,\qquad c_2=\beta\mathbf j\bar\beta,\qquad c_3=\gamma\mathbf k\bar\gamma, \qquad C_i(x)=\sum_{a=1}^3c_aw_i^{(a)}(x). \tag{2.13}
(2.13)

The letters cac_a are colour coefficients; the index ii labels a spatial component. Each cac_a is constant as a function of xx. In particular,

C1=c1(χ+x2χ2)+c2(−x1χ2)+c3(−x1χ3),C2=c1(−x2χ1)+c2(χ+x1χ1)+c30,C3=c10+c20+c3(χ+x1χ1). \begin{aligned} C_1&=c_1(\chi+x_2\chi_2)+c_2(-x_1\chi_2)+c_3(-x_1\chi_3),\\ C_2&=c_1(-x_2\chi_1)+c_2(\chi+x_1\chi_1)+c_3\,0,\\ C_3&=c_1\,0+c_2\,0+c_3(\chi+x_1\chi_1). \end{aligned} \tag{2.14}
(2.14)

Let 𝒱div,R\mathcal V_{\mathrm{div},R} be the real vector space of smooth, compactly supported, divergence-free vector fields with support in BR(0)¯\overline{B_R(0)}. We have constructed a map

ℳ:W→Im⁡ℍ⊗𝒱div,R,ℳ(z,v;α,β;γ,δ)=∑aca⊗w(a). \mathcal M:W\longrightarrow \operatorname{Im}\mathbb H\otimes\mathcal V_{\mathrm{div},R},\qquad \mathcal M(z,v;\alpha,\beta;\gamma,\delta) =\sum_a c_a\otimes w^{(a)}. \tag{2.15}
(2.15)

Theorem 3.1. The map (2.15) is smooth, quadratic and equivariant. Its image is the full nine-dimensional space

{∑a=13ca⊗w(a):(c1,c2,c3)∈(Imℍ)3}. \left\{\sum_{a=1}^3c_a\otimes w^{(a)}: (c_1,c_2,c_3)\in(\operatorname{Im}\mathbb H)^3\right\}. \tag{2.16}
(2.16)

Its zero set is exactly

ℝtriv5⊕Im⁡ℍAd⊕0q,1⊕0q,2⊕0p,1⊕ℍp,2. \mathbb R^5_{\mathrm{triv}}\oplus\operatorname{Im}\mathbb H_{\mathrm{Ad}} \oplus0_{q,1}\oplus0_{q,2}\oplus0_{p,1}\oplus\mathbb H_{p,2}. \tag{2.17}
(2.17)

This subspace has real dimension 1212. If exactly mm of α,β,γ\alpha,\beta,\gamma are nonzero, the derivative has rank 3m3m and kernel dimension 24−3m24-3m.

Proof. Define a real-linear map L(c1,c2,c3)=∑aca⊗w(a)L(c_1,c_2,c_3)=\sum_a c_a\otimes w^{(a)}. Evaluation at the origin, taking the three spatial components, is an explicit left inverse:

(C1(0),C2(0),C3(0))=(c1,c2,c3). (C_1(0),C_2(0),C_3(0))=(c_1,c_2,c_3). \tag{2.18}
(2.18)

Thus LL is injective. The map ℳ\mathcal M is exactly LQLQ, where QQ is the onto quadratic map of Lesson 1, Theorem 6.1. Its image is therefore (2.16), and injectivity of LL gives ker⁡Dℳ=ker⁡DQ\ker D\mathcal M=\ker DQ at every original input. The zero set and all four ranks now follow from that theorem, including the twelve z,v,δz,v,\delta directions which remain in the domain.

For completeness, the group action fixes zz, conjugates vv, and left-multiplies each of α,β,γ,δ\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta by the same unit quaternion uu. Since (uα)euα¯=u(αeα‾)u‾(u\alpha)e\overline{u\alpha}=u(\alpha e\bar\alpha)\bar u, each cac_a, and hence every Ci(x)C_i(x), is conjugated by uu. This is the required equivariance. The target coefficients are smooth quadratic polynomials in the input, proving smoothness and degree. ▫\square

4. Curvature throughout the cutoff region

The quaternionic spatial curvature and its matrix counterpart are

ℱij=∂iCj−∂jCi+[Ci,Cj],Fij=φ(ℱij). \mathcal F_{ij} =\partial_iC_j-\partial_jC_i+[C_i,C_j], \qquad F_{ij}=\varphi(\mathcal F_{ij}). \tag{2.19}
(2.19)

Hija=∂iwj(a)−∂jwi(a),Wijab=wi(a)wj(b)−wi(b)wj(a). H^a_{ij}=\partial_iw_j^{(a)}-\partial_jw_i^{(a)},\qquad W^{ab}_{ij}=w_i^{(a)}w_j^{(b)}-w_i^{(b)}w_j^{(a)}. \tag{2.20}
(2.20)

When needed with other index orders, retain Hjia=−HijaH^a_{ji}=-H^a_{ij}, Wjiab=−WijabW^{ab}_{ji}=-W^{ab}_{ij}, and the zero diagonal values. Then the exact curvature is

ℱij=∑a=13caHija+∑1≤a<b≤3[ca,cb]Wijab. \mathcal F_{ij} =\sum_{a=1}^3 c_aH^a_{ij} +\sum_{1\le a<b\le3}[c_a,c_b]W^{ab}_{ij}. \tag{2.21}
(2.21)

To prove this, expand [Ci,Cj][C_i,C_j] as the ordered double sum ∑a,b[ca,cb]wi(a)wj(b)\sum_{a,b}[c_a,c_b]w_i^{(a)}w_j^{(b)}. The a=ba=b terms are zero. Pairing its (a,b)(a,b) and (b,a)(b,a) terms gives precisely the two products in (2.20). The derivative part follows directly from (2.13).

5. The full static source

For a quaternion-valued function VV, set

DiV=∂iV+[Ci,V],Sj=∑i=13Diℱij. D_iV=\partial_iV+[C_i,V],\qquad S_j=\sum_{i=1}^3D_i\mathcal F_{ij}. \tag{2.26}
(2.26)

The four complete groups of terms obtained from (2.21) are

Sj=∑aca∑i∂iHija+∑a<b[ca,cb]∑i∂iWijab+∑d,a[cd,ca]∑iwi(d)Hija+∑da<b[cd,[ca,cb]]∑iwi(d)Wijab. \begin{aligned} S_j={}& \sum_a c_a\sum_i\partial_iH^a_{ij}\\ &+\sum_{a<b}[c_a,c_b]\sum_i\partial_iW^{ab}_{ij}\\ &+\sum_{d,a}[c_d,c_a]\sum_iw_i^{(d)}H^a_{ij}\\ &+\sum_{\substack{d\\a<b}}[c_d,[c_a,c_b]] \sum_iw_i^{(d)}W^{ab}_{ij}. \end{aligned} \tag{2.27}
(2.27)

The sums a,d,ia,d,i each range from 11 to 33; a<ba<b ranges over (1,2),(1,3),(2,3)(1,2),(1,3),(2,3). No derivative acts on a cac_a. In particular the derivative of each minor means all four product-rule terms:

(2.28)

∑i∂iHija=∑i∂i2wj(a)−∂j(∑i∂iwi(a))=Δwj(a)−∂j0. \sum_i\partial_iH^a_{ij} =\sum_i\partial_i^2w_j^{(a)} -\partial_j\left(\sum_i\partial_iw_i^{(a)}\right) =\Delta w_j^{(a)}-\partial_j0. \tag{2.29}

(2.30)

For instance, Δ(xkf)=xkΔf+2∂kf\Delta(x_k f)=x_k\Delta f+2\partial_kf, since the second derivative of xkx_k is zero. Applying this identity to each product in (2.9) proves (2.30), including every third derivative within ∂iΔχ\partial_i\Delta\chi. 6. Why the source is covariantly conserved

The source (2.26) satisfies

∑jDjSj=0. \sum_jD_jS_j=0. \tag{2.32}
(2.32)

We prove this identity rather than infer it from the ordinary divergence of CC. Expanding two covariant derivatives on any smooth VV gives

(DiDj−DjDi)V=[ℱij,V]. (D_iD_j-D_jD_i)V=[\mathcal F_{ij},V]. \tag{2.33}
(2.33)

Indeed the mixed ordinary derivatives cancel, the derivatives of VV in the cross terms cancel, and the remaining terms are [∂iCj−∂jCi,V]+[Ci,[Cj,V]]−[Cj,[Ci,V]][\partial_iC_j-\partial_jC_i,V] +[C_i,[C_j,V]]-[C_j,[C_i,V]]. Expanding the last two commutators in the associative quaternion algebra leaves [[Ci,Cj],V][[C_i,C_j],V], proving (2.33).

Now exchange the two indices in the full ordered sum:

2∑jDjSj=∑i,j(DjDi−DiDj)ℱij=∑i,j[ℱji,ℱij]=0. \begin{aligned} 2\sum_jD_jS_j &=\sum_{i,j}(D_jD_i-D_iD_j)\mathcal F_{ij}\\ &=\sum_{i,j}[\mathcal F_{ji},\mathcal F_{ij}]=0. \end{aligned} \tag{2.34}
(2.34)

The last equality holds term by term because ℱji=−ℱij\mathcal F_{ji}=-\mathcal F_{ij}. In ordinary derivatives (2.32) reads

∑j∂jSj+∑j[Cj,Sj]=0. \sum_j\partial_jS_j+\sum_j[C_j,S_j]=0. \tag{2.35}
(2.35)

It includes the second sum. It does not say that either sum separately vanishes, nor that SS is zero.

7. Energy with all cutoff coefficients

At zero electric field, the physical energy of the profile is

ℰg=12g2∫ℝ3∑i<j|φ(ℱij)|mat2d3x=2g2∫ℝ3∑i<j|ℱij|2d3x. \mathscr E_g =\frac1{2g^2}\int_{\mathbb R^3} \sum_{i<j}|\varphi(\mathcal F_{ij})|_{\mathrm{mat}}^2\,d^3x =\frac2{g^2}\int_{\mathbb R^3} \sum_{i<j}|\mathcal F_{ij}|^2\,d^3x. \tag{2.36}
