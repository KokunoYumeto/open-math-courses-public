Constraints, initial data and energy

This lesson answers three questions about a classical Yang–Mills field. Which data may be prescribed at one time? How can a gauge be chosen without changing those data’s physical content? What quantity is conserved, and how does it leave a region?

The preparation is the action and field equations, with the connection and transport constructions of Lesson 6. All fields in Sections 1–9 are smooth. Smoothness here means that every derivative used exists and is continuous. Existence for general initial data is the subject of the next lesson; none of the energy identities below assumes such an existence theorem as its proof.

2. What can be prescribed at one time?

The pure equation is ℰν=0\mathcal E^\nu=0. More generally, use the current convention of Lesson 7, ℰν=gYM2Jν\mathcal E^\nu=g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2J^\nu. Because Fit=c−2EiF^{it}=c^{-2}E_i and Ftj=−c−2EjF^{tj}=-c^{-2}E_j, its four components are exactly

∑i𝒟iEi=c2gYM2Jt,𝒟tEj=c2∑i𝒟iFij−c2gYM2Jj(j=1,2,3). \begin{aligned} \sum_i\mathcal D_iE_i&=c^2g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2J^t,\\ \mathcal D_tE_j&=c^2\sum_i\mathcal D_iF_{ij} -c^2g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2J^j &&(j=1,2,3). \end{aligned} \tag{F8.5}
(F8.5)

The first equation has no time derivative of EE. It is the Gauss constraint. Initial data for the pure field consist of three connection coefficients ai(x)=Γi(t0,x)a_i(x)=\Gamma_i(t_0,x) and three electric coefficients ei(x)=Ei(t0,x)e_i(x)=E_i(t_0,x), obeying

∑i(∂iei+[ai,ei])=0,b1=f23,b2=f31,b3=f12,fij=∂iaj−∂jai+[ai,aj]. \sum_i\bigl(\partial_i e_i+[a_i,e_i]\bigr)=0,\qquad b_1=f_{23},\ b_2=f_{31},\ b_3=f_{12},\quad f_{ij}=\partial_i a_j-\partial_j a_i+[a_i,a_j]. \tag{F8.6}
(F8.6)

Thus bib_i are computed from aia_i. The magnetic field is fixed by the connection data. Smooth pure data satisfying (F8.6) will be called smooth constraint data. This definition concerns the constraint; it makes no assertion about finite energy or a solution’s lifespan.

Specifying aia_i and eie_i is also different from specifying Γi\Gamma_i and ∂tΓi\partial_t\Gamma_i without a gauge:

Ei=∂tΓi−𝒟iΓt,∂tΓi(t0)=ei+∂iΓt(t0)+[ai,Γt(t0)]. E_i=\partial_t\Gamma_i-\mathcal D_i\Gamma_t,\qquad \partial_t\Gamma_i(t_0)=e_i+ \partial_i\Gamma_t(t_0)+[a_i,\Gamma_t(t_0)]. \tag{F8.7}
(F8.7)

The equality follows by expanding FtiF_{ti}, including [Γt,Γi]=−[Γi,Γt][\Gamma_t,\Gamma_i]=-[\Gamma_i,\Gamma_t]. It shows the precise role of the time component.

There are many actual constraint data. Fix T∈𝔤T\in\mathfrak g, choose smooth real functions aiaba_i^{\mathrm{ab}}, and a smooth real vector field vv. Set

ai=Taiab,ei=T(∇×v)i. a_i=T a_i^{\mathrm{ab}},\qquad e_i=T(\nabla\times v)_i. \tag{F8.8}
(F8.8)

All commutators vanish and ∑i∂i(∇×v)i=0\sum_i\partial_i(\nabla\times v)_i=0, since the mixed second derivatives cancel in pairs. Compactly supported choices have finite energy. Conversely, ai=0a_i=0, e1=x1Te_1=x^1T, e2=e3=0e_2=e_3=0, with T≠0T\ne0, fail the constraint on every open set. The ability to write smooth functions does not make them permissible data.

Noncommutativity matters even when the coefficients are constant. For Tj=−iσj/2T_j=-i\sigma_j/2 in 𝔰𝔲(2)\mathfrak{su}(2), [Ti,Tj]=ϵijkTk[T_i,T_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}T_k and κ(Ti,Tj)=δij/2\kappa(T_i,T_j)=\delta_{ij}/2. On all of ℝ3\mathbb R^3 nonzero constant field strengths have infinite total energy; on a finite periodic box the same data have finite energy.

3. Temporal gauge is an actual construction

A gauge transformation is a smooth map u:I×U→Gu:I\times U\to G acting by

Γμu=uΓμu−1−(∂μu)u−1,Fμνu=uFμνu−1. \Gamma_\mu^u=u\Gamma_\mu u^{-1}-(\partial_\mu u)u^{-1},\qquad F_{\mu\nu}^u=uF_{\mu\nu}u^{-1}. \tag{F8.9}
(F8.9)

The temporal gauge condition is Γtu=0\Gamma_t^u=0. It is equivalent to a matrix ordinary differential equation with the multiplication on the indicated side:

∂tu(t,x)=u(t,x)Γt(t,x),u(t0,x)=IN. \partial_tu(t,x)=u(t,x)\Gamma_t(t,x),\qquad u(t_0,x)=I_N. \tag{F8.10}
(F8.10)

To construct it, solve ∂tP=−ΓtP\partial_tP=-\Gamma_tP, P(t0,x)=INP(t_0,x)=I_N, by the ordered-integral series in Lesson 6, Section 6. Set u=P−1u=P^{-1}. Differentiating P−1P=INP^{-1}P=I_N gives ∂t(P−1)=P−1Γt\partial_t(P^{-1})=P^{-1}\Gamma_t, exactly (F8.10). On a compact time interval and a compact spatial neighbourhood let ∥Γt∥≤M\|\Gamma_t\|\le M and |t−t0|≤L|t-t_0|\le L. The term with nn factors has norm at most MnLn/n!M^nL^n/n!; the series and its differentiated parameter series converge as proved there. They give a smooth solution on every such compact set. Uniqueness makes the solutions agree on overlaps, so they define uu throughout I×UI\times U. The group-preservation argument of Lesson 6 gives P,u∈U(N)P,u\in U(N) and, for traceless coefficients, P,u∈SU(N)P,u\in SU(N). Pn(t)−P(t)=∫t0tP(t)P(s)−1(Γt(s)−Bn(s))Pn(s)ds. P_n(t)-P(t)=\int_{t_0}^t P(t)P(s)^{-1}(\Gamma_t(s)-B_n(s))P_n(s)\,ds.

4. Why the constraint persists

This section proves propagation along a smooth solution of the three spatial evolution equations. It does not presume the fourth equation. The connection identity established in Lesson 7 is

𝒟νℰν=𝒟ν𝒟μFμν=12[Fνμ,Fμν]=0. \mathcal D_\nu\mathcal E^\nu =\mathcal D_\nu\mathcal D_\mu F^{\mu\nu} =\tfrac12[F_{\nu\mu},F^{\mu\nu}]=0. \tag{F8.13}
(F8.13)

For clarity, exchanging μ,ν\mu,\nu and using antisymmetry turns the double derivative into half its commutator. The curvature commutator formula gives the middle expression. Its sum vanishes: the coefficient gμαgνβg^{\mu\alpha}g^{\nu\beta} is unchanged when the pairs (ν,μ)(\nu,\mu) and (β,α)(\beta,\alpha) exchange, whereas their bracket changes sign. In our diagonal metric each nonzero contracted summand is already a scalar multiple of [Fνμ,Fνμ]=0[F_{\nu\mu},F_{\nu\mu}]=0.

Set Rν=ℰν−gYM2JνR^\nu=\mathcal E^\nu-g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2J^\nu, and define the constraint residual

C=∑i𝒟iEi−c2gYM2Jt=c2Rt. C=\sum_i\mathcal D_iE_i-c^2g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2J^t=c^2R^t. \tag{F8.14}
(F8.14)

For pure Yang–Mills take Jν=0J^\nu=0. With matter, require its actual equation, which supplies 𝒟νJν=0\mathcal D_\nu J^\nu=0; the specified scalar model and proof are given in Section 7. If Rj=0R^j=0 for j=1,2,3j=1,2,3, (F8.13) now yields

𝒟tC=0. \mathcal D_t C=0. \tag{F8.15}
(F8.15)

Let PP be the transport of Section 3. Differentiating directly gives ∂t(P−1CP)=P−1(𝒟tC)P=0\partial_t(P^{-1}CP)=P^{-1}(\mathcal D_tC)P=0. Therefore

C(t,x)=P(t,x)C(t0,x)P(t,x)−1. C(t,x)=P(t,x)C(t_0,x)P(t,x)^{-1}. \tag{F8.16}
(F8.16)

Zero initial residual remains zero. In temporal gauge P=INP=I_N, so C(t,x)=C(t0,x)C(t,x)=C(t_0,x). More strongly, the norm κ(C,C)\kappa(C,C) remains constant along each time line, by invariance of κ\kappa. Constraint violation is transported by these exact evolution equations; they do not automatically remove incorrect data. If an externally specified current fails covariant conservation, the same calculation gives 𝒟tC=−c2gYM2𝒟νJν\mathcal D_tC=-c^2g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2\mathcal D_\nu J^\nu. This formula identifies the compatibility failure explicitly.

5. The stress tensor, derived from the metric

The stress tensor records the response of the action to the metric. For a compactly supported symmetric variation hμν=δgμνh^{\mu\nu}=\delta g^{\mu\nu}, hold the connection fixed and define its covariant components by δgS=−12∫wTμνhμνd4x\delta_gS=-\tfrac12\int w T_{\mu\nu}h^{\mu\nu}\,d^4x. The metric is varied within nondegenerate metrics of the same signature. Differentiate gμαgαν=δμνg_{\mu\alpha}g^{\alpha\nu}=\delta_\mu^\nu and the determinant identity to obtain

δgμν=−gμαhαβgβν,δw=−12wgμνhμν. \delta g_{\mu\nu}=-g_{\mu\alpha}h^{\alpha\beta}g_{\beta\nu}, \qquad \delta w=-\tfrac12w g_{\mu\nu}h^{\mu\nu}. \tag{F8.17}
(F8.17)

The determinant derivative follows by multilinearity in its columns: δdet⁡g=(det⁡g)tr⁡(g−1δg)\delta\det g=(\det g)\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}\delta g). The sign of det⁡g\det g is locally constant, so the same logarithmic derivative applies to |det⁡g||\det g|. Taking its square root gives (F8.17).

Put K=κ(Fαβ,Fαβ)K=\kappa(F_{\alpha\beta},F^{\alpha\beta}) and Qμν=κ(Fμα,Fνα)Q_{\mu\nu}=\kappa(F_{\mu\alpha},F_\nu{}^\alpha). Varying each of the two inverse metrics in K=gαγgβδκ(Fαβ,Fγδ)K=g^{\alpha\gamma}g^{\beta\delta} \kappa(F_{\alpha\beta},F_{\gamma\delta}) gives QμνhμνQ_{\mu\nu}h^{\mu\nu} twice. Pair symmetry of κ\kappa and antisymmetry of FF show that Qμν=QνμQ_{\mu\nu}=Q_{\nu\mu}. Thus

δK=2Qμνhμν,TμνYM=2ℏgYM2(κ(Fμα,Fνα)−14gμνK). \begin{aligned} \delta K&=2Q_{\mu\nu}h^{\mu\nu},\\ T^{\mathrm{YM}}_{\mu\nu} &=\frac{2\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \left(\kappa(F_{\mu\alpha},F_\nu{}^\alpha) -\tfrac14g_{\mu\nu}K\right). \end{aligned} \tag{F8.18}
(F8.18)

This is the stress tensor for the full action (F8.4), including its pairing and factor 1/21/2. Its useful components are

TttYM=ℏgYM2(|E|κ2+c2|B|κ2),TtiYM=2ℏgYM2∑j,kϵijkκ(Ej,Bk),TijYM=2ℏgYM2[−c−2κ(Ei,Ej)−κ(Bi,Bj)+12δij(c−2|E|κ2+|B|κ2)]. \begin{aligned} T^{\mathrm{YM}}_{tt} &=\frac{\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} (|E|_\kappa^2+c^2|B|_\kappa^2),\\ T^{\mathrm{YM}}_{ti} &=\frac{2\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \sum_{j,k}\epsilon_{ijk}\kappa(E_j,B_k),\\ T^{\mathrm{YM}}_{ij} &=\frac{2\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \left[-c^{-2}\kappa(E_i,E_j)-\kappa(B_i,B_j) +\tfrac12\delta_{ij} (c^{-2}|E|_\kappa^2+|B|_\kappa^2)\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{F8.19}
(F8.19)

To check the spatial line, multiply ϵikℓϵjkm\epsilon_{ik\ell}\epsilon_{jkm} and sum over kk; the result is δijδℓm−δimδℓj\delta_{ij}\delta_{\ell m}-\delta_{im}\delta_{\ell j}. The time summand in QijQ_{ij} is −c−2κ(Ei,Ej)-c^{-2}\kappa(E_i,E_j). Substituting (F8.4) in the metric term then gives the displayed expression. All components are gauge invariant real numbers by conjugation invariance of κ\kappa.

We also need their divergence, including the force term when a current is present. Pairing invariance implies ∂μκ(X,Y)=κ(𝒟μX,Y)+κ(X,𝒟μY)\partial_\mu\kappa(X,Y)= \kappa(\mathcal D_\mu X,Y)+\kappa(X,\mathcal D_\mu Y). At the fixed constant metric of (F8.1), the Bianchi identity gives

κ(Fμα,𝒟μFνα)=14∂νK. \kappa(F^{\mu\alpha},\mathcal D_\mu F_{\nu\alpha}) =\tfrac14\partial_\nu K. \tag{F8.20}
(F8.20)

Here is the contraction in full. Write its left side as AA. Pair FμαF^{\mu\alpha} with 𝒟μFνα−𝒟νFμα+𝒟αFμν=0\mathcal D_\mu F_{\nu\alpha}-\mathcal D_\nu F_{\mu\alpha} +\mathcal D_\alpha F_{\mu\nu}=0. The middle contraction is −12∂νK-\tfrac12\partial_\nu K. In the last contraction exchange μ,α\mu,\alpha; antisymmetry of both FμαF^{\mu\alpha} and FανF_{\alpha\nu} makes it AA. Hence 2A−12∂νK=02A-\tfrac12\partial_\nu K=0, proving (F8.20). The product rule in (F8.18) now cancels these derivative terms exactly:

∂μ(TYM)μν=2ℏgYM2κ(ℰα,Fνα)=2ℏκ(Jα,Fνα). \partial_\mu (T^{\mathrm{YM}})^\mu{}_\nu =\frac{2\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} \kappa(\mathcal E^\alpha,F_{\nu\alpha}) =2\hbar\,\kappa(J^\alpha,F_{\nu\alpha}). \tag{F8.21}
(F8.21)

The final equality uses the gauge field equation. Pure Yang–Mills has zero divergence. This proof used Bianchi, the field equation and the actual metric variation, rather than an assumption that any quadratic expression should be conserved.

6. Energy, its gauge term, and its flux

The momentum paired with a variation of ∂tΓi\partial_t\Gamma_i in ℒYM\mathscr L_{\mathrm{YM}} is πi=2ℏEi/(gYM2c)\pi_i=2\hbar E_i/(g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2c). There is no derivative ∂tΓt\partial_t\Gamma_t in the action, so its momentum is zero. In temporal gauge the Legendre expression ∑iκ(πi,∂tΓi)−ℒYM\sum_i\kappa(\pi_i,\partial_t\Gamma_i)-\mathscr L_{\mathrm{YM}} equals

eYM=ℏgYM2(c−1|E|κ2+c|B|κ2)=TttYMc. e_{\mathrm{YM}} =\frac{\hbar}{g_{\mathrm{YM}}^2} (c^{-1}|E|_\kappa^2+c|B|_\kappa^2) =\frac{T^{\mathrm{YM}}_{tt}}{c}. \tag{F8.22}
(F8.22)

We call eYMe_{\mathrm{YM}} the energy density relative to the coordinate measure d3xd^3x. It is nonnegative and vanishes exactly when every Ei,BiE_i,B_i is zero.
