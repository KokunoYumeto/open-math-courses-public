Integrating variables and reading formal expansions

Complete companion to Unit 18. These are independent educational derivations of familiar finite-dimensional facts. They make no novelty claim. Every integral, coordinate, source, determinant and additive constant used below is specified. None of the three comparisons establishes a Yang–Mills spectral statement.

G0. Coordinates, units and the elementary Gaussian integral

Let p,q≥1p,q\geq1 be integers. The coordinates x∈ℝpx\in\mathbb R^p and y∈ℝqy\in\mathbb R^q are dimensionless, with the specified reference measures dx=∏i=1pdxidx=\prod_{i=1}^p dx_i and dy=∏j=1qdyjdy=\prod_{j=1}^qdy_j. Fix an action unit. The parameter ℏ>0\hbar>0, each entry of the matrices A,B,CA,B,C, each entry of the source vectors Jx,JyJ_x,J_y, and the real number S0S_0 have action units. Thus every term in the action below has action units and every exponential has a dimensionless exponent. An energy convention instead requires a specified time scale; it is not introduced here.

For completeness, write I=∫ℝe−t2/2dtI=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t^2/2}\,dt. This is finite because e−t2/2≤e−|t|/2e^{-t^2/2}\leq e^{-|t|/2} when |t|≥1|t|\geq1. Tonelli’s theorem for the nonnegative product, followed by polar coordinates on disks and monotone convergence, gives

I2=∫ℝ2e−(s2+t2)/2dsdt=∫02π∫0∞e−r2/2rdrdθ=2π. I^2=\int_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-(s^2+t^2)/2}\,ds\,dt =\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi. \tag{G0.1}
(G0.1)

Since I>0I>0, I=2πI=\sqrt{2\pi}. The absolute first and second moments are finite by exponential decay. Oddness gives ∫te−t2/2dt=0\int t e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=0. Integration by parts on [−R,R][-R,R], with te−t2/2→0t e^{-t^2/2}\to0 at both ends, gives ∫t2e−t2/2dt=2π\int t^2e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\sqrt{2\pi}.

We also recall a direct triangular factorization proof. If a real symmetric positive-definite n×nn\times n matrix is written as

H=(abTbD), H=\begin{pmatrix}a&b^T\\b&D\end{pmatrix},

then a>0a>0, and for w≠0w\ne0

wT(D−bbT/a)w=(−bTw/a,w)TH(−bTw/a,w)>0. w^T(D-bb^T/a)w=(-b^Tw/a,w)^TH(-b^Tw/a,w)>0.

Induction starting with n=1n=1 factors D−bbT/a=R2TR2D-bb^T/a=R_2^TR_2, where R2R_2 is upper triangular with positive diagonal. Then

R=(abT/a0R2),H=RTR. R=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt a&b^T/\sqrt a\\0&R_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad H=R^TR. \tag{G0.2}
(G0.2)

Consequently RR is invertible, det⁡R>0\det R>0, and det⁡H=(det⁡R)2>0\det H=(\det R)^2>0. With action units on HH, fix an arbitrary m∈ℝnm\in\mathbb R^n, and let zz range over ℝn\mathbb R^n. The substitution

t=R(z−m)/ℏ,z=m+ℏR−1t,dz=ℏn/2det⁡Rdt t=R(z-m)/\sqrt\hbar, \qquad z=m+\sqrt\hbar R^{-1}t, \qquad dz=\frac{\hbar^{n/2}}{\det R}\,dt \tag{G0.3}
(G0.3)

is a bijection of the indicated Euclidean spaces. The determinant is the absolute Jacobian. Applying G0.1 separately to all coordinates proves

∫ℝnexp⁡[−(z−m)TH(z−m)2ℏ]dz=(2πℏ)n/2det⁡H. \int_{\mathbb R^n}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(z-m)^TH(z-m)}{2\hbar}\right]dz =\frac{(2\pi\hbar)^{n/2}}{\sqrt{\det H}}. \tag{G0.4}
(G0.4)

In the probability density obtained by dividing the integrand by G0.4, the mean is mm and the covariance is

ℏR−1R−T=ℏH−1. \hbar R^{-1}R^{-T}=\hbar H^{-1}. \tag{G0.5}
(G0.5)

Indeed the transformed coordinates tit_i have product density (2π)−n/2e−∑iti2/2(2\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-\sum_i t_i^2/2}. Their means vanish; their diagonal second moments are one; their off-diagonal second moments vanish by the one-dimensional odd integrals. Transforming these moments by G0.3 proves G0.5. The polynomial moments used here are absolutely integrable, so each use of Fubini is valid.

G1. Eliminate yy, keeping all factors

Assume

M=(ABBTC)>0,A=AT∈ℝp×p,C=CT∈ℝq×q,B∈ℝp×q, M=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B^T&C\end{pmatrix}>0, \quad A=A^T\in\mathbb R^{p\times p},\quad C=C^T\in\mathbb R^{q\times q}, \quad B\in\mathbb R^{p\times q}, \tag{G1.1}
(G1.1)

where >0>0 means zTMz>0z^TMz>0 for every nonzero real column zz. The source vectors are arbitrary Jx∈ℝp,Jy∈ℝqJ_x\in\mathbb R^p,J_y\in\mathbb R^q, and

For clarity, the added xx-dependent term from completing the yy-square is

(G1.5)

Translation y↦y−my(x)y\mapsto y-m_y(x), for fixed xx, is a bijection of ℝq\mathbb R^q with Jacobian one. G0.4 therefore proves the finite positive partial integral

W(x):=∫ℝqe−S(x,y)/ℏdy=(2πℏ)q/2det⁡Cexp⁡[−12xTKx−jTx+S0−12JyTC−1Jyℏ]. W(x):=\int_{\mathbb R^q}e^{-S(x,y)/\hbar}\,dy =\frac{(2\pi\hbar)^{q/2}}{\sqrt{\det C}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{\frac12x^TKx-j^Tx+S_0-\frac12J_y^TC^{-1}J_y}{\hbar}\right]. \tag{G1.6}

Relative to the specified dimensionless Lebesgue measure dydy, define the effective action by the equality W(x)=e−Seff(x)/ℏW(x)=e^{-S_{\rm eff}(x)/\hbar}. It is exactly

Seff(x)=12xTKx−jTx+S0−12JyTC−1Jy+ℏ2log⁡det⁡(C2πℏ). S_{\rm eff}(x)=\frac12x^TKx-j^Tx+S_0-\frac12J_y^TC^{-1}J_y +\frac\hbar2\log\det\!\left(\frac{C}{2\pi\hbar}\right). \tag{G1.7}
(G1.7)

Here C/(2πℏ)C/(2\pi\hbar) is a dimensionless positive-definite matrix, so its determinant is a positive dimensionless real number. In particular the logarithm is not the logarithm of a dimensional determinant by itself. The last term is exactly −ℏlog⁡((2πℏ)q/2/det⁡C)-\hbar\log((2\pi\hbar)^{q/2}/\sqrt{\det C}). G2. Determinant, inverse, full partition and probability laws

The linear map

T(x,y)=(x,y+C−1BTx),T=(Ip0C−1BTIq) T(x,y)=(x,y+C^{-1}B^Tx),\qquad T=\begin{pmatrix}I_p&0\\C^{-1}B^T&I_q\end{pmatrix} \tag{G2.1}
(G2.1)

is invertible, with inverse obtained by changing the off-diagonal sign; both determinants are one. Matrix multiplication proves the congruence, its determinant, and its inverse:

M=TT(K00C)T,det⁡M=det⁡Cdet⁡K, M=T^T\begin{pmatrix}K&0\\0&C\end{pmatrix}T, \qquad \det M=\det C\det K, \tag{G2.2}
(G2.2)
M−1=T−1(K−100C−1)T−T=(K−1−K−1BC−1−C−1BTK−1C−1+C−1BTK−1BC−1). M^{-1}=T^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}K^{-1}&0\\0&C^{-1}\end{pmatrix}T^{-T} =\begin{pmatrix} K^{-1}&-K^{-1}BC^{-1}\\ -C^{-1}B^TK^{-1}&C^{-1}+C^{-1}B^TK^{-1}BC^{-1} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{G2.3}
(G2.3)

These equations prove the exact relation between the full quadratic form and the retained one. Conversely C>0C>0 and K>0K>0 imply M>0M>0 by G2.2, since TT maps a nonzero vector to a nonzero vector. Thus this condition is also sufficient.

Set J=(Jx,Jy)TJ=(J_x,J_y)^T, z=(x,y)Tz=(x,y)^T, and

μ=M−1J,μx=K−1j,μy=C−1(Jy−BTμx). \mu=M^{-1}J,\qquad \mu_x=K^{-1}j,\qquad \mu_y=C^{-1}(J_y-B^T\mu_x). \tag{G2.4}
(G2.4)

The last two equations follow from G2.3; equivalently they solve the two original equations Aμx+Bμy=JxA\mu_x+B\mu_y=J_x and BTμx+Cμy=JyB^T\mu_x+C\mu_y=J_y. Multiplying the inverse in G2.3 by JJ on both sides also gives

JTM−1J=JyTC−1Jy+jTK−1j. J^TM^{-1}J=J_y^TC^{-1}J_y+j^TK^{-1}j. \tag{G2.5}
(G2.5)

Indeed the right side expands to

JxTK−1Jx−2JxTK−1BC−1Jy+JyT(C−1+C−1BTK−1BC−1)Jy, J_x^TK^{-1}J_x-2J_x^TK^{-1}BC^{-1}J_y +J_y^T(C^{-1}+C^{-1}B^TK^{-1}BC^{-1})J_y,

which is the left side from G2.3. Completing the remaining square, or completing the full original square, gives

S(x,y)=12(z−μ)TM(z−μ)+S0−12JTM−1J. S(x,y)=\frac12(z-\mu)^TM(z-\mu)+S_0-\frac12J^TM^{-1}J. \tag{G2.6}
(G2.6)

The full partition integral exists and is

(G2.7)

The equality to the finite expression follows from G0.4 and G2.6. Tonelli applies before finiteness because the integrand is nonnegative; the resulting finiteness also justifies Fubini. This addresses the order of integration without any assumption on the signs of S0S_0 or the sources.

The probability density relative to dxdydx\,dy, obtained by dividing e−S/ℏe^{-S/\hbar} by ZZ, is

pX,Y(x,y)=det⁡M(2πℏ)(p+q)/2e−(z−μ)TM(z−μ)/(2ℏ). p_{X,Y}(x,y)=\frac{\sqrt{\det M}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{(p+q)/2}} e^{-(z-\mu)^TM(z-\mu)/(2\hbar)}. \tag{G2.8}
(G2.8)

Its marginal relative to dxdx and conditional density relative to dydy are

pX(x)=det⁡K(2πℏ)p/2e−(x−μx)TK(x−μx)/(2ℏ), p_X(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\det K}}{(2\pi\hbar)^{p/2}} e^{-(x-\mu_x)^TK(x-\mu_x)/(2\hbar)}, \tag{G2.9}
(G2.9)

To prove these are the asserted laws, divide G1.6 by G2.7 and complete the xx-square for G2.9. The quotient of G2.8 by the strictly positive G2.9 equals G2.10 by G1.4. G0.4 makes G2.10 integrate to one for every xx. Its coefficients depend continuously on xx, so it defines a measurable probability kernel. Thus the formula is a conditional version valid at every xx, even though conditional versions are generally only determined almost everywhere.

G0.5 now proves

In G2.13, Cov⁡(X,Y)\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) denotes the covariance of the concatenated column (X,Y)T(X,Y)^T. The cross-covariance block itself is −ℏK−1BC−1-\hbar K^{-1}BC^{-1}.

G4. Elimination in two stages is associative
