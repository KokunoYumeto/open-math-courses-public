Oscillators and free quantum fields

Lesson YM-F11 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

Classical evolution assigns a field to each time once its initial data are given. A quantum description assigns probabilities to measurements using a state and observables. The first purpose of this lesson is to make those words mathematical. Its second purpose is to construct a quantum field whose states, observables and Hamiltonian we can write and analyze completely.

We start with one harmonic oscillator. Its full solution supplies a family of states and the operators that move between them. Fourier coordinates then turn a finite free scalar field into finitely many oscillators. Finally we construct the spaces and operators needed when the number of modes becomes infinite. Every passage specifies its space, measure and operator domain.

Read the field, coordinate and unit conventions in Lesson 1 and the scalar action in Lesson 7. The needed Fourier and Hilbert-space arguments are provided below. The earlier measure and integration chapter supplies the complete integral convergence results used in those arguments. The scalar field is a first quantum example; its action is the explicitly specified free neutral real restriction of F7.41. Gauge constraints enter the next lesson.

0. States, observables and probabilities

A complex Hilbert space is a complete complex vector space with a positive inner product. We take the inner product to be conjugate-linear in its first slot and linear in its second. A pure quantum state is represented by a vector ψ\psi of norm one. Multiplying that vector by a complex number of modulus one represents the same state.

For a particle coordinate qq, the space used below is L2(ℝ,dq;ℂ)L^2(\mathbb R,dq;\mathbb C). We specify the position measurement rule by assigning to a measurable set B⊂ℝB\subset\mathbb R the probability

ℙψ(q∈B)=∫B|ψ(q)|2dq. {\mathbb P}_\psi(q\in B)=\int_B|\psi(q)|^2\,dq. \tag{F11.1}
(F11.1)

This is the Born rule in the position representation. It is part of the quantum model, rather than a theorem deduced from a classical equation. It does define a probability measure: the integral is nonnegative, the whole-line value is one, and countable additivity follows by applying monotone convergence to disjoint indicator functions. A constant phase leaves every such probability unchanged.

An observable will be a self-adjoint operator on a specified dense domain in the Hilbert space. For a bounded diagonal operator with orthonormal basis ene_n, real diagonal entries ono_n and state ψ=∑ncnen\psi=\sum_n c_n e_n, its measurement probabilities and expectation are

ℙψ(o∈B)=∑{n:on∈B}|cn|2,⟨o⟩ψ=⟨ψ,Oψ⟩=∑non|cn|2. {\mathbb P}_\psi(o\in B)=\sum_{\{n:o_n\in B\}}|c_n|^2,\qquad \langle o\rangle_\psi=\langle\psi,O\psi\rangle =\sum_n o_n|c_n|^2. \tag{F11.2}
(F11.2)

Orthonormality proves that the squared coefficients sum to one and proves the expectation identity by expanding the inner product. Repeated eigenvalues contribute the sum of their probabilities. For the unbounded Hamiltonians below, their exact domains ensure that the displayed operator and expectation exist. A finite variance is expressed as ∥(O−⟨o⟩ψ)ψ∥2\|(O-\langle o\rangle_\psi)\psi\|^2 for a state in D(O)D(O); this definition does not presume that O2ψO^2\psi exists.

Here a domain D(T)D(T) is a linear subspace on which an operator TT is defined. It is dense if every Hilbert-space vector is a norm limit of domain vectors. For a densely defined TT, its adjoint domain consists of those ff for which there is a vector gg satisfying ⟨f,Tv⟩=⟨g,v⟩\langle f,Tv\rangle=\langle g,v\rangle for every v∈D(T)v\in D(T); then T*f=gT^*f=g. Density makes gg unique. The operator is self-adjoint when D(T)=D(T*)D(T)=D(T^*) and their values agree. Checking an integration-by-parts identity on some smooth functions establishes symmetry there, but the domain equality needs its own proof. Sections 1–3 provide that proof for the actual operators used.

A mixed state may already be illustrated by a finite ensemble: choose unit vectors ψj\psi_j and numbers pj≥0p_j\ge0 with ∑jpj=1\sum_jp_j=1, and average their measurement probabilities using those weights. Its operator is 𝜚f=∑jpjψj⟨ψj,f⟩\varrho f=\sum_jp_j\psi_j\langle\psi_j,f\rangle. For any ff, its quadratic form is ⟨f,𝜚f⟩=∑jpj|⟨ψj,f⟩|2≥0\langle f,\varrho f\rangle=\sum_jp_j|\langle\psi_j,f\rangle|^2\ge0. Taking a complete orthonormal basis and summing diagonal entries gives trace one, by each ψj\psi_j’s squared-coefficient identity. For any bounded observable OO, direct expansion gives tr⁡(𝜚O)=∑jpj⟨ψj,Oψj⟩\operatorname{tr}(\varrho O)=\sum_jp_j\langle\psi_j,O\psi_j\rangle. This makes the probability mixture precise without treating it as a superposition vector.

Time evolution below is a strongly continuous unitary group U(t)=e−itH/ℏU(t)=e^{-itH/\hbar}, with HH its specified self-adjoint Hamiltonian. We will construct this group from explicit diagonal or multiplication formulas. Unitarity preserves total probability; the factor ℏ\hbar turns energy times time into a dimensionless phase. A state’s probabilities can change because different energy components acquire different phases.

Sections 7–10 construct the infinite-mode spaces, spectra and smeared fields. Section 11 provides the exact human-source comparison. The worked examples and complete exercises then let you use each construction on explicit states and test functions.

A Schwartz function on ℝd\mathbb R^d is smooth and has sup⁡x|x1a1⋯xdad∂1b1⋯∂dbdf(x)|<∞\sup_x|x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_d^{a_d} \partial_1^{b_1}\cdots\partial_d^{b_d}f(x)|<\infty for every choice of nonnegative integer powers aj,bja_j,b_j. 1. Hilbert spaces, Fourier facts and multiplication operators

The one-particle quantum oscillator Hilbert space is ℋ=L2(ℝ,dq;ℂ)\mathcal H=L^2(\mathbb R,dq;\mathbb C), with ⟨f,g⟩=∫f¯gdq\langle f,g\rangle=\int\overline f g\,dq, linear in the second slot. Vectors agreeing almost everywhere are identified. Completeness can be seen directly: from a Cauchy sequence choose a subsequence whose successive L2 differences have summable norms. The sum of their absolute values has finite L2 norm by the triangle inequality and monotone convergence, hence is finite almost everywhere. The pointwise series defines a limit, and the same tail bound proves L2 convergence. The original Cauchy sequence has this limit too.

We use the full Fourier conventions

f̂(k)=∫ℝde−ik⋅xf(x)ddx,ℱf=(2π)−d/2f̂,∥f∥22=(2π)−d∥f̂∥22. \widehat f(k)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}e^{-ik\cdot x}f(x)\,d^dx, \quad\mathcal Ff=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\widehat f, \quad\|f\|_2^2=(2\pi)^{-d}\|\widehat f\|_2^2. \tag{QF.1}
(QF.1)

Here is a sufficient proof of the Fourier facts used below. The one-dimensional Gaussian integral is ∫e−ax2dx=π/a\int e^{-a x^2}dx=\sqrt{\pi/a}, a>0a>0: square the integral, integrate in polar coordinates, then take its positive square root. Differentiation and completing the square, or the differential equation obtained by integrating a derivative in x, give e−a|x|2̂(k)=(π/a)d/2e−|k|2/(4a)\widehat{e^{-a|x|^2}}(k)=(\pi/a)^{d/2}e^{-|k|^2/(4a)}. For Schwartz f,g, insert e−ϵ|k|2e^{-\epsilon|k|^2} in (2π)−d∫f̂¯ĝ(2\pi)^{-d}\int\overline{\widehat f}\widehat g. Fubini and the Gaussian formula turn this into ⟨f,Gϵ*g⟩\langle f,G_\epsilon*g\rangle, where Gϵ(x)=(4πϵ)−d/2e−|x|2/(4ϵ)G_\epsilon(x)=(4\pi\epsilon)^{-d/2}e^{-|x|^2/(4\epsilon)}. Translation continuity and its unit integral give Gϵ*g→gG_\epsilon*g\to g in L2. Dominated convergence on the Fourier side proves QF.1 on Schwartz functions. Integration by parts makes their transforms Schwartz, and the same Gaussian argument proves Fourier inversion. Smooth compact functions are dense in L2, by cutoff and mollification. Thus the transform extends to a unitary map on L2, since its isometric range contains the dense Schwartz space and is closed. The same Gaussian convolution proves L1 Fourier uniqueness: if an L1 function has zero transform, all its Gaussian convolutions vanish, and their L1 approximate-identity limit is the function itself.

For any real measurable finite function b on a sigma-finite measure space, define

D(Mb)={f∈L2:bf∈L2},Mbf=bf. D(M_b)=\{f\in L^2:bf\in L^2\},\qquad M_bf=bf. \tag{QF.2}
(QF.2)

This is densely defined, because truncating to |b|≤n|b|\le n gives domain vectors converging to every f. It is self-adjoint: symmetry is direct, and testing an adjoint-domain vector against functions supported where |b|≤n|b|\le n identifies its adjoint image as bf; letting n increase shows bf lies in L2. If z has positive distance from the essential range of b, multiplication by (b−z)−1(b-z)^{-1} is a bounded inverse, whose image lies in D(M_b) since b/(b−z)=1+z/(b−z)b/(b-z)=1+z/(b-z). Conversely, every neighbourhood of a point in that essential range has a positive-measure preimage. A unit vector supported in a finite positive-measure part of this preimage is an approximate eigenvector. A bounded inverse there is impossible. This proves the spectrum is the closed essential range, and an eigenvalue occurs precisely when its level set has positive measure. The same proof applies to a counting measure and to a direct sum of measure spaces. It supplies all self-adjointness and spectral claims about multiplication operators used in this lesson.

The position operator Q is multiplication by q on {f:qf∈L2}\{f:qf\in L^2\}. For physical momentum use the unitary transform

(ℱℏf)(p)=(2πℏ)−1/2∫e−ipq/ℏf(q)dq,P=ℱℏ−1Mpℱℏ. (\mathcal F_\hbar f)(p)=(2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2} \int e^{-ipq/\hbar}f(q)\,dq, \qquad P=\mathcal F_\hbar^{-1}M_p\mathcal F_\hbar. \tag{QF.3}
(QF.3)

It follows from QF.1 by the exact change of integration variable p=ℏkp=\hbar k, with its Jacobian. Thus P is self-adjoint on H1(ℝ)={f∈L2:f′∈L2}H^1(\mathbb R)=\{f\in L^2:f'\in L^2\}, acting as −iℏf′-i\hbar f' distributionally. On the invariant Schwartz domain [Q,P]f=iℏf[Q,P]f=i\hbar f. This commutator is a domain statement about unbounded operators, not an identity of bounded operators on all L2.

3. Closed ladders, the Hamiltonian domain and its full spectrum

The coefficient map 𝒰f=(cn)n≥0\mathcal U f=(c_n)_{n\ge0}, cn=⟨ψn,f⟩c_n=\langle\psi_n,f\rangle, is unitary onto ℓ2\ell^2. Define the closed ladders by their full coefficient domains:

(QF.7)

Both domains are the same set, with different displayed graph norms. Coordinate testing proves these are adjoints of each other; convergence of each coordinate and of the output in ℓ2\ell^2 proves closedness. Finite sequences approximate in each graph norm, so the finite Hermite span is a core. Testing against ψn\psi_n identifies these operators with the differential expressions on Schwartz functions. Moreover

(QF.8)

For finite Hermite sums this is integration by parts in QF.4. Graph approximation then proves one inclusion and the identity: the right side makes derivatives and q-multiples Cauchy and testing identifies their limits. Conversely, for f in the right-hand domain, the differential expression a f lies in L2; testing it against the Hermite basis identifies its coefficients as QF.7, which supplies the other inclusion. Boundary integrations can equivalently be justified by cutoff and H1 approximation, whose weighted errors tend to zero.

4. The original real scalar action and a finite torus regulator
