Symmetry through matrices

Lesson YM-F03 · Geometry and states in Yang–Mills theory

A rotation changes a vector’s components while preserving its length. A complex phase changes a complex number while preserving its absolute value. Matrices put both operations into a common language. They also record a feature that ordinary numbers can conceal: the order of two transformations can matter.

We will build that language from arithmetic, then apply it to fields. The final calculations connect complex phases directly to the gauge transformations of Lesson 2, including the domain with a missing axis.

Preparation is Lesson 1, the gauge formulas of Lesson 2, and elementary trigonometry. We use the usual sine and cosine, their derivatives, their addition formulas and their parametrization of the unit circle with period 2π2\pi. No prior abstract algebra or quantum mechanics is assumed.

1. Complex numbers and phases

A complex number is a pair of real numbers, written z=a+ibz=a+ib, with the multiplication rule i2=−1i^2=-1. The operations are

(a+ib)+(c+id)=(a+c)+i(b+d),(a+ib)(c+id)=(ac−bd)+i(ad+bc),a+ib¯=a−ib,|a+ib|2=a2+b2. \begin{aligned} (a+ib)+(c+id)&=(a+c)+i(b+d),\\ (a+ib)(c+id)&=(ac-bd)+i(ad+bc),\\ \overline{a+ib}&=a-ib,\qquad |a+ib|^2=a^2+b^2. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.1}
(F3.1)

The bar means complex conjugation, and |z||z| is the nonnegative square root of |z|2|z|^2. The real numbers are the pairs with second component zero.

These rules make addition and multiplication associative and distributive. Here is the multiplication check that will matter for matrices. For z=a+ibz=a+ib, w=c+idw=c+id, and u=e+ifu=e+if, both (zw)u(zw)u and z(wu)z(wu) have real part

ace−bde−adf−bcf ace-bde-adf-bcf \tag{F3.2}
(F3.2)

and imaginary part

acf−bdf+ade+bce. acf-bdf+ade+bce. \tag{F3.3}
(F3.3)

Expanding (F3.1) in either order gives those four terms. Addition and distributivity follow by expanding the two real coordinates. The multiplicative identity is 1+0i1+0i. If z≠0z\ne0, then

z−1=z¯|z|2=a−iba2+b2,zz−1=z−1z=1. z^{-1}=\frac{\overline z}{|z|^2} =\frac{a-ib}{a^2+b^2},\qquad zz^{-1}=z^{-1}z=1. \tag{F3.4}
(F3.4)

The denominator is strictly positive. Zero has no multiplicative inverse, because (F3.1) makes its product with every number zero.

Conjugation preserves sums and products, as direct expansion shows. In particular,

|zw|2=(ac−bd)2+(ad+bc)2=a2c2−2abcd+b2d2+a2d2+2abcd+b2c2=(a2+b2)(c2+d2)=|z|2|w|2. \begin{aligned} |zw|^2 &=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2\\ &=a^2c^2-2abcd+b^2d^2+a^2d^2+2abcd+b^2c^2\\ &=(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)=|z|^2|w|^2. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.5}
(F3.5)

Taking nonnegative square roots gives |zw|=|z||w||zw|=|z|\,|w|.

For a real angle θ\theta, write

p(θ)=eiθ=cos⁡θ+isin⁡θ. p(\theta)=e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta. \tag{F3.6}
(F3.6)

This defines the notation eiθe^{i\theta} needed in this lesson. The trigonometric identities and derivatives give

|p(θ)|=1,p(θ+η)=p(θ)p(η),p(−θ)=p(θ)¯=p(θ)−1,ddθp(θ)=ip(θ). \begin{aligned} |p(\theta)|&=1,& p(\theta+\eta)&=p(\theta)p(\eta),\\ p(-\theta)&=\overline{p(\theta)}=p(\theta)^{-1},& \frac d{d\theta}p(\theta)&=i p(\theta). \end{aligned} \tag{F3.7}
(F3.7)

Every complex number of absolute value one is p(θ)p(\theta) for some θ\theta. Its angle is determined modulo 2π2\pi: p(θ)=p(η)p(\theta)=p(\eta) exactly when θ−η∈2πℤ\theta-\eta\in2\pi\mathbb Z. Here ℤ\mathbb Z denotes the integers, including zero and negative integers.

A phase is a number of absolute value one. Multiplying by a phase preserves |z||z| by (F3.5). For z=a+ibz=a+ib, its complete effect on the real coordinates is

p(θ)z=(acos⁡θ−bsin⁡θ)+i(asin⁡θ+bcos⁡θ). p(\theta)z =(a\cos\theta-b\sin\theta) +i(a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta). \tag{F3.8}
(F3.8)

Thus a phase is exactly a rotation of the real coordinate pair.

2. Matrices are maps with a specified order

Throughout the matrix calculations, the sizes m,n,rm,n,r are positive integers.

A complex mm-by-nn matrix M=(Mab)M=(M_{ab}) represents the map ℂn→ℂm\mathbb C^n\to\mathbb C^m given by

(Mv)a=∑b=1nMabvb(1≤a≤m). (Mv)^a=\sum_{b=1}^n M_{ab}v^b\qquad(1\le a\le m). \tag{F3.9}
(F3.9)

The first index is the row and output component; the second is the column and input component. A vector here is a column. A map is complex-linear when it preserves addition and multiplication by every complex scalar. Formula (F3.9) has both properties by distributivity.

If NN is nn-by-rr, composition has the matrix

(MN)ac=∑b=1nMabNbc,MN:ℂr→ℂm. (MN)_{ac}=\sum_{b=1}^n M_{ab}N_{bc}, \qquad MN:\mathbb C^r\longrightarrow\mathbb C^m. \tag{F3.10}
(F3.10)

The rightmost matrix acts first. Indeed, (M(Nv))a=∑b∑cMabNbcvc=(MNv)a(M(Nv))^a=\sum_b\sum_c M_{ab}N_{bc}v^c=(MNv)^a. All sums are finite, so changing their order uses only distributivity.

For compatible matrices L,M,NL,M,N, associativity follows entry by entry:

((LM)N)ad=∑c∑bLabMbcNcd=∑b∑cLabMbcNcd=(L(MN))ad. ((LM)N)_{ad} =\sum_c\sum_b L_{ab}M_{bc}N_{cd} =\sum_b\sum_c L_{ab}M_{bc}N_{cd} =(L(MN))_{ad}. \tag{F3.11}
(F3.11)

Each sum ranges over its indicated intermediate coordinate space. Matrix addition and scalar multiplication are entrywise, and the same finite-sum calculation proves distributivity.

The nn-by-nn identity InI_n has entries (In)ab=1(I_n)_{ab}=1 if a=ba=b, and zero otherwise. A square matrix MM is invertible if a matrix M−1M^{-1} satisfies MM−1=M−1M=InMM^{-1}=M^{-1}M=I_n. Its inverse is unique: if LL is a left inverse and RR a right inverse, then L=L(MR)=(LM)R=RL=L(MR)=(LM)R=R.

Lemma 2.1. A coordinate fact about square matrices

If an nn-by-nn matrix MM has no nonzero vector in its kernel, then it is invertible.

Proof. Its columns v1,…,vnv_1,\ldots,v_n are independent: ∑jajvj=0\sum_j a_jv_j=0 means M(a1,…,an)T=0M(a_1,\ldots,a_n)^T=0, so every coefficient is zero. We prove by induction that nn independent vectors in ℂn\mathbb C^n span it. For n=1n=1, the one nonzero coordinate is invertible by (F3.4).

For n>1n>1, some coordinate of v1v_1 is nonzero. Reorder coordinates to make it the first. For j=2,…,nj=2,\ldots,n, set wj=vj−(vj1/v11)v1w_j=v_j-(v_j^1/v_1^1)v_1. These vectors have zero first coordinate. They are independent, since a relation among them would give a relation among the original vjv_j. Their last n−1n-1 coordinates therefore form an independent list in ℂn−1\mathbb C^{n-1} and span it by induction. For any v∈ℂnv\in\mathbb C^n, the vector v−(v1/v11)v1v-(v^1/v_1^1)v_1 has zero first coordinate, so is a combination of the wjw_j. It follows that vv is a combination of the original columns. Independence makes its coefficients unique.

Thus the map v↦Mvv\mapsto Mv is bijective. The inverse is linear: applying MM to a sum or scalar multiple of inverse images gives the required sum or scalar multiple, and uniqueness identifies the inverse image. Its values on the coordinate vectors give an inverse matrix with both products equal to InI_n. ▫\square

A real rotation matrix for every phase

Define

R(θ)=(cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ),F(a+ib)=(ab). R(\theta)= \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad F(a+ib)=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}. \tag{F3.12}
(F3.12)

Equation (F3.8) is the exact map identity

F(p(θ)z)=R(θ)F(z). F(p(\theta)z)=R(\theta)F(z). \tag{F3.13}
(F3.13)

The trigonometric addition formulas, or composition in (F3.13), give R(θ)R(η)=R(θ+η)R(\theta)R(\eta)=R(\theta+\eta). Its inverse is R(−θ)R(-\theta). Expanding the two coordinates of R(θ)(a,b)TR(\theta)(a,b)^T gives

(acos⁡θ−bsin⁡θ)2+(asin⁡θ+bcos⁡θ)2=a2+b2; (a\cos\theta-b\sin\theta)^2 +(a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta)^2=a^2+b^2; \tag{F3.14}
(F3.14)

the cross terms cancel and each coefficient of a2,b2a^2,b^2 is cos⁡2θ+sin⁡2θ=1\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1.

3. Adjoints, lengths and unitary matrices

For v,w∈ℂnv,w\in\mathbb C^n, define

⟨v,w⟩=∑a=1nva¯wa,∥v∥2=⟨v,v⟩=∑a=1n|va|2. \langle v,w\rangle=\sum_{a=1}^n\overline{v^a}w^a, \qquad \|v\|^2=\langle v,v\rangle =\sum_{a=1}^n|v^a|^2. \tag{F3.15}
(F3.15)

The first variable is conjugate-linear and the second is linear. This choice fixes the signs in later identities. The squared norm is nonnegative and is zero exactly when every coordinate is zero.

The adjoint of an mm-by-nn matrix is the nn-by-mm matrix

(M†)ba=Mab¯. (M^\dagger)_{ba}=\overline{M_{ab}}. \tag{F3.16}
(F3.16)

It satisfies

⟨Mv,w⟩=⟨v,M†w⟩,(MN)†=N†M†,(M†)†=M. \langle Mv,w\rangle=\langle v,M^\dagger w\rangle, \qquad (MN)^\dagger=N^\dagger M^\dagger, \qquad (M^\dagger)^\dagger=M. \tag{F3.17}
(F3.17)

For the first equality, expand its left side as ∑a∑bMab¯vb¯wa\sum_a\sum_b\overline{M_{ab}}\,\overline{v^b}w^a and group by bb. For the second, (MN)ac¯=∑bNbc¯Mab¯\overline{(MN)_{ac}}=\sum_b\overline{N_{bc}}\, \overline{M_{ab}}, which is precisely the (c,a)(c,a) entry of N†M†N^\dagger M^\dagger. The last equality follows from conjugating twice and transposing twice.

Theorem 3.1. Equivalent tests for unitarity

For a square matrix UU, the following conditions are equivalent: it preserves the inner product; it preserves the norm of every vector; it satisfies U†U=InU^\dagger U=I_n. When they hold, UU is invertible, U−1=U†U^{-1}=U^\dagger, and UU†=InUU^\dagger=I_n. Such a matrix is called unitary.

Proof. Inner-product preservation gives norm preservation by setting the two arguments equal. Conversely, direct expansion of (F3.15) gives the polarization identity

⟨v,w⟩=14(∥v+w∥2−∥v−w∥2−i∥v+iw∥2+i∥v−iw∥2). \langle v,w\rangle=\frac14\left( \|v+w\|^2-\|v-w\|^2 -i\|v+iw\|^2+i\|v-iw\|^2\right). \tag{F3.18}
(F3.18)

The first difference is 4Re⁡⟨v,w⟩4\operatorname{Re}\langle v,w\rangle; the difference of the last two norms before multiplication by −i-i is −4Im⁡⟨v,w⟩-4\operatorname{Im}\langle v,w\rangle. Thus preservation of every norm, together with complex linearity, preserves the inner product.

By (F3.17), inner-product preservation is ⟨v,(U†U−In)w⟩=0\langle v,(U^\dagger U-I_n)w\rangle=0 for every v,wv,w. Taking coordinate vectors for v,wv,w proves that every entry of U†U−InU^\dagger U-I_n is zero. Conversely that matrix equality immediately preserves the inner product.

Finally, Uv=0Uv=0 implies v=U†Uv=0v=U^\dagger Uv=0, so Lemma 2.1 gives an inverse. The left inverse U†U^\dagger must equal it by uniqueness of inverses. Therefore UU†=InUU^\dagger=I_n too. ▫\square

A real unitary matrix is called orthogonal; its adjoint is its ordinary transpose. The matrices R(θ)R(\theta) in (F3.12) are orthogonal by (F3.14). For a two-by-two real matrix (abcd)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right), the determinant is ad−bcad-bc. The determinant of R(θ)R(\theta) is one.

In fact every orthogonal two-by-two matrix of determinant one is R(θ)R(\theta). To check this, write its first column as (a,b)T(a,b)^T, with a2+b2=1a^2+b^2=1. A unit column perpendicular to it must be σ(−b,a)T\sigma(-b,a)^T, with σ=1\sigma=1 or −1-1: the perpendicular space is spanned by (−b,a)T(-b,a)^T. Indeed, if au+bv=0au+bv=0 and a≠0a\ne0, then (u,v)=(v/a)(−b,a)(u,v)=(v/a)(-b,a). If a=0a=0, then b≠0b\ne0, v=0v=0, and (u,0)=(−u/b)(−b,0)(u,0)=(-u/b)(-b,0). The unit norm forces the real coefficient to have absolute value one. The determinant is σ(a2+b2)=σ\sigma(a^2+b^2)=\sigma. Determinant one therefore selects the column (−b,a)T(-b,a)^T. Choose θ\theta with a=cos⁡θ,b=sin⁡θa=\cos\theta,b=\sin\theta, giving (F3.12).

4. Groups, representations and order

A group is a set with an associative operation, an identity, and an inverse for each element. The identity acts without changing an element, and the inverse undoes the operation. Commutativity is not required. A group is called abelian when all pairs commute.

Invertible nn-by-nn complex matrices form a group GL⁡n(ℂ)\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb C) under multiplication. The identity and associativity were proved above, and the inverse of MNMN is N−1M−1N^{-1}M^{-1}: multiplying in either order and using associativity gives InI_n.

The unitary matrices form a group U(n)U(n). Indeed,

(UV)†(UV)=V†U†UV=V†V=In, (UV)^\dagger(UV)=V^\dagger U^\dagger UV=V^\dagger V=I_n, \tag{F3.19}
(F3.19)

and the identity and inverse are unitary. For the inverse, use (U†)†U†=UU†=In(U^\dagger)^\dagger U^\dagger=UU^\dagger=I_n. The group U(1)U(1) is the complex unit circle from Section 1. It is abelian because complex multiplication commutes.

A representation of a group GG on ℂn\mathbb C^n is a map π:G→GL⁡n(ℂ)\pi:G\to\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb C) satisfying

π(gh)=π(g)π(h),π(e)=In. \pi(gh)=\pi(g)\pi(h),\qquad \pi(e)=I_n. \tag{F3.20}
(F3.20)

These equations make (g,v)↦π(g)v(g,v)\mapsto\pi(g)v a group action: applying hh, then gg, has the same effect as applying ghgh. They also imply π(g−1)=π(g)−1\pi(g^{-1})=\pi(g)^{-1}. A representation is unitary when its values are unitary matrices, and faithful when different group elements give different matrices. Representations on real coordinate spaces are defined in the same way with real matrices.

Here are three exact examples:

  1. The action of U(n)U(n) on ℂn\mathbb C^n by its defining matrices is unitary and faithful.
  2. For any integer mm, z↦zmz\mapsto z^m is a one-dimensional unitary representation of U(1)U(1). Negative powers use (F3.4). The identity (zw)m=zmwm(zw)^m=z^mw^m holds by repeated multiplication for nonnegative mm and by inverses for negative mm.
  3. The map a+ib↦(a−bba)a+ib\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{smallmatrix}\right) for a2+b2=1a^2+b^2=1 is a faithful real representation onto the orthogonal two-by-two matrices of determinant one. The exact intertwining map is FF in (F3.13), and Section 3 proved surjectivity onto that set.

Two three-dimensional rotations that do not commute

Consider the actual matrices

Q3=(0−10100001),Q1=(10000−1010). Q_3=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}. \tag{F3.21}
(F3.21)

They send (x1,x2,x3)(x^1,x^2,x^3) to (−x2,x1,x3)(-x^2,x^1,x^3) and (x1,−x3,x2)(x^1,-x^3,x^2), respectively. Each preserves the sum of the three coordinate squares and is therefore orthogonal. They are right-angle rotations about the third and first coordinate axes. Multiplication gives

Q3Q1=(001100010),Q1Q3=(0−1000−1100). Q_3Q_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q_1Q_3=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}. \tag{F3.22}
(F3.22)

For the same vector v=(1,2,3)Tv=(1,2,3)^T, the two full calculations are

v↦Q1(1,−3,2)T↦Q3(3,1,2)T,v↦Q3(−2,1,3)T↦Q1(−2,−3,1)T. \begin{aligned} v&\overset{Q_1}{\longmapsto}(1,-3,2)^T\overset{Q_3}{\longmapsto}(3,1,2)^T,\\ v&\overset{Q_3}{\longmapsto}(-2,1,3)^T\overset{Q_1}{\longmapsto}(-2,-3,1)^T. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.23}

The final vectors differ. Both have squared length 1414, the same as the initial vector; norm preservation does not imply that the operations commute.

What the difference of the two orders satisfies

For square matrices define the commutator [M,N]=MN−NM[M,N]=MN-NM. It is bilinear, [M,M]=0[M,M]=0, and [M,N]=−[N,M][M,N]=-[N,M], directly by distributivity. It also satisfies

[L,[M,N]]+[M,[N,L]]+[N,[L,M]]=0. [L,[M,N]]+[M,[N,L]]+[N,[L,M]]=0. \tag{F3.24}
(F3.24)

Here is the full proof, reusing the associative-algebra calculation of RT-LIE-01, Proposition 1.1, in this matrix setting. Its required associativity is (F3.11). The three terms expand to

[L,[M,N]]=LMN−LNM−MNL+NML,[M,[N,L]]=MNL−MLN−NLM+LNM,[N,[L,M]]=NLM−NML−LMN+MLN. \begin{aligned} [L,[M,N]]&=LMN-LNM-MNL+NML,\\ [M,[N,L]]&=MNL-MLN-NLM+LNM,\\ [N,[L,M]]&=NLM-NML-LMN+MLN. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.25}
(F3.25)

Each ordered product has one positive and one negative occurrence, so their sum is zero. This is the Jacobi identity. 5. Constant actions, moving frames and local transformations

Let ψ:I×Ω→ℂn\psi:I\times\Omega\to\mathbb C^n be a smooth field, where II is an open interval and Ω⊆ℝ3\Omega\subseteq\mathbb R^3 is open. The superscript on ψa\psi^a labels an internal component, 1≤a≤n1\le a\le n; it is not a spatial coordinate. All components have the same physical unit, if a physical unit has been assigned. The transformation matrices are dimensionless.

A constant matrix U∈U(n)U\in U(n) acts by ψ′=Uψ\psi'=U\psi. This is often called a global internal transformation: the same matrix is used at every point. It preserves ∥ψ(t,x)∥2\|\psi(t,x)\|^2 pointwise by Theorem 3.1. Whether it is a symmetry of a dynamical model also depends on that model’s equations and any imposed boundary or initial conditions.

A complete equation test

Fix a speed c>0c>0 and a constant λ≥0\lambda\ge0 with unit inverse length. Define the componentwise differential operator

Lψ=1c2∂t2ψ−∑i=13∂i2ψ+λ2ψ. L\psi=\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\psi -\sum_{i=1}^3\partial_i^2\psi+\lambda^2\psi. \tag{F3.26}
(F3.26)

Every term has the unit of ψ\psi divided by length squared. For any constant matrix MM, finite-sum differentiation gives L(Mψ)=M(Lψ)L(M\psi)=M(L\psi). Thus any constant invertible MM maps solutions of Lψ=0L\psi=0 bijectively to solutions, with inverse transformation M−1M^{-1}. This equation alone has a larger constant-matrix invariance than U(n)U(n): every element of GL⁡n(ℂ)\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb C) works. The unitary subgroup additionally preserves the chosen Hermitian norm. Initial data transform by the same matrix; a requirement to fix particular data is an additional restriction.

Now let U:I×Ω→U(n)U:I\times\Omega\to U(n) vary smoothly. The product rule gives, for each coordinate derivative ∂μ\partial_\mu,

∂μ(Uψ)=U∂μψ+(∂μU)ψ,∂0=∂t,μ=0,1,2,3. \partial_\mu(U\psi) =U\partial_\mu\psi+(\partial_\mu U)\psi, \qquad \partial_0=\partial_t,\quad \mu=0,1,2,3. \tag{F3.27}
(F3.27)

This identity follows componentwise from ∂μ∑bUabψb=∑b(Uab∂μψb+(∂μUab)ψb)\partial_\mu\sum_b U_{ab}\psi^b =\sum_b(U_{ab}\partial_\mu\psi^b +(\partial_\mu U_{ab})\psi^b). Applying it twice retains all terms in the equation:

L(Uψ)=U(Lψ)+(1c2∂t2U−∑i=13∂i2U)ψ+2c2(∂tU)∂tψ−2∑i=13(∂iU)∂iψ. \begin{aligned} L(U\psi) &=U(L\psi) +\left(\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2U -\sum_{i=1}^3\partial_i^2U\right)\psi\\ &\quad+\frac2{c^2}(\partial_tU)\partial_t\psi -2\sum_{i=1}^3(\partial_iU)\partial_i\psi. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.28}
(F3.28)

The λ2\lambda^2 term is already included in U(Lψ)U(L\psi). For a concrete failure of local invariance, take n=1n=1, λ=0\lambda=0, ψ=1\psi=1, and U=eikx1U=e^{ikx^1} with real k≠0k\ne0 of unit inverse length. Then Lψ=0L\psi=0, but

L(Uψ)=k2eikx1≠0. L(U\psi)=k^2e^{ikx^1}\ne0. \tag{F3.29}
(F3.29)

Pointwise norm preservation still holds, but this specified equation has not been preserved.

The spatial derivative quantity also displays the complete change:

∑i=13∥∂i(Uψ)∥2=∑i=13[∥∂iψ∥2+2Re⟨U∂iψ,(∂iU)ψ⟩+∥(∂iU)ψ∥2]. \begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^3\|\partial_i(U\psi)\|^2 &=\sum_{i=1}^3\left[ \|\partial_i\psi\|^2 +2\operatorname{Re}\langle U\partial_i\psi, (\partial_iU)\psi\rangle +\|(\partial_iU)\psi\|^2\right]. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.30}
(F3.30)

To prove it, substitute (F3.27), expand the Hermitian norm, use ⟨b,a⟩=⟨a,b⟩¯\langle b,a\rangle=\overline{\langle a,b\rangle}, and use unitarity on U∂iψU\partial_i\psi. The cross term and the last nonnegative term are both required.

The exact relation to a change of frame

An orthonormal frame at a point is an ordered orthonormal basis b1,…,bnb_1,\ldots,b_n of ℂn\mathbb C^n. Put these vectors in the columns of a matrix BB. Its column inner products give B†B=InB^\dagger B=I_n, so it is unitary. A vector with component column ψ\psi in that frame is W=BψW=B\psi.

If the frame and components are changed together by

B′=BU−1,ψ′=Uψ,thenB′ψ′=Bψ=W. B'=BU^{-1},\qquad \psi'=U\psi, \quad\hbox{then}\quad B'\psi'=B\psi=W. \tag{F3.31}
(F3.31)

This is an exact identity, including when the frames vary with (t,x)(t,x). In contrast, keeping BB fixed and applying the matrix to the vector’s components sends WW to BUψBU\psi, which can be a different vector. The formulas specify whether a matrix changes the vector or its description. They also explain why derivatives of a varying frame matter: ∂μW=(∂μB)ψ+B∂μψ\partial_\mu W=(\partial_\mu B)\psi+B\partial_\mu\psi. The field’s internal components and the spatial coordinate labels have been kept separate throughout this calculation.

6. The electromagnetic phase and its exact derivative

We can now connect (F3.27) directly to the potentials in Lesson 2. Let qq be a real charge and let ℏ>0\hbar>0 be a constant with the unit of action, Nms\mathrm{N\,m\,s}. The familiar physical use of this constant is Planck’s constant divided by 2π2\pi; no numerical value or quantization postulate is needed for the identities below.

For a real gauge function χ\chi with the units fixed in Lesson 2, qχ/ℏq\chi/\hbar is dimensionless. Define

Uχ=eiqχ/ℏ,ψ′=Uχψ,Ai′=Ai+∂iχ,ϕ′=ϕ−∂tχ. U_\chi=e^{iq\chi/\hbar},\qquad \psi'=U_\chi\psi,\qquad A_i'=A_i+\partial_i\chi,\qquad \phi'=\phi-\partial_t\chi. \tag{F3.32}
(F3.32)

Here ψ\psi is a complex scalar field. In the Cartesian coordinates of this lesson, AiA_i denotes the same numerical spatial component as AiA^i in Lesson 2; no spacetime index has been lowered using a Lorentzian metric.

Introduce the operators on smooth complex functions

Diψ=∂iψ−iqℏAiψ(i=1,2,3),Dtψ=∂tψ+iqℏϕψ. D_i\psi=\partial_i\psi-\frac{iq}{\hbar}A_i\psi \quad(i=1,2,3),\qquad D_t\psi=\partial_t\psi+\frac{iq}{\hbar}\phi\psi. \tag{F3.33}
(F3.33)

The coefficients qAi/ℏqA_i/\hbar and qϕ/ℏq\phi/\hbar have units inverse length and inverse time, respectively. These operators are called covariant derivatives because their transformation law is the following exact one.

Theorem 6.1. Covariance with every coefficient retained

Let Di′,Dt′D_i',D_t' use the transformed potentials in (F3.32). Then

Di′ψ′=UχDiψ(i=1,2,3),Dt′ψ′=UχDtψ. D_i'\psi'=U_\chi D_i\psi\quad(i=1,2,3),\qquad D_t'\psi'=U_\chi D_t\psi. \tag{F3.34}
(F3.34)

Proof. Differentiating the phase with (F3.7) gives ∂μUχ=(iq/ℏ)(∂μχ)Uχ\partial_\mu U_\chi=(iq/\hbar) (\partial_\mu\chi)U_\chi. Therefore

Di′(Uχψ)=Uχ∂iψ+iqℏ(∂iχ)Uχψ−iqℏ(Ai+∂iχ)Uχψ=Uχ(∂iψ−iqℏAiψ),Dt′(Uχψ)=Uχ∂tψ+iqℏ(∂tχ)Uχψ+iqℏ(ϕ−∂tχ)Uχψ=Uχ(∂tψ+iqℏϕψ). \begin{aligned} D_i'(U_\chi\psi) &=U_\chi\partial_i\psi +\frac{iq}{\hbar}(\partial_i\chi)U_\chi\psi -\frac{iq}{\hbar}(A_i+\partial_i\chi)U_\chi\psi\\ &=U_\chi\left(\partial_i\psi-\frac{iq}{\hbar}A_i\psi\right),\\ D_t'(U_\chi\psi) &=U_\chi\partial_t\psi +\frac{iq}{\hbar}(\partial_t\chi)U_\chi\psi +\frac{iq}{\hbar}(\phi-\partial_t\chi)U_\chi\psi\\ &=U_\chi\left(\partial_t\psi+\frac{iq}{\hbar}\phi\psi\right). \end{aligned} \tag{F3.35}
(F3.35)

The two added derivative terms cancel with the indicated signs. This proves all four identities, also for q=0q=0, when the phase is one and the operators are ordinary derivatives. ▫\square

Because |Uχ|=1|U_\chi|=1, the identities preserve |ψ|2|\psi|^2, each |Diψ|2|D_i\psi|^2, and |Dtψ|2|D_t\psi|^2. They also intertwine the second-order operator

LA,ϕψ=1c2DtDtψ−∑i=13DiDiψ+λ2ψ:LA′,ϕ′(Uχψ)=UχLA,ϕψ. L_{A,\phi}\psi =\frac1{c^2}D_tD_t\psi-\sum_{i=1}^3D_iD_i\psi +\lambda^2\psi: \qquad L_{A',\phi'}(U_\chi\psi)=U_\chi L_{A,\phi}\psi. \tag{F3.36}
(F3.36)

Indeed, (F3.34) holds for every smooth function. Apply it first to ψ\psi and then to DiψD_i\psi or DtψD_t\psi; the scalar coefficient λ2\lambda^2 commutes with the phase. Here λ\lambda is the inverse-length parameter already defined in (F3.26); no physical mass identification is assumed.

Equation (F3.36) is a precise covariance statement for this specified classical complex-field operator. Together with Lesson 2’s preservation of E,BE,B, it gives an explicit bridge from electromagnetic potentials to the phase action on a charged scalar. It does not construct a quantum field theory.

7. Global phases need not have a global real angle

The distinction matters already on the missing-axis domain of Lesson 2. We now make the larger class of transformations explicit. Assume q≠0q\ne0, and let g:I×Ω→U(1)g:I\times\Omega\to U(1) be any smooth, single-valued phase. Define

ψ′=gψ,Ai′=Ai+ℏiqg−1∂ig(i=1,2,3),ϕ′=ϕ−ℏiqg−1∂tg. \begin{aligned} \psi'&=g\psi,\\ A_i'&=A_i+\frac{\hbar}{iq}g^{-1}\partial_i g \quad(i=1,2,3),\\ \phi'&=\phi-\frac{\hbar}{iq}g^{-1}\partial_t g. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.37}
(F3.37)

The added potential components are real. To prove this, differentiate g¯g=1\overline g g=1. It gives g−1∂μg¯=−g−1∂μg\overline{g^{-1}\partial_\mu g}=-g^{-1}\partial_\mu g, so g−1∂μgg^{-1}\partial_\mu g is purely imaginary. Division by ii is real, and ℏ/q\hbar/q is real.

The covariance proof in (F3.35) works here without assuming a global angle: the additional spatial term in Di′(gψ)D_i'(g\psi) is

(∂ig)ψ−iqℏℏiqg−1(∂ig)gψ=0, (\partial_i g)\psi -\frac{iq}{\hbar}\frac{\hbar}{iq} g^{-1}(\partial_i g)g\psi=0, \tag{F3.38}
(F3.38)

and the temporal cancellation has the same two opposite terms. Thus (F3.34) and (F3.36) hold with gg in place of UχU_\chi.

These transformations also preserve the electromagnetic fields. For any two coordinates μ,ν\mu,\nu, differentiating an inverse and using commutativity of scalar multiplication gives

∂μ(g−1∂νg)−∂ν(g−1∂μg)=−g−2(∂μg)(∂νg)+g−2(∂νg)(∂μg)+g−1(∂μ∂νg−∂ν∂μg)=0. \begin{aligned} \partial_\mu(g^{-1}\partial_\nu g) -\partial_\nu(g^{-1}\partial_\mu g) &=-g^{-2}(\partial_\mu g)(\partial_\nu g) +g^{-2}(\partial_\nu g)(\partial_\mu g)\\ &\quad+g^{-1}(\partial_\mu\partial_\nu g -\partial_\nu\partial_\mu g)=0. \end{aligned} \tag{F3.39}
(F3.39)

The spatial cases give zero curl for the added vector potential; the time-spatial cases cancel the changes in −∂iϕ−∂tAi-\partial_i\phi-\partial_tA_i. If g=eiqχ/ℏg=e^{iq\chi/\hbar} for a globally defined real χ\chi, (F3.37) reduces exactly to (F3.32). Thus the previous transformations are included, with an explicit map between the two descriptions.

Lemma 7.1. A phase along an interval has an angle

Let z:[a,b]→U(1)z:[a,b]\to U(1) be smooth. Choose a real βa\beta_a with eiβa=z(a)e^{i\beta_a}=z(a), and define

β(s)=βa+1i∫asz(r)−1z′(r)dr. \beta(s)=\beta_a+\frac1i\int_a^s z(r)^{-1}z'(r)\,dr. \tag{F3.40}
(F3.40)

Then β\beta is real and z(s)=eiβ(s)z(s)=e^{i\beta(s)}. If z(a)=z(b)z(a)=z(b), the difference β(b)−β(a)\beta(b)-\beta(a) is 2πm2\pi m for an integer mm.

Proof. Reality follows from the imaginary-valued derivative calculation preceding (F3.38). Differentiate e−iβ(s)z(s)e^{-i\beta(s)}z(s). Its derivative is e−iβ(z′−iβ′z)=0e^{-i\beta}(z'-i\beta'z)=0, since iβ′=z−1z′i\beta'=z^{-1}z'. At s=as=a, this product is one, so the fundamental theorem of calculus makes it one throughout the interval. The endpoint statement follows from the exact phase periodicity in (F3.7). ▫\square

Theorem 7.2. Exact equivalence in the missing-axis family

On Ω={(x1)2+(x2)2>0}\Omega=\{(x^1)^2+(x^2)^2>0\}, set r=(x1)2+(x2)2r=\sqrt{(x^1)^2+(x^2)^2}. Let κ\kappa be a real constant with unit action divided by charge, and define

Aκ=κ(−x2r2,x1r2,0),ϕκ=0. A_\kappa=\kappa\left(-\frac{x^2}{r^2}, \frac{x^1}{r^2},0\right), \qquad \phi_\kappa=0. \tag{F3.41}
(F3.41)

Two members with coefficients κ1,κ2\kappa_1,\kappa_2 are related by a smooth global transformation (F3.37) exactly when

q(κ2−κ1)ℏ∈ℤ. \frac{q(\kappa_2-\kappa_1)}{\hbar}\in\mathbb Z. \tag{F3.42}
(F3.42)

Proof. For necessity, fix time and restrict a proposed gg to the circle γ(θ)=(Rcos⁡θ,Rsin⁡θ,z0)\gamma(\theta)=(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta,z_0), with R>0R>0. Write z(θ)=g(t,γ(θ))z(\theta)=g(t,\gamma(\theta)). The endpoints agree. By Lemma 7.1,

∫γ(Aκ2−Aκ1)⋅dx=ℏiq∫02πz−1z′dθ=2πℏmq \int_\gamma (A_{\kappa_2}-A_{\kappa_1})\cdot dx =\frac{\hbar}{iq}\int_0^{2\pi}z^{-1}z'\,d\theta =\frac{2\pi\hbar m}{q} \tag{F3.43}
(F3.43)

for some integer mm. Lesson 2’s complete circle calculation makes the left side 2π(κ2−κ1)2\pi(\kappa_2-\kappa_1), proving (F3.42).

For sufficiency, let m=q(κ2−κ1)/ℏ∈ℤm=q(\kappa_2-\kappa_1)/\hbar\in\mathbb Z and define the smooth global function

g(x)=(x1+ix2r)m. g(x)=\left(\frac{x^1+ix^2}{r}\right)^m. \tag{F3.44}
(F3.44)

The denominator is positive everywhere on the domain, the factor has absolute value one, and integer powers, including negative powers, are single-valued. Put h=(x1+ix2)/rh=(x^1+ix^2)/r. Using ∂1r=x1/r\partial_1r=x^1/r, ∂2r=x2/r\partial_2r=x^2/r and h−1=(x1−ix2)/rh^{-1}=(x^1-ix^2)/r, direct differentiation gives

h−1∂1h=−ix2r2,h−1∂2h=ix1r2,∂3h=∂th=0. h^{-1}\partial_1h=-\frac{i x^2}{r^2},\qquad h^{-1}\partial_2h=\frac{i x^1}{r^2},\qquad \partial_3h=\partial_th=0. \tag{F3.45}
(F3.45)

The product rule for integer powers, and differentiation of hh−1=1hh^{-1}=1 for negative powers, gives g−1∂μg=mh−1∂μhg^{-1}\partial_\mu g=m h^{-1}\partial_\mu h. Substitution in (F3.37) adds the following vector to AA:

(ℏm/q)(−x2/r2,x1/r2,0)=(κ2−κ1)(−x2/r2,x1/r2,0). (\hbar m/q)(-x^2/r^2,x^1/r^2,0) =(\kappa_2-\kappa_1)(-x^2/r^2,x^1/r^2,0).

The scalar potential remains ϕ=0\phi=0. This constructs the required transformation. ▫\square

For example, m=1m=1 in (F3.44) has no single-valued global real angle on this domain. If g=eiβg=e^{i\beta} with such a β\beta, the integral of g−1dg/i=dβg^{-1}dg/i=d\beta around the circle would be zero; (F3.45) makes it 2π2\pi. Locally the angle exists by the same elementary construction as in Lesson 2.

The earlier classification under real gauge functions remains unchanged: it required κ2=κ1\kappa_2=\kappa_1. The enlarged, explicitly defined phase action now identifies coefficients differing by integer multiples of ℏ/q\hbar/q. Equivalently, the complete invariant for this particular family under (F3.37) is

exp⁡(iqℏ∫γAκ⋅dx)=e2πiqκ/ℏ. \exp\!\left(\frac{iq}{\hbar}\int_\gamma A_\kappa\cdot dx\right) =e^{2\pi i q\kappa/\hbar}. \tag{F3.46}
(F3.46)

Equality of these phases is equivalent to (F3.42), by periodicity, so both invariance and completeness within the family are proved. Every value in U(1)U(1) occurs: for a target phase eiαe^{i\alpha}, choose κ=ℏα/(2πq)\kappa=\hbar\alpha/(2\pi q). Consequently (F3.46) gives a bijection from the set of gauge classes of this family onto U(1)U(1). Changing α\alpha by 2πm2\pi m changes κ\kappa by ℏm/q\hbar m/q, exactly the equivalence already proved, so the construction of a class from a phase is well-defined. This is a statement about the declared classical potentials and their action on complex fields. No claim about a quantum spectrum or a Yang–Mills mass gap follows from this calculation.
