A positive quadrature rule

Written by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Public domain (CC0).

A cardinal function \(p\) and its transformed function \(K_p\) are given by integrals over the frequencies \(t\in[-1,1]\) (The sine product and cardinal functions, Gaussian damping and Fourier pairs). The construction of the functions \(H_1,H_2\) of the normalized statement needs explicit numbers: values and derivatives of \(p\) and \(K_p\) at many points, for many coefficient lists. This lesson replaces the integrals by finite sums with \(2304\) terms and proves that every quantity that is needed changes by less than \(10^{-31}\).

  • The rule of Fejér with \(N\) points integrates polynomials of degree less than \(N\) exactly, and its weights are positive (Theorem 1.2).
  • For a function that is holomorphic and bounded by \(A\) on a disc of twice the radius of the interval, the error of the rule is at most \(4A\cdot2^{-N}\) (Proposition 2.1).
  • For \(N=384\) on each of six frequency intervals, the values and all scaled derivatives of \(p\) and \(K_p\) on \([0,168]\), for lists with one entry \(1\) at a node up to \(168\), are reproduced with an error below \(10^{-31}\) (Theorem 3.3).

The lesson follows [OpenAI-T, Section 3, the part on the positive rule for the density integrals]. The rule is the first rule of Fejér, with the formula for the weights as given by J. Waldvogel [Waldvogel, formula (2.3)], divided by two. The lesson uses the estimate of Cauchy for Taylor coefficients (core course Complex Analysis).

1. The rule and its weights

Let \(N\ge2\) be an even integer. For \(v=0,1,\dots,N-1\) let \[ \begin{gathered} \theta_v=\frac{\pi(v+\frac12)}N,\qquad x_v=\cos\theta_v,\\ o_v=\frac1N\Bigl(1-2\sum_{a=1}^{N/2-1}\frac{\cos(2a\theta_v)}{4a^2-1}\Bigr). \end{gathered} \] The points \(x_v\) lie in the open interval \((-1,1)\).

Lemma 1.1. Let \(l\) be an integer with \(1\le l\le2N-1\). Then \(\sum_{v=0}^{N-1}\cos(l\theta_v)=0\).

Proof. For real \(\tau\), \[ \begin{aligned} &2\sin\frac\tau2\,\cos((v+\tfrac12)\tau)\\ &\quad=\sin((v+1)\tau)-\sin(v\tau). \end{aligned} \] Summing over \(v\), \[ 2\sin\frac\tau2\sum_{v=0}^{N-1}\cos((v+\tfrac12)\tau)=\sin(N\tau). \] For \(\tau=l\pi/N\) the right side is \(\sin(l\pi)=0\), and \(\sin(\tau/2)=\sin(l\pi/(2N))\ne0\) because \(0<l\pi/(2N)<\pi\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 1.2.

  1. For every polynomial \(f\) of degree at most \(N-1\), \[ \sum_{v=0}^{N-1}o_vf(x_v)=\frac12\int_{-1}^1f(x)\,dx . \]
  2. \(\sum_vo_v=1\), and \(o_v\ge\dfrac1{N(N-1)}\) for every \(v\).

Proof. (1) Let \(C_l\) be the polynomial of degree \(l\) with \(C_l(\cos\theta)=\cos(l\theta)\); it exists by the recursion \[ \begin{aligned} &\cos((l+1)\theta)\\ &\quad=2\cos\theta\cos(l\theta)-\cos((l-1)\theta). \end{aligned} \] The polynomials \(C_0,\dots,C_{N-1}\) span the polynomials of degree at most \(N-1\). So it is enough to prove (1) for \(f=C_l\), \(0\le l\le N-1\).

The right side. With \(x=\cos\theta\), \[ \begin{aligned} &\frac12\int_{-1}^1C_l(x)\,dx\\ &\quad=\frac12\int_0^\pi\cos(l\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta . \end{aligned} \] Here \[ \begin{aligned} &\cos(l\theta)\sin\theta\\ &\quad=\tfrac12\sin((l+1)\theta)-\tfrac12\sin((l-1)\theta), \end{aligned} \] and \(\int_0^\pi\sin(k\theta)\,d\theta\) is \(2/k\) for odd \(k\) and \(0\) for even \(k\). So the value is \(1\) for \(l=0\), it is \(0\) for odd \(l\), and for even \(l\ge2\) it is \[ \frac12\Bigl(\frac1{l+1}-\frac1{l-1}\Bigr)=\frac1{1-l^2}. \]

The left side. Let \(0\le l\le N-1\) and \(1\le a\le N/2-1\). Then \[ \begin{aligned} &\cos(l\theta_v)\cos(2a\theta_v)\\ &\quad=\tfrac12\cos((l+2a)\theta_v)\\ &\qquad+\tfrac12\cos((l-2a)\theta_v), \end{aligned} \] and \(1\le l+2a\le2N-1\), \(|l-2a|\le2N-1\). By Lemma 1.1 the sum over \(v\) of the first term is \(0\), and the sum of the second term is \(0\) unless \(l=2a\), when it is \(N/2\). So the number \[ \kappa_{l,a}=\frac1N\sum_v\cos(l\theta_v)\cos(2a\theta_v) \] is \(\tfrac12\) if \(l=2a\) and \(0\) otherwise. Now \(\sum_vo_vC_l(x_v)=\sum_vo_v\cos(l\theta_v)\) is, by the definition of \(o_v\), \[ \frac1N\sum_v\cos(l\theta_v)-2\sum_{a=1}^{N/2-1}\frac{\kappa_{l,a}}{4a^2-1}. \] For \(l=0\) the first term is \(1\) and the second is \(0\). For odd \(l\) both are \(0\). For even \(l\) with \(2\le l\le N-2\) the first is \(0\), and the second is \(-2\cdot\tfrac1{l^2-1}\cdot\tfrac12\), from \(a=l/2\). These are the values of the right side.

(2) The case \(l=0\) of (1) is \(\sum_vo_v=1\). Further \[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{a=1}^{N/2-1}\frac2{4a^2-1}\\ &\quad=\sum_{a=1}^{N/2-1}\Bigl(\frac1{2a-1}-\frac1{2a+1}\Bigr)\\ &\quad=1-\frac1{N-1}. \end{aligned} \] Since every cosine is at most \(1\), \(No_v\ge1-(1-\tfrac1{N-1})=\tfrac1{N-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)

So the rule \(f\mapsto\sum_vo_vf(x_v)\) approximates the average of \(f\) over \([-1,1]\), and it is a positive functional of total mass \(1\): \(|\sum_vo_vf(x_v)|\le\max|f|\).

2. The error for holomorphic functions

For an interval with midpoint \(u\) and half-length \(\rho\) the rule is transported by \(t=u+\rho x\).

Proposition 2.1. Let \(u\) be real, \(\rho>0\), and let \(g\) be holomorphic on an open set containing the closed disc \(|t-u|\le2\rho\), with \(|g(t)|\le A\) on this disc. Let \[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal A=\frac1{2\rho}\int_{u-\rho}^{u+\rho}g(t)\,dt,\\ \mathcal R=\sum_{v=0}^{N-1}o_v\,g(u+\rho x_v) \end{gathered} \] be the average of \(g\) over the interval and the value of the rule. Then \[ |\mathcal A-\mathcal R|\le4A\cdot2^{-N}. \]

Proof. Let \(g(t)=\sum_ra_r(t-u)^r\) be the Taylor series at \(u\). By the estimate of Cauchy on the circle of radius \(2\rho\), \(|a_r|\le A/(2\rho)^r\). Let \(g_0\) be the sum of the terms with \(r\le N-1\) and \(g_1=g-g_0\). For real \(t\) with \(|t-u|\le\rho\), \[ |g_1(t)|\le A\sum_{r\ge N}2^{-r}=2A\cdot2^{-N}. \] The function \(x\mapsto g_0(u+\rho x)\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(N-1\), so by Theorem 1.2(1) the rule gives its average exactly. For \(g_1\), both the average and the rule are at most \(\max|g_1|\) in absolute value, the rule by Theorem 1.2(2). So the two differ by at most \(2\cdot2A\cdot2^{-N}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The positivity of the weights is used here: without it the rule applied to \(g_1\) could only be bounded by \(\sum_v|o_v|\max|g_1|\).

3. The rule for the spectral integrals

Let \(\mathbf a\) be a coefficient list with finitely many nonzero entries \(C,c_n,d_n\), let \(p\) be its cardinal function, \(K=K_p\) its transformed function, and \(m\) the function on \([-1,1]\) of The transformed function at distant nodes, Section 2. With \(t_l=l/6\), \(\pi_l=P_le^{i\pi t_ln}\), and \(j\) the index with \(t_{j-1}<t<t_j\), \(1\le j\le6\), it is \(m=\sum_n(c_nm_n''+d_nm_n')\) with \[ \begin{aligned} m_n'(t)&=i\pi\,e^{-i\pi tn}\sum_{l=j}^6\pi_l,\\ m_n''(t)&=-\pi^2e^{-i\pi tn}\sum_{l=j}^6\pi_l\,(t_l-t). \end{aligned} \] On each interval \(I_j=(t_{j-1},t_j)\) these formulas define entire functions of \(t\).

Lemma 3.1. For \(0<t<1\) and real \(s,y\), and every integer \(a\ge0\): \(m(-t)=\overline{m(t)}\), and \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{y^a}{a!}p^{(a)}(s)={}&C\,\frac{y^a}{a!}P^{(a)}(s)\\ &+2\operatorname{Re}\int_0^1m(t)\,\Phi_a(t)\,dt,\\ \frac{y^a}{a!}K^{(a)}(s)={}&C\sum_{l=-6}^6P_l\,\Psi_a(t_l)\\ &+2\operatorname{Re}\int_0^1m(t)\,\Psi_a(t)\,dt, \end{aligned} \] where \[ \begin{aligned} \Phi_a(t)&=e^{i\pi ts}\,\frac{(i\pi ty)^a}{a!},\\ \Psi_a(t)&=\lambda(t)\,e^{i\pi z(t)s}\,\frac{(i\pi z(t)y)^a}{a!}. \end{aligned} \]

Proof. For \(-1<t<0\) the functions \(m_n',m_n''\) are given by the sums over the indices \(l\) with \(t_l<t\), with the opposite sign. Since \(P_{-l}=\overline{P_l}\) and \(t_{-l}=-t_l\), we have \(\pi_{-l}=\overline{\pi_l}\), and the formulas give \(m_n'(-t)=\overline{m_n'(t)}\) and \(m_n''(-t)=\overline{m_n''(t)}\). The entries of the list are real, so \(m(-t)=\overline{m(t)}\).

The derivatives of \(p\) and \(K\) are obtained by differentiating under the integral sign (Lemma 3.1 of the lesson on Gaussian damping), which gives the integrals of \(m\Phi_a\) and \(m\Psi_a\) over \([-1,1]\). For real \(s\) and \(y\), \(\Phi_a(-t)=\overline{\Phi_a(t)}\). Also \(\lambda(-t)=\overline{\lambda(t)}\) and \(z(-t)=-\overline{z(t)}\) by the definitions, hence \(\Psi_a(-t)=\overline{\Psi_a(t)}\). So the integrals over \([-1,0]\) are the complex conjugates of those over \([0,1]\). \(\blacksquare\)

Now fix \(N=384\), with the points \(x_v\) and weights \(o_v\) of Section 1. The interval \(I_j\) has the midpoint \(u_j=(2j-1)/12\) and the half-length \(1/12\). For \(1\le j\le6\) and \(0\le v<N\) let \[ \tau_{jv}=u_j+\frac{x_v}{12}. \] For a function \(g\) on \((0,1)\) define \[ \mathcal Q(g)=\sum_{j=1}^6\sum_{v=0}^{N-1}\frac{o_v}6\,g(\tau_{jv}). \] The quadrature form of \(\tfrac{y^a}{a!}p^{(a)}(s)\) or \(\tfrac{y^a}{a!}K^{(a)}(s)\) is the expression of Lemma 3.1 with \(\int_0^1m\Phi_a\,dt\) replaced by \(\mathcal Q(m\Phi_a)\), respectively \(\int_0^1m\Psi_a\,dt\) by \(\mathcal Q(m\Psi_a)\). The terms with the factor \(C\) are not changed. The quadrature forms are finite sums, with \(6N=2304\) points.

Lemma 3.2. Let \(w,a\) be complex numbers and \(\rho>0\) with \(|w-a|\le\rho<|a|\). Then \[ \Bigl|\frac1w-\frac{\overline a}{|a|^2-\rho^2}\Bigr|\le\frac\rho{|a|^2-\rho^2}. \]

Proof. Let \(e=|a|^2-\rho^2>0\). The left side is \(|e-\overline aw|/(e|w|)\), so the claim is \(|e-\overline aw|^2\le\rho^2|w|^2\). Now \[ \begin{aligned} &|e-\overline aw|^2-\rho^2|w|^2\\ &\quad=e^2-2e\operatorname{Re}(\overline aw)+(|a|^2-\rho^2)|w|^2\\ &\quad=e\,\bigl(|a|^2-\rho^2-2\operatorname{Re}(\overline aw)+|w|^2\bigr)\\ &\quad=e\,\bigl(|w-a|^2-\rho^2\bigr)\le0 . \end{aligned} \] \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 3.3. Let \(M=168\). Let \(\mathbf a\) be a list with exactly one nonzero entry, equal to \(1\): the entry \(C\), or \(c_n\) or \(d_n\) for a node \(n\in\mathcal N_0\) with \(n\le M\). Let \(a\ge0\) be an integer, and \(s,y\) real with \(0\le s\le M\) and \(|y|\le3/2\). Then each of \[ \frac{y^a}{a!}p^{(a)}(s),\qquad\frac{y^a}{a!}K^{(a)}(s) \] differs from its quadrature form by less than \(10^{-31}\).

Proof. If the entry is \(C\), then \(m=0\) and nothing is changed. Let the entry be \(c_n\) or \(d_n\). Fix \(j\), and let \(t\) be a complex number in the disc \(|t-u_j|\le1/6\), which has twice the radius of the interval \(I_j\). We bound the functions that occur, extended to this disc by their formulas.

The frequency. \(|t|\le\tfrac{11}{12}+\tfrac16<1.1\) and \(|\operatorname{Im}t|\le\tfrac16\).

The functions \(\lambda\) and \(z\). Recall \(\lambda(t)=i/(b(t+ih))\) and \(z(t)=-B/(t+ih)-ih\). The imaginary part of \(t+ih\) is at least \(h-\tfrac16=\tfrac7{30}\). So \(\lambda\) and \(z\) are holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the disc, and \[ \begin{gathered} |\lambda(t)|\le\frac{30}{7b}=\frac{60}{7\sqrt3}<5,\\ |z(t)|\le\frac{30B}7+h=\frac{214}{35}<6.2 . \end{gathered} \] The point \(w=t+ih\) lies in the disc with centre \(u_j+ih\) and radius \(\tfrac16\). By Lemma 3.2, with \(e=u_j^2+h^2-\tfrac1{36}\), \[ \operatorname{Im}\frac1w\le\frac{-h+\frac16}e . \] Hence \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Im}z(t)&=-B\operatorname{Im}\frac1w-h\\ &\ge\frac{B(h-\frac16)}e-h . \end{aligned} \] The right side is smallest for the largest \(u_j\), which is \(\tfrac{11}{12}\). There \(e=\tfrac{3501}{3600}\), and the right side is \(\tfrac{1120}{3501}-\tfrac25=-\tfrac{1402}{17505}\), which is more than \(-0.081\).

The density. \(|e^{-i\pi tn}|=e^{\pi n\operatorname{Im}t}\le e^{\pi M/6}\), and \(|\pi_l|=|P_l|\). For \(l\ge j\), \(|t_l-t|\le t_l-u_j+\tfrac16\). The numbers \(|P_1|,\dots,|P_6|\) are \(2,2,2,1,2,1\), with \(\sum_l|P_l|=10\) and \(\sum_lt_l|P_l|=\tfrac{16}3\). Since \(\tfrac16-u_j\) is \(\tfrac1{12}\) for \(j=1\) and negative for \(j\ge2\), \[ \begin{aligned} &\sum_{l=j}^6|P_l|\,\bigl(t_l-u_j+\tfrac16\bigr)\\ &\quad\le\frac{16}3+\frac{10}{12}=\frac{37}6 . \end{aligned} \] So \[ \begin{gathered} |2m_n''(t)|\le\frac{37\pi^2}3\,e^{\pi M/6},\\ |2m_n'(t)|\le20\pi\,e^{\pi M/6}, \end{gathered} \] and both are less than \(220\,e^{\pi M/6}\).

The kernels. For \(x\ge0\), \(x^a/a!\le e^x\). With \(|ty|\le1.65\) and \(|z(t)y|\le9.3\), \[ \begin{aligned} |\Phi_a(t)|&\le e^{\pi s|\operatorname{Im}t|}\,e^{\pi|ty|}\\ &\le e^{\pi M/6}\,e^{1.65\pi},\\ |\Psi_a(t)|&\le5\,e^{-\pi s\operatorname{Im}z(t)}\,e^{\pi|z(t)y|}\\ &\le5\,e^{0.081\pi M}\,e^{9.3\pi}. \end{aligned} \] These bounds do not depend on \(a\).

The products. On the disc, \(|2m\Phi_a|\le220\,e^{\pi(M/3+1.65)}\) and \(|2m\Psi_a|\le1100\,e^{\pi(M/6+0.081M+9.3)}\). With \(M=168\) the exponents are \(57.65\,\pi\) and \(50.908\,\pi\); since \(\pi<22/7\) they are less than \(182\) and \(160\). Since \(e^{2.3}<10\), we get \(e^{182}<10^{79.2}\) and \(e^{160}<10^{69.6}\). So both products are bounded on the disc by \(A=10^{82}\).

The rule. By Proposition 2.1 with \(\rho=\tfrac1{12}\), for \(g=2m\Phi_a\) or \(g=2m\Psi_a\) on \(I_j\), \[ \begin{gathered} \Bigl|\int_{I_j}g\,dt-\sum_v\frac{o_v}6\,g(\tau_{jv})\Bigr|\\ \le\frac{4A}6\cdot2^{-N}. \end{gathered} \] Summing over the six intervals, \(\int_0^1g\,dt\) and \(\mathcal Q(g)\) differ by at most \(4A\cdot2^{-384}\). Now \(2^{384}=1024^{38}\cdot16>16\cdot10^{114}\), so \(4A\cdot2^{-384}<\tfrac14\cdot10^{-32}\). Taking real parts does not increase the difference. \(\blacksquare\)

By linearity, for a list supported on the nodes up to \(M\) the difference is less than \(10^{-31}\) times the sum of the absolute values of its entries \(c_n,d_n\).

4. Exercises

4.1. Write down the rule of Section 1 for \(N=2\) and for \(N=4\). Check \(\sum_vo_v=1\) and the lower bound of Theorem 1.2(2) for \(N=4\).

4.2. Show that the rule with \(N=2\) is not exact for \(f(x)=x^2\).

4.3. In the proof of Theorem 3.3 the bound \(A=10^{82}\) is used. Which values of \(N\) give, by the same argument, an error below \(10^{-31}\)?

4.4. Let \(g\) be as in Proposition 2.1, but holomorphic and bounded by \(A\) on the larger disc \(|t-u|\le4\rho\). What bound does the proof give?

5. Solutions

4.1. For \(N=2\): \(\theta_0=\pi/4\), \(\theta_1=3\pi/4\), \(x_0=\tfrac{\sqrt2}2\), \(x_1=-\tfrac{\sqrt2}2\); the sum in \(o_v\) is empty, so \(o_0=o_1=\tfrac12\). For \(N=4\): \(\theta_v=\pi/8,3\pi/8,5\pi/8,7\pi/8\), and \(o_v=\tfrac14(1-\tfrac23\cos(2\theta_v))\). The numbers \(\cos(2\theta_v)\) are \(\tfrac{\sqrt2}2,-\tfrac{\sqrt2}2,-\tfrac{\sqrt2}2,\tfrac{\sqrt2}2\). So \(o_0=o_3=\tfrac14(1-\tfrac{\sqrt2}3)\), about \(0.132\), and \(o_1=o_2=\tfrac14(1+\tfrac{\sqrt2}3)\), about \(0.368\). Their sum is \(1\), and \(0.132>\tfrac1{12}\).

4.2. The rule gives \(\tfrac12(\tfrac12+\tfrac12)=\tfrac12\), and \(\tfrac12\int_{-1}^1x^2\,dx=\tfrac13\).

4.3. The argument gives the error \(4\cdot10^{82}\cdot2^{-N}\). This is below \(10^{-31}\) when \(2^N>4\cdot10^{113}\), that is \(N>2+113\log_210\), which is about \(377.4\). So every even \(N\ge378\) suffices.

4.4. Then \(|a_r|\le A/(4\rho)^r\) and \(|g_1(t)|\le A\sum_{r\ge N}4^{-r}=\tfrac43A\cdot4^{-N}\) on the interval. The error is at most \(\tfrac83A\cdot4^{-N}\).
