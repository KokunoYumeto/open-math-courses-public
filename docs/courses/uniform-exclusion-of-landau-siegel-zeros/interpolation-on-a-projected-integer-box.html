Interpolation on a projected integer box

Written by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Public domain (CC0).

The determinant of A weighted determinant in a biquadratic field is formed from evaluation rows: monomials \(z_1^{\alpha_1}z_2^{\alpha_2}z_3^{\alpha_3}\) evaluated at the \(N^4\) points \(An\in\mathbf C^3\), where \(n\) runs over the integer box \(\{0,\dots,N-1\}^4\) and \(A:\mathbf C^4\to\mathbf C^3\) is linear. For that determinant to be nonzero, enough of the rows must be linearly independent. This lesson proves that polynomials with separately bounded degrees \(t_1,t_2,t_3\) take arbitrary values at these points once \(\prod_j(t_j-3(N-1)+1)>(4N-3)^4\), provided the kernel of \(A\) is spanned by a vector whose coordinates are linearly independent over \(\mathbf Q\). The three degrees may be very unequal. The proof, which follows OpenAI [OpenAI-LS, Section 3], combines a dimension count, a nearest-point argument for an integer polytope, and Lagrange interpolation on one of its faces.

1. Convex sets

For a finite set \(S\subset\mathbf R^k\), \(\operatorname{conv}(S)\) is the set of convex combinations of points of \(S\); it is compact and convex. For a convex set \(P\) and \(t\ge0\), put \(tP=\{tv:v\in P\}\). If \(s,t\ge0\) and \(s+t>0\), then \[ sP+tP=(s+t)P, \] because \(sv+tw=(s+t)\bigl(\tfrac s{s+t}v+\tfrac t{s+t}w\bigr)\) and \((s+t)v=sv+tv\) for \(v,w\in P\).

Lemma 2.1 (nearest points). Let \(\mathcal K\subset\mathbf R^k\) be nonempty, compact and convex, and \(m\in\mathbf R^k\). Some point \(y\in\mathcal K\) is nearest to \(m\), and \(h=m-y\) satisfies \(h\cdot(x-y)\le0\) for every \(x\in\mathcal K\).

Proof. The distance to \(m\) is continuous on the compact set \(\mathcal K\), so it has a minimum at some \(y\). For \(x\in\mathcal K\) and \(0<t\le1\), the point \(y+t(x-y)\) lies in \(\mathcal K\), so \[ \begin{aligned} &\|h-t(x-y)\|^2-\|h\|^2\\ &\quad=-2t\,h\cdot(x-y)+t^2\|x-y\|^2\ge0 . \end{aligned} \] Divide by \(t\) and let \(t\to0\). \(\square\)

Lemma 2.2 (faces). Let \(S\subset\mathbf R^k\) be finite and nonempty, \(P=\operatorname{conv}(S)\), \(h\in\mathbf R^k\), and \[ G=\{v\in P:h\cdot v=\max_{w\in P}h\cdot w\}. \] Then \(G=\operatorname{conv}(S\cap G)\). If \(h\ne0\), then \(G\) lies in an affine hyperplane.

Proof. Put \(\mu=\max_Ph\cdot w\). If \(v=\sum_i\lambda_is_i\) is a convex combination of points \(s_i\in S\) with all \(\lambda_i>0\) and \(v\in G\), then \(\sum_i\lambda_i(\mu-h\cdot s_i)=0\) with nonnegative terms, so every \(s_i\) lies in \(G\). Hence \(G\subset\operatorname{conv}(S\cap G)\). Conversely \(G\) is convex and contains \(S\cap G\). If \(h\ne0\), then \(G\) lies in the hyperplane \(\{v:h\cdot v=\mu\}\). \(\square\)

Lemma 2.3 (Carathéodory). Let \(T\subset\mathbf R^k\) lie in an affine subspace of dimension \(e\). Every convex combination of points of \(T\) is a convex combination of at most \(e+1\) points of \(T\).

Proof. Write \(v=\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_it_i\) with \(t_i\in T\), \(\lambda_i>0\), \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\) and \(r\) least. If \(r>e+1\), the points \(t_i\) are affinely dependent: there are real \(\mu_i\), not all zero, with \(\sum_i\mu_it_i=0\) and \(\sum_i\mu_i=0\). Some \(\mu_i\) is positive. Let \(\kappa\) be the least value of \(\lambda_i/\mu_i\) over the indices with \(\mu_i>0\). The coefficients \(\lambda_i-\kappa\mu_i\) are nonnegative, have sum \(1\), represent \(v\), and at least one of them is zero. This contradicts the minimality of \(r\). \(\square\)

2. Polynomials

Lemma 2.4. A polynomial in \(k\) variables with complex coefficients that vanishes at every point of \(\mathbf Z^k\) is zero.

Proof. Induction on \(k\); for \(k=0\) there is nothing to prove. Write \(f=\sum_jf_j(x_1,\dots,x_{k-1})x_k^j\). For each \(x'\in\mathbf Z^{k-1}\), the polynomial \(f(x',x_k)\) in one variable has infinitely many roots, so all \(f_j(x')\) vanish. By induction every \(f_j\) is zero. \(\square\)

The polynomials \(B\in\mathbf C[z_1,z_2,z_3]\) with \(\deg_{z_j}B\le b_j\) for \(j=1,2,3\) form a vector space with basis the monomials \(z^\alpha=z_1^{\alpha_1}z_2^{\alpha_2}z_3^{\alpha_3}\), \(0\le\alpha_j\le b_j\), of dimension \(\prod_j(b_j+1)\). Translating the variable, \(B(z)\mapsto B(z+w)\), preserves each separate degree, and a polynomial of total degree at most \(D\) has each separate degree at most \(D\).

3. The interpolation theorem

Write \(E_N=\{0,\dots,N-1\}^4\). For integers \(t_1,t_2,t_3\ge0\), let \(\mathcal P_t\) be the space of \(B\in\mathbf C[z_1,z_2,z_3]\) with \(\deg_{z_j}B\le t_j\) for \(j=1,2,3\).

Theorem 2.5 (OpenAI). Let \(A:\mathbf C^4\to\mathbf C^3\) be a surjective linear map whose kernel is spanned by a vector \(c\) whose four coordinates are linearly independent over \(\mathbf Q\). Let \(N\ge1\) and \(t_1,t_2,t_3\) be integers with \(t_j\ge3(N-1)\) for \(j=1,2,3\) and \[ \prod_{j=1}^3\bigl(t_j-3(N-1)+1\bigr)>(4N-3)^4 . \] Then the evaluation map \(\mathcal P_t\to\mathbf C^{E_N}\), \(B\mapsto(B(An))_{n\in E_N}\), is surjective.

Proof. Suppose the image is a proper subspace. A nonzero linear form vanishes on it: there are complex numbers \(v_n\), not all zero, such that for every \(B\in\mathcal P_t\) \[ \sum_{n\in E_N}v_nB(An)=0 . \tag{2.1} \] Let \(S=\{n\in E_N:v_n\ne0\}\), \(P=\operatorname{conv}(S)\subset\mathbf R^4\) and \(\mathcal K=4P\).

Step 1: an auxiliary polynomial. Put \(b_j=t_j-3(N-1)\ge0\). Since \(\mathcal K\subset[0,4(N-1)]^4\), it contains at most \((4N-3)^4\) integer points. Vanishing at the points \(Am\), \(m\in\mathcal K\cap\mathbf Z^4\), imposes at most \((4N-3)^4\) linear conditions on the space of polynomials with \(\deg_{z_j}\le b_j\), whose dimension \(\prod_j(b_j+1)\) is larger. So there is a nonzero \(R\) with \(\deg_{z_j}R\le b_j\) such that, for every \(m\in\mathcal K\cap\mathbf Z^4\), \[ R(Am)=0 . \tag{2.2} \]

Step 2: the nearest nonzero value. The polynomial \(R\circ A\) on \(\mathbf C^4\) is nonzero: otherwise \(R\) would vanish on \(A(\mathbf C^4)=\mathbf C^3\). By Lemma 2.4 some \(m\in\mathbf Z^4\) has \(R(Am)\ne0\). Choose such an \(m\) at least distance from \(\mathcal K\); this is possible because only finitely many integer points lie within a given distance of the bounded set \(\mathcal K\). By Lemma 2.1 some \(y\in\mathcal K\) is nearest to \(m\), and \(h=m-y\) satisfies \(h\cdot(x-y)\le0\) for \(x\in\mathcal K\). By (2.2), \(m\notin\mathcal K\), so \(h\ne0\).

Step 3: a face and one of its points. Let \(G=\{v\in P:h\cdot v=\max_Ph\cdot w\}\). For \(v\in P\), \(4v\in\mathcal K\) gives \(h\cdot v\le h\cdot(y/4)\), and \(y/4\in P\); so \(y/4\in G\). By Lemma 2.2, \(G=\operatorname{conv}(S\cap G)\), and \(S\cap G\) lies in an affine hyperplane. By Lemma 2.3, \[ \frac y4=\sum_{i=1}^r\lambda_iu_i,\qquad r\le4,\quad u_i\in S\cap G, \] with \(\lambda_i>0\) and \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\). Some \(\lambda_i\) is at least \(\frac14\); let \(u\) be its point. In \[ y-u=(4\lambda_u-1)u+\sum_{u_i\ne u}4\lambda_iu_i \] the coefficients are nonnegative with sum \(3\). Therefore \[ u\in S\cap G,\quad y-u\in3P . \tag{2.3} \]

Step 4: vanishing off the face. Let \(n\in S\setminus G\). By (2.3), \(y'=y+n-u\) lies in \(3P+P=\mathcal K\), and \(m'=m+n-u=y'+h\) is an integer point. Since \(u\in G\) and \(n\notin G\), \(h\cdot(u-n)>0\). For \(0<\eta\le1\) the point \(y'+\eta(y-y')\) lies in \(\mathcal K\), and as \(y-y'=u-n\), \[ \begin{aligned} &\|m'-y'-\eta(y-y')\|^2\\ &\quad=\|h\|^2-2\eta\,h\cdot(u-n)\\ &\qquad+\eta^2\|u-n\|^2 . \end{aligned} \] For small \(\eta\) this is less than \(\|h\|^2\). So \(m'\) is closer to \(\mathcal K\) than \(m\), and the choice of \(m\) gives, for every \(n\in S\setminus G\), \[ R\bigl(A(m+n-u)\bigr)=0 . \tag{2.4} \]

Step 5: a rational hyperplane through the face. Fix \(u_0\in S\cap G\). The integer vectors \(w-u_0\), \(w\in S\cap G\), span a real subspace of dimension at most \(3\); since they are rational, their span over \(\mathbf Q\) has the same dimension. Choose \(0\ne r\in\mathbf Z^4\) orthogonal to all of them, and put \(k=r\cdot u_0\in\mathbf Z\). The complex hyperplane \[ \Pi=\{x\in\mathbf C^4:r\cdot x=k\} \] contains \(S\cap G\). As \(r\) is a nonzero integer vector and the coordinates of \(c\) are linearly independent over \(\mathbf Q\), \(r\cdot c\ne0\). Consequently \(A\) maps \(\Pi\) bijectively onto \(\mathbf C^3\). Indeed, two points of \(\Pi\) with the same image differ by a multiple \(tc\) with \(t\,(r\cdot c)=0\), hence coincide. And for \(z\in\mathbf C^3\), choose \(x\) with \(Ax=z\); the point \(x+tc\) with \(t=(k-r\cdot x)/(r\cdot c)\) lies in \(\Pi\) and maps to \(z\). The inverse is affine: if \(x_0\in\Pi\) and \(L\) is the inverse of the linear bijection \(A\) from \(\{r\cdot x=0\}\) onto \(\mathbf C^3\), the inverse sends \(z\) to \(x_0+L(z-Ax_0)\). Let \(\lambda_1(z),\dots,\lambda_4(z)\) be its coordinates, polynomials of degree at most \(1\) in \(z\). Then \(\lambda_i(An)=n_i\) for \(n\in\Pi\).

Step 6: Lagrange interpolation on the face. Choose \(i_0\) with \(r_{i_0}\ne0\), and put \[ Q(z)=\prod_{i\ne i_0}\ \prod_{\substack{0\le a<N\\ a\ne u_i}}\frac{\lambda_i(z)-a}{u_i-a}, \] with empty products equal to \(1\). Its total degree is at most \(3(N-1)\). Let \(n\in S\cap G\). Then \(n\in\Pi\) and \[ Q(An)=\prod_{i\ne i_0}\ \prod_{a\ne u_i}\frac{n_i-a}{u_i-a}. \] If \(n_i\ne u_i\) for some \(i\ne i_0\), then \(n_i\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\) is one of the excluded values \(a\), and \(Q(An)=0\). Otherwise \(n\) and \(u\) agree outside the coordinate \(i_0\), and \(r\cdot n=k=r\cdot u\) forces \(n=u\). Thus \[ Q(An)=\begin{cases}1,&n=u,\\0,&n\in(S\cap G)\setminus\{u\}.\end{cases} \]

Step 7: the contradiction. Let \(B(z)=Q(z)R(z+A(m-u))\). By Section 2, \(\deg_{z_j}B\le3(N-1)+b_j=t_j\), so \(B\in\mathcal P_t\). Since \(B(An)=Q(An)R(A(m+n-u))\), (2.1) gives \[ 0=\sum_{n\in S}v_nQ(An)R\bigl(A(m+n-u)\bigr). \] By (2.4) the terms with \(n\notin G\) vanish, and by Step 6 so do the terms with \(n\in G\), \(n\ne u\). The remaining term is \(v_uR(Am)\ne0\). \(\square\)

The proof never bounds the coefficients of \(R\) or \(Q\); the theorem is a statement of linear algebra, uniform in \(A\).

4. Rectangular boxes of exponents

Corollary 2.6 (OpenAI). Let \(A\) be as in Theorem 2.5, and let \(H,N\) be integers with \(1\le H\le N\). Put \[ \begin{aligned} U&=N^{4/3},\qquad T_1=32H^{2/3}U,\\ T_2&=T_3=32H^{-1/3}U . \end{aligned} \] The vectors \(\bigl((An)^\alpha\bigr)_{n\in E_N}\), for \(\alpha\in\mathbf Z_{\ge0}^3\) with \(\alpha_j\le T_j\) for \(j=1,2,3\), span \(\mathbf C^{E_N}\). Here \((An)^\alpha=(An)_1^{\alpha_1}(An)_2^{\alpha_2}(An)_3^{\alpha_3}\).

Proof. Put \(t_j=\lfloor T_j\rfloor\). Since \(H\le N\), \[ \begin{aligned} T_1&\ge32N^{4/3}\ge32N,\\ T_2&=32H^{-1/3}N^{4/3}\ge32N, \end{aligned} \] and \(T_1T_2T_3=32^3N^4\). As \(32N\) is an integer, \(t_j\ge32N\ge3(N-1)\). Also \(t_j+1>T_j\) and \(T_j\ge6(N-1)\), so \[ \begin{aligned} &\prod_{j=1}^3\bigl(t_j-3(N-1)+1\bigr)\\ &\quad>\prod_{j=1}^3\bigl(T_j-3(N-1)\bigr)\\ &\quad\ge\frac{T_1T_2T_3}8=4096N^4\\ &\quad=(8N)^4>(4N-3)^4 . \end{aligned} \] By Theorem 2.5 the evaluation map on \(\mathcal P_t\) is surjective. Its image is spanned by the images of the monomials \(z^\alpha\) with \(\alpha_j\le t_j\), and for integers \(\alpha_j\le t_j\) is the same as \(\alpha_j\le T_j\). \(\square\)

With \(H\) large, the box is long in the first exponent and short in the other two: \(T_1/T_2=H\), while the number of exponents stays of order \(N^4\). This is what the third lesson uses.

Remark 2.7 (derivations on a torus). On \((\mathbf C^\times)^4\) put \(D_j=\sum_iA_{ji}x_i\,\partial/\partial x_i\) for \(j=1,2,3\). For the monomial \(x^n=x_1^{n_1}\cdots x_4^{n_4}\), \(x_i\partial x^n/\partial x_i=n_ix^n\), so \(D_jx^n=(An)_jx^n\) and \[ \bigl(D_1^{\alpha_1}D_2^{\alpha_2}D_3^{\alpha_3}x^n\bigr)(1,1,1,1)=(An)^\alpha . \] Corollary 2.6 therefore says that no nonzero combination of the monomials \(x^n\), \(n\in E_N\), vanishes at \((1,1,1,1)\) together with all derivatives \(D^\alpha\), \(\alpha_j\le T_j\). Statements of this kind are multiplicity estimates; Philippon [Philippon] proved general multiplicity estimates on commutative algebraic groups.

5. Exercises

2.1 (easy). Suppose the kernel of \(A\) is spanned by a nonzero integer vector \(w\) with \(\max_i|w_i|\le N-1\). Show that the evaluation map of Theorem 2.5 is not surjective, whatever \(t\) is.

2.2 (easy). For \(N=1\), show that the evaluation map is surjective for all \(t_1,t_2,t_3\ge0\), although the hypothesis \(\prod_j(t_j+1)>1\) of Theorem 2.5 fails for \(t=(0,0,0)\). So the hypothesis is sufficient, not necessary.

2.3 (easy). Show that \(P+P=2P\) can fail for a set \(P\) that is not convex.

2.4 (medium). Suppose the first row \((A_{11},\dots,A_{14})\) of \(A\) has entries that are linearly independent over \(\mathbf Q\). Show that the numbers \((An)_1\), \(n\in E_N\), are pairwise distinct and that the vectors \(\bigl((An)_1^j\bigr)_{n\in E_N}\), \(0\le j<N^4\), span \(\mathbf C^{E_N}\). Thus a single exponent of size \(N^4\) suffices; Corollary 2.6 keeps every exponent of order \(N^{4/3}H^{2/3}\) at most.

6. Solutions

2.1. Choose \(n\in E_N\) with \(n+w\in E_N\): take \(n_i=0\) if \(w_i\ge0\) and \(n_i=-w_i\) otherwise. Then \(A(n+w)=An\), so every vector in the image has equal entries at \(n\) and \(n+w\), two different points of \(E_N\).

2.2. \(E_1=\{0\}\), and the constant polynomial \(1\) lies in every \(\mathcal P_t\) and has the value \(1\) at \(A0\).

2.3. For \(P=\{0,1\}\subset\mathbf R\), \(P+P=\{0,1,2\}\) and \(2P=\{0,2\}\).

2.4. If \((An)_1=(An')_1\), then \(\sum_iA_{1i}(n_i-n'_i)=0\) with integer coefficients, so \(n=n'\). The matrix \(\bigl((An)_1^j\bigr)\) is a Vandermonde matrix in \(N^4\) distinct numbers, and its determinant is nonzero.
