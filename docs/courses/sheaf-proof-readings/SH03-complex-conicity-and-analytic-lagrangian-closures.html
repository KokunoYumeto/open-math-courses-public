Complex conicity and analytic Lagrangian closures

A real Lagrangian tangent plane need not be a complex tangent plane. Complex fibre dilations supply the extra information: they put both an Euler vector and its imaginary multiple in the tangent plane. The real symplectic form then detects both parts of the complex canonical form. We will use this calculation to prove that an involutive set with a subanalytic isotropic bound becomes complex analytic when it is locally invariant under complex dilations.

Let XX be a complex analytic manifold of complex dimension nn, Hausdorff and countable at infinity. Put P=T*XP=T^*X, with its holomorphic cotangent structure. The corresponding real manifolds have dimensions 2n2n and 4n4n. All statements are local on components of fixed dimension. There are no sheaf coefficients or derived shifts in this geometric lesson.

We give the tangent, boundary and rank arguments explicitly. The subanalytic uniformization input is supplied by the dimension-controlled proper uniformization proof. The pure-dimensional analytic removal step is proved below from subanalytic dimension calculus and elementary holomorphic analysis. The source account below credits the classical geometric mechanism and distinguishes its conventions from ours.

Real covectors associated to holomorphic covectors

The real identification we use is

ρ:(T*X)ℝ⟶T*(Xℝ),ρ(ξ)(v)=Re⁡(ξ(v)).(1) \rho:(T^*X)^{\mathbb R}\longrightarrow T^*(X^{\mathbb R}), \qquad \rho(\xi)(v)=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(\xi(v)\bigr). \qquad\text{(1)}

Here vv is a real tangent vector, regarded as a vector in the underlying real space of the complex tangent space. This identification is bijective: given a real covector β\beta, its corresponding complex-linear covector is

ξ(v)=β(v)−iβ(iv).(2) \xi(v)=\beta(v)-i\beta(iv). \qquad\text{(2)}

Indeed ξ(iv)=iξ(v)\xi(iv)=i\xi(v), and the real part of (2) is β(v)\beta(v). In coordinates zj=xj+iyjz_j=x_j+iy_j and ξj=aj+ibj\xi_j=a_j+ib_j,

ρ(∑jξjdzj)=∑j(ajdxj−bjdyj).(3) \rho\left(\sum_j\xi_j\,dz_j\right) =\sum_j(a_j\,dx_j-b_j\,dy_j). \qquad\text{(3)}

Consequently, for the complex canonical form and symplectic form,

α=∑jξjdzj,Ω=dα=∑jdξj∧dzj,(4) \alpha=\sum_j\xi_j\,dz_j,\qquad \Omega=d\alpha=\sum_jd\xi_j\wedge dz_j, \qquad\text{(4)}

the real canonical form and real symplectic form are Re⁡α\operatorname{Re}\alpha and ω=Re⁡Ω\omega=\operatorname{Re}\Omega. In particular ω\omega is nondegenerate. A vector annihilated by Re⁡Ω\operatorname{Re}\Omega against every real vector is also annihilated by Im⁡Ω\operatorname{Im}\Omega: test against the imaginary multiple of each vector. Complex nondegeneracy of Ω\Omega then makes that vector zero.

For a complex submanifold N⊂XN\subset X, (1) identifies its complex conormal with its real conormal. If Re⁡ξ\operatorname{Re}\xi annihilates the real tangent space of NN, test both vv and iviv in that tangent space. Both parts of ξ(v)\xi(v) vanish, so ξ\xi annihilates the whole complex tangent space. This explains why complex conormals can be used in real microsupport estimates without changing the underlying subset.

Analytic pieces and the different conicity conditions

A locally closed subset S⊂XS\subset X is a complex analytic piece when its closure S¯\overline S and its frontier S¯\S\overline S\setminus S are closed complex analytic subsets of XX. Thus ℂ\{0}\mathbb C\setminus\{0\} is an analytic piece in ℂ\mathbb C. An arbitrary open disc in ℂ\mathbb C is not: its closed-disc closure is not complex analytic. These pieces are subanalytic in the underlying real manifold.

Complex fibre multiplication is

mλ(x;ξ)=(x;λξ),λ∈ℂ*.(5) m_\lambda(x;\xi)=(x;\lambda\xi),\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb C^*. \qquad\text{(5)}

A subset is positive-conic when invariant under all positive real λ\lambda, and complex-conic when it is a union of entire ℂ*\mathbb C^*-orbits. It is locally complex-conic when its intersection with each such orbit is open in that orbit. For a set closed in an open U⊂PU\subset P, this condition allows an orbit to leave UU; it asserts the local orbit directions at each point of the set. A zero covector has a singleton orbit.

If A⊂PA\subset P is closed complex analytic, positive conicity implies complex conicity. Fix (x;ξ)∈A(x;\xi)\in A with ξ≠0\xi\ne0 and pull back AA by the holomorphic orbit map λ↦(x;λξ)\lambda\mapsto(x;\lambda\xi) on ℂ*\mathbb C^*. Its inverse image is an analytic subset of the connected complex curve ℂ*\mathbb C^*. It contains every positive real λ\lambda, so the one-variable identity theorem makes it the entire curve. The zero orbit is already invariant. Conversely, complex conicity includes positive conicity.

This argument uses analyticity of AA. A closed positive real ray in a complex cotangent fibre does not become invariant under multiplication by ii.

The real Lagrangian recovery used here

Suppose U⊂PU\subset P is open and A⊂UA\subset U is relatively closed and involutive. Assume it is locally complex-conic and is contained in a relatively closed positive-conic subanalytic real-isotropic set A0⊂UA_0\subset U. Then the real recovery theorem gives

A is subanalytic and real Lagrangian,A=Areg¯U,dim⁡ℝAreg=2n.(6) A\text{ is subanalytic and real Lagrangian},\qquad A=\overline{A_{\mathrm{reg}}}^{\,U},\qquad \dim_{\mathbb R}A_{\mathrm{reg}}=2n. \qquad\text{(6)}

Involutivity here is the singular two-set normal-cone condition, not an assumption that the set is already smooth. Real isotropy of the containing set is the singular canonical-form condition of the earlier lessons. At smooth conic points it gives the usual symplectic isotropy.

Here is why the earlier real recovery argument applies in this relative open setting. On the regular locus of A0A_0, a relatively closed involutive subset of a smooth isotropic manifold is open; the smooth openness proof, using the local C1 flow and its differential, also forces dimension 2n2n. Its intersection there is therefore a union of regular components and is subanalytic. Let BB be this union. That residue is isotropic and has dimension less than 2n2n. The no-small-involutive-subset argument makes the remainder empty. Thus A=B¯UA=\overline B^{\,U}, which is subanalytic. Its regular points are simultaneously isotropic and coisotropic, hence real Lagrangian of dimension 2n2n; regular density gives the middle equality in (6).

All these steps take place in arbitrarily small ambient neighborhoods. The closed-set invariance proof requires closedness only in the chosen open flow domain and uses only times for which the trajectory exists there. The dimension theory, local finiteness of regular components and singular normal-cone involutivity remain the exact prerequisites from that earlier argument. Constructing the minimal complex Lagrangian hull

Fix a sufficiently small connected real analytic patch of S′S'. A real analytic manifold has a local complexification: real analytic coordinate functions extend holomorphically, with the real patch a totally real submanifold of that complexification. Complexify the embedding into PP, obtaining a holomorphic map

h:Sℂ′⟶P.(16) h:S'_{\mathbb C}\longrightarrow P. \qquad\text{(16)}

At points of the real patch, its complex differential image is the complex span in (15), so its rank is nn. Every (n+1)(n+1)-minor of the differential is holomorphic and vanishes on that real patch. The identity theorem on a totally real coordinate patch makes it vanish nearby. Some nn-minor is nonzero at the chosen point. After shrinking, hh has constant complex rank nn. The holomorphic constant-rank theorem therefore gives a complex nn-submanifold ZZ containing the patch of S′S'.

This ZZ is minimal as a germ: any complex submanifold containing the real patch contains the germ of ZZ. Compose its local holomorphic defining functions with hh. They vanish on the totally real patch, so they vanish on its complexification. That puts the image of hh in the given submanifold.

At points of S′S', (14)–(15) identify TZTZ with a limiting complex Lagrangian tangent plane of RR. Thus Ω|TZ=0\Omega|_{TZ}=0 there. Pull back this two-form along (16). Each holomorphic coefficient vanishes on the real patch and hence throughout its complexification. Since hh is a submersion onto ZZ, Ω|TZ=0\Omega|_{TZ}=0 nearby. Its dimension is nn, so ZZ is a complex Lagrangian submanifold.

The complexification, real-patch identity theorem and holomorphic constant-rank theorem are the complex-analysis inputs in this construction. Minimality is a germ assertion; it does not assert a global smallest analytic subset containing an arbitrary real set.

Regular points outside the hull cannot accumulate along an open boundary patch

S′∩R\Z¯ is nowhere dense in S′.(17) S'\cap\overline{R\setminus Z} \text{ is nowhere dense in }S'. \qquad\text{(17)}

The exact analytic removal step

The analytic removal criterion says that a relatively closed subanalytic subset of a complex manifold is complex analytic if, on every open restriction, the points where its germ is not a complex submanifold have real dimension at least two less than that restriction. An exact formulation is Peterzil–Starchenko, author manuscript, Corollary 4.2, PDF 11. For a pure complex regular locus this is the small-boundary theorem associated with Shiffman. The proof in the next section establishes this pure-dimensional case by constructing local holomorphic equations; the exceptional subset may be subanalytic without being complex analytic.

Apply the pure-dimensional theorem proved below to AA. By (6) its nonempty local restrictions have pure real dimension 2n2n. Every real regular point is complex regular by (11), so the complex singular points lie in DD. Equation (22) gives the required local dimension bound. Therefore AA is complex analytic in UU.

We have proved the complete application theorem: a relatively closed, locally complex-conic involutive set with a relatively closed positive-conic subanalytic real-isotropic bound is complex analytic. Its regular locus is complex Lagrangian of complex dimension nn. The extension step does not assume that DD is itself complex analytic. The empty set is analytic; when n=0n=0, the ambient manifold is discrete and the conclusion holds directly, with no boundary argument needed.

Proof of removal across a subanalytic exceptional set

The small-boundary theorem is classically associated with Shiffman. Peterzil–Starchenko, Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.2 state the subanalytic criterion and explain that attribution. We now prove the pure-dimensional case used above, beginning with scalar holomorphic extension and constructing local equations for the entire closed set.

The statement and its lower inputs

Theorem. Let AA be a relatively closed subanalytic subset of a complex manifold VV. Suppose that E⊂AE\subset A is relatively closed and subanalytic, that

R=A\E R=A\setminus E

dim⁡ℝE≤2d−2.(AR1) \dim_{\mathbb R}E\leq 2d-2. \qquad\text{(AR1)}

For this statement a submanifold need not be closed in all of VV; it is embedded and locally closed. Since EE and AA are closed, RR is open in AA, so its complex-manifold charts describe the entire set AA near each of its points. The case d=0d=0 is immediate: A=RA=R is locally a point. Stating this case separately avoids an ambiguity in conventions for the dimension of the empty set. The empty set is also immediate. Work henceforth with d≥1d\geq1.

On a fixed compact set, cover the compact exceptional trace in a slightly larger neighborhood by finitely many balls of arbitrarily small total volume. Outside their union the compact set stays a positive distance from FF, and u=fu=f is uniformly continuous on a compact neighborhood there. Consequently uϵ→uu_\epsilon\to u uniformly on that part. This proves uϵ→uu_\epsilon\to u in local L1L^1.

On a compact set K0K_0 inside a larger compact polydisc region K1K_1, the iterated holomorphic mean inequality gives

supK0|uϵ−uη|≤CK0,K1∥uϵ−uη∥L1(K1).(AR4) \sup_{K_0}|u_\epsilon-u_\eta| \leq C_{K_0,K_1}\|u_\epsilon-u_\eta\|_{L^1(K_1)}. \qquad\text{(AR4)}

Hence the convolutions converge locally uniformly to a holomorphic function, by the same Cauchy formula. A projection that is proper near the point to be filled

Fix p∈Ap\in A, take a coordinate ball in ℂN\mathbb C^N, and put p=0p=0. If N=dN=d, the set RR is open in the ambient manifold. In a small convex ball DD about pp, the set EE is thin, so D\ED\setminus E is connected. Its intersection with AA is open because it is R∩DR\cap D, closed because AA is closed, and nonempty because RR is dense in AA. It is therefore all of D\ED\setminus E, whose closure is DD; closedness then gives D⊂AD\subset A. We may assume N>dN>d, and write k=N−dk=N-d.

By closure invariance of subanalytic dimension, dim⁡ℝA=2d\dim_{\mathbb R}A=2d in a sufficiently small neighborhood of 00. Consider the lifted punctured set

L={u:(0,u)∈T¯}(AR5) L=\{u:(0,u)\in\overline T\} \qquad\text{(AR5)}

is compact and subanalytic. The strict-frontier inequality, applied to TT, gives

dim⁡ℝL≤2d−1.(AR6) \dim_{\mathbb R}L\leq 2d-1. \qquad\text{(AR6)}

We claim that a surjective complex-linear map P:ℂN→ℂdP:\mathbb C^N\to\mathbb C^d can be chosen with ker⁡P∩L=∅\ker P\cap L=\varnothing, and with rank dd at some point of every connected component of RR in the coordinate ball.

First partition LL into its finitely many smooth pieces. In the space of all complex dd-by-NN matrices, of real dimension 2dN2dN, the incidence condition Pu=0Pu=0 imposes 2d2d independent real linear equations for each nonzero uu. Over a piece of dimension ss, its solution incidence is a smooth vector bundle of dimension 2dN−2d+s≤2dN−12dN-2d+s\leq2dN-1. Its projection to matrix space is Lebesgue null: use countably many smooth coordinate patches and the elementary cube estimate above. Thus some matrices avoid LL. The avoiding set is open, because LL is compact. Surjective matrices are dense, since an appropriate maximal minor is a nonzero polynomial.

The complex manifold RR has countably many connected components. Select a point xνx_\nu in each. For every fixed complex tangent plane TxνRT_{x_\nu}R, the condition that PP restrict to an isomorphism there is the complement of the zero set of a nonzero polynomial in the entries of PP. Complete z=Pxz=Px to complex linear coordinates (z,w)∈ℂd×ℂk(z,w)\in\mathbb C^d\times\mathbb C^k. Choose a small fibre ball B={|w|<r}B=\{|w|<r\} and then a sufficiently small base ball DD about zero. A∩(D×∂B)=∅.(AR8) A\cap(D\times\partial B)=\varnothing. \qquad\text{(AR8)}

is proper. For a compact K⊂DK\subset D, its preimage is a closed subset of the compact cylinder K×B¯K\times\overline B, and (AR8) keeps every limiting point in BB. No discreteness of the exceptional fibres has been assumed.

The exceptional values and the finite holomorphic covering

Let

C={x∈R:rank⁡ℂd(π|R)x<d},F=π(E∪C),(AR10) C=\{x\in R:\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb C}d(\pi|_R)_x<d\}, \qquad F=\pi(E\cup C), \qquad\text{(AR10)}

with all sets restricted to the cylinder. The set CC is closed in RR, so E∪CE\cup C is closed in AA. Properness of (AR9) makes FF closed in DD. Here is the needed size estimate, without presuming that FF or EE is complex analytic.

By (AR1) and the analytic partition theorem, EE is covered by countably many smooth parameterized pieces of real dimension at most 2d−22d-2. On a connected complex coordinate patch in any connected component of RR, the holomorphic determinant of dπd\pi is not identically zero. Indeed, the exterior form dπ1∧⋯∧dπdd\pi_1\wedge\cdots\wedge d\pi_d is nonzero at the chosen point of that component; the identity theorem, along overlapping charts, forbids its vanishing on any open patch.

The zero set of a nonzero holomorphic function gg on a connected coordinate domain is covered by countably many smooth complex hypersurface patches. To see the asserted covering, take a zero xx. Some Taylor coefficient is nonzero by the identity theorem; let its least order be m≥1m\geq1. Choose a multi-index α\alpha of order m−1m-1 for which a first derivative of ∂αg\partial^\alpha g is nonzero at xx. Then

∂αg(x)=0,d(∂αg)x≠0. \partial^\alpha g(x)=0, \qquad d(\partial^\alpha g)_x\ne0.

The complex implicit function theorem makes the zero set of ∂αg\partial^\alpha g a smooth complex hypersurface near xx. For each of the countably many α\alpha, the noncritical part of this zero set has a countable chart cover. These patches cover all zeros of gg. The patches need not lie in the zero set of gg; containment of its zeros in their union is the only assertion needed.

Apply this observation to the determinant on countably many charts of RR. It shows that CC, and hence E∪CE\cup C, is contained in countably many smooth parameterized pieces of real dimension at most 2d−22d-2. Composing those parameterizations with the linear projection makes FF thin in DD. It has empty interior, its complement is path connected, and the extension lemma applies to bounded holomorphic functions on that complement.

On π−1(D\F)\pi^{-1}(D\setminus F), the complex inverse function theorem makes π\pi a local biholomorphism. Properness makes each fibre finite: it is both compact and discrete. A proper local homeomorphism with finite fibres is a covering of its image. To check this explicitly over a fibre {x1,…,xm}\{x_1,\ldots,x_m\}, choose disjoint inverse-coordinate neighborhoods of these points. If no smaller base neighborhood excluded additional preimages outside them, a sequence of such preimages over base points converging to the chosen value would, by properness, have a limiting preimage outside their union. This contradicts the displayed complete fibre. Shrinking now gives an evenly covered neighborhood.

The covering image is open and closed in D\FD\setminus F. It is nonempty: RR is dense in AA, the noncritical points are dense in each component of RR, and their local images are open, so one such image meets D\FD\setminus F. Consequently the image is all of the connected set D\FD\setminus F. Its sheet number is a fixed finite integer m≥1m\geq1.

We also record the density that will be needed at exceptional fibres:

A=π−1(D\F)¯inside D×B.(AR11) A=\overline{\pi^{-1}(D\setminus F)} \quad\text{inside }D\times B. \qquad\text{(AR11)}

Indeed, an arbitrary neighborhood of a point of AA meets RR; the noncritical points are dense in RR; near a noncritical point π\pi is open; and a nonempty open base image meets D\FD\setminus F. This reasoning remains valid in every prescribed neighborhood, proving (AR11).

Equations that retain the complete fibre points

Over an evenly covered neighborhood write the sheets as

w=a1(z),…,am(z),ai(z)∈B⊂ℂk. w=a_1(z),\ldots,a_m(z),\qquad a_i(z)\in B\subset\mathbb C^k.

For an auxiliary vector λ=(λ1,…,λk)\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k), use the complex bilinear pairing λ⋅w=∑jλjwj\lambda\cdot w=\sum_j\lambda_jw_j, and form

Q(z,w,λ)=∏i=1m(λ⋅(w−ai(z))).(AR12) Q(z,w,\lambda) =\prod_{i=1}^m\bigl(\lambda\cdot(w-a_i(z))\bigr). \qquad\text{(AR12)}

Permuting the sheets does not change this polynomial, so its coefficients as a polynomial in (w,λ)(w,\lambda) are single-valued holomorphic functions on D\FD\setminus F. They are bounded there: mm is fixed and every aia_i lies in the fixed bounded ball BB. Extend every one of its finitely many coefficients by the preceding lemma. Denote the resulting polynomial, with coefficients holomorphic on DD, by Q̃\widetilde Q.

Expand only in λ\lambda:

Q̃(z,w,λ)=∑|β|=mqβ(z,w)λβ. \widetilde Q(z,w,\lambda) =\sum_{|\beta|=m}q_\beta(z,w)\lambda^\beta.

The finitely many functions qβq_\beta are holomorphic on D×ℂkD\times\mathbb C^k. Let

Y={(z,w)∈D×B:qβ(z,w)=0 for every β}.(AR13) Y=\{(z,w)\in D\times B:q_\beta(z,w)=0 \text{ for every }\beta\}. \qquad\text{(AR13)}

This is a complex analytic subset. For z∉Fz\notin F, it is exactly the original fibre: a product of linear polynomials in λ\lambda vanishes identically precisely when one of its factors is the zero polynomial, because ℂ[λ1,…,λk]\mathbb C[\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k] is an integral domain. Thus (AR12) is identically zero in λ\lambda precisely when w=ai(z)w=a_i(z) for some ii. This is why we use the full vector pairing; separate coordinate root equations could add points made from coordinates belonging to different sheets.

By (AR11), closedness of YY, and equality over D\FD\setminus F, we have A⊂YA\subset Y. For the reverse inclusion fix (z0,w0)∈Y(z_0,w_0)\in Y. Choose zν∈D\Fz_\nu\in D\setminus F tending to z0z_0, enumerate each finite fibre arbitrarily as a1,ν,…,am,νa_{1,\nu},\ldots,a_{m,\nu}, and take a subsequence on which every coordinate of this finite tuple converges in B¯\overline B. Write its limit as b1,…,bmb_1,\ldots,b_m. Closedness of AA and (AR8) give

(z0,bi)∈A,bi∈B. (z_0,b_i)\in A,\qquad b_i\in B.

Continuity of the extended coefficients and the finite product gives the identity

Q̃(z0,w,λ)=∏i=1m(λ⋅(w−bi)).(AR14) \widetilde Q(z_0,w,\lambda) =\prod_{i=1}^m\bigl(\lambda\cdot(w-b_i)\bigr). \qquad\text{(AR14)}

Since (z0,w0)∈Y(z_0,w_0)\in Y, the left side is the zero polynomial in λ\lambda. The integral-domain argument again yields w0=biw_0=b_i for some ii. Therefore (z0,w0)∈A(z_0,w_0)\in A, proving Y⊂AY\subset A.

We have proved A=YA=Y near the arbitrary point pp. This proves the theorem, including discreteness of the exceptional fibres as a consequence, not a premise. ▫\square

Application to complex conicity

Take AA to be the closed subanalytic involutive set considered above, RR its dense real regular part and E=D=A\RE=D=A\setminus R. Its earlier proof gives pure real dimension 2n2n, makes every tangent plane of RR complex, and proves that RR is a complex submanifold of dimension nn. Equation (22) gives dim⁡ℝD≤2n−2\dim_{\mathbb R}D\leq2n-2 on every local restriction, and the subanalytic regular-locus provider makes DD closed and subanalytic. Thus the theorem above with d=nd=n supplies exactly the final analyticity step. The conicity, involutivity and isotropic-bound hypotheses are exactly those used in the preceding argument.

The theorem proved here is the pure-dimensional case used by this application. Peterzil–Starchenko’s broader criterion also permits regular components of differing dimensions.

Recovering a holomorphic covector from its real part

Solution. Formula (3) gives ξ=(3−2i)dz\xi=(3-2i)\,dz. Multiplication by ii gives (2+3i)dz(2+3i)\,dz, corresponding to 2dx−3dy2\,dx-3\,dy. Since i∂x=∂yi\partial_x=\partial_y and i∂y=−∂xi\partial_y=-\partial_x, (2) gives ξ(∂x)=3−2i\xi(\partial_x)=3-2i and ξ(∂y)=2+3i=i(3−2i)\xi(\partial_y)=2+3i=i(3-2i). A positive ray cannot be a closed complex analytic cone

Difficulty: Introductory.

In T*ℂT^*\mathbb C, fix x=0x=0 and set A={(0;ξ):ξ∈ℝ≥0}A=\{(0;\xi):\xi\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}\}. Show it is closed and positive-conic but not complex analytic. Find the smallest closed complex analytic subset of that fibre containing it.

Solution. The real ray is closed and invariant under positive multiplication. If it were complex analytic in the fibre ℂ\mathbb C, its holomorphic defining functions near any positive point would vanish on a real interval, hence on a complex neighborhood by the identity theorem. Analytic continuation along the fibre makes every holomorphic equation vanishing on the whole ray vanish identically. Its analytic closure is the full fibre T0*ℂT_0^*\mathbb C. The ray is not the full fibre; for example ii does not lie in it. The same conclusion follows from positive-to-complex conicity for a closed analytic subset: that result would force the entire orbit of ξ=1\xi=1.

A real conormal has only one Euler direction

Difficulty: Intermediate.

Let N=ℝ⊂ℂN=\mathbb R\subset\mathbb C. Express its real conormal under (1), check that it is real Lagrangian, and show why the calculation (9) fails to force a complex tangent plane.

Solution. Write z=x+iyz=x+iy and ξ=a+ib\xi=a+ib. The real tangent to NN is spanned by ∂x\partial_x, so its conormal is

Λ={y=0,a=0},z=x,ξ=ib.(23) \Lambda=\{y=0,\ a=0\}, \qquad z=x,\quad \xi=ib. \qquad\text{(23)}

It has real dimension two and tangent spanned by ∂x,∂b\partial_x,\partial_b. Since ω=da∧dx−db∧dy\omega=da\wedge dx-db\wedge dy, its symplectic restriction is zero, so it is real Lagrangian in the real four-dimensional cotangent manifold. The real Euler vector E=b∂bE=b\partial_b is tangent. At b≠0b\ne0, the imaginary Euler vector is iE=−b∂aiE=-b\partial_a, which is not tangent. Moreover α(∂x)=ib\alpha(\partial_x)=ib, so its real part is zero but its imaginary part is not. Equation (9) has lost its second test. Thus (23) is positive-conic but not locally complex-conic at a nonzero covector, and it is not a complex submanifold.

The minimal hull does not allow a half-Lagrangian boundary

Difficulty: Advanced.

In the zero section of T*ℂnT^*\mathbb C^n, for n≥1n\ge1, let S={Im⁡zn=0}S=\{\operatorname{Im}z_n=0\} and let B={Im⁡zn≥0}B=\{\operatorname{Im}z_n\ge0\}. Compute the complex span of TSTS, exhibit its complexification map, and test involutivity of BB at a boundary point.

Solution. The real tangent to SS contains ∂xj,∂yj\partial_{x_j},\partial_{y_j} for j<nj<n, and ∂xn\partial_{x_n}. Its complex span also contains i∂xn=∂yni\partial_{x_n}=\partial_{y_n}, hence is the full tangent of the zero section, of complex dimension nn. Complexifying the real coordinates gives the map

(u1,v1,…,un−1,vn−1,un)⟼(u1+iv1,…,un−1+ivn−1,un;0).(24) (u_1,v_1,\ldots,u_{n-1},v_{n-1},u_n) \longmapsto (u_1+iv_1,\ldots,u_{n-1}+iv_{n-1},u_n;0). \qquad\text{(24)}

It has complex rank nn, and its image is the zero section near the point. This is the minimal hull.

At a boundary point of BB, its point cone inside the zero-section tangent satisfies δyn≥0\delta y_n\ge0, while its two-set cone is the entire zero-section tangent plane: differences of two nonnegative yny_n-coordinates can have either sign. In the real cotangent coordinates of (3), the fibre coefficient of dyndy_n is −bn-b_n. The covector θ=−dbn\theta=-db_n on the ambient cotangent manifold annihilates the two-set cone. Thus BB is not involutive at its boundary, although its complex regular locus is Lagrangian. This is the missing hypothesis that the openness step uses.

A crossing has a permissible boundary of real codimension two

Difficulty: Intermediate.

For X=ℂX=\mathbb C, put A={ξ=0}∪{z=0}⊂T*XA=\{\xi=0\}\cup\{z=0\}\subset T^*X. Identify its regular and singular parts, verify isotropy and involutivity, and compare its singular dimension with (22).

Solution. The equation zξ=0z\xi=0 makes AA closed complex analytic and complex-conic. Its regular locus is the two punctured complex lines, each of complex dimension one and real dimension two. Their intersection (0;0)(0;0) is the only singular point, of real dimension zero. The canonical form ξdz\xi\,dz vanishes on both regular lines, so the subanalytic singular one-form criterion gives isotropy including their closure.

Each regular line is Lagrangian, hence involutive. At the crossing, the two-set cone is the full ambient underlying real ℂ2\mathbb C^2: a scaled point (hz,0)(hz,0) on one line minus (0,−hξ)(0,-h\xi) on the other realizes any vector (z,ξ)(z,\xi). Its annihilator is zero. The singular involutivity implication therefore has only the zero covector to test there, and it holds. Thus AA is involutive everywhere. With n=1n=1, (22) says the singular dimension is at most zero, and this example attains it. The theorem removes hypersurface boundaries, not isolated intersections of complex branches.

Complexification rank is not half of real rank

Difficulty: Intermediate.

Compare the real analytic maps f(t)=(t;0)f(t)=(t;0) and g(s,t)=(s+it;0)g(s,t)=(s+it;0) into T*ℂT^*\mathbb C. Compute their real ranks and the complex ranks of their complexifications. Explain the bound (19).

Solution. The map ff has real rank one. Its complexification fℂ(u)=(u;0)f_{\mathbb C}(u)=(u;0) has complex rank one, not zero and not a half-integer. The map gg has real rank two. Its complexification gℂ(u,v)=(u+iv;0)g_{\mathbb C}(u,v)=(u+iv;0) has complex rank one; its differential has kernel {δu+iδv=0}\{\delta u+i\delta v=0\}. Both differential images lie in the complex one-dimensional tangent plane to the zero section. That containment bounds the dimension of their complex spans by one. In (19) the target tangent plane has complex dimension nn; this is why all larger holomorphic minors vanish, even where the original map has less than maximal real rank.

Analytic conicity alone does not give generic conormality

Difficulty: Advanced.

Let X=ℂX=\mathbb C, Λ=T*X\Lambda=T^*X, and Y=XY=X. Show that Λ\Lambda is closed, complex analytic and complex-conic, but no open dense Y0⊂YY_0\subset Y satisfies Λ∩π−1Y0⊂TY0*X\Lambda\cap\pi^{-1}Y_0\subset T_{Y_0}^*X. Also show that finitely many closed analytic base conormals cannot cover Λ\Lambda.

Solution. All three properties of Λ\Lambda hold because it is the whole cotangent manifold. An open subset Y0Y_0 of XX has full base tangent space, so its conormal is the zero section over Y0Y_0. At every point of a nonempty such subset, Λ\Lambda contains nonzero covectors; these are not in that conormal. Open density forces Y0Y_0 nonempty, proving failure of the generic assertion.

Every proper closed complex analytic subset of the connected complex line is discrete. A finite union of such subsets remains closed discrete locally and has a nonempty open complement. A closed analytic base equal to all of XX contributes only the zero section. At a point outside the finitely many proper bases, none of their conormals has a fibre, and the all-base conormals have only the zero covector. They cannot cover the nonzero cotangent fibre there.

The omitted condition is isotropy: the whole T*XT^*X has nonzero canonical form on its regular locus and has complex dimension two, greater than the Lagrangian dimension one. The conormal-cover theorem must retain isotropy, as in the real cover theorem and the Lagrangian microsupport applications. This counterexample shows the exact mathematical obstruction when that condition is omitted.

The geometric input for complex constructibility

The result separates three mechanisms. Involutivity and a subanalytic isotropic bound recover real Lagrangian regularity. The two complex Euler directions make those regular tangent planes complex. Minimal complex hulls and involutive openness exclude a boundary of real codimension one; the proved small-boundary removal step then makes the whole set analytic. Applying this theorem to involutive microsupport will be one step in the complex constructibility criteria. The analytic conormal-cover and complex stratification arguments retain their own isotropy and geometry hypotheses.
