Analytic conormal covers and singular involutivity

The conormal of a singular analytic set consists of limits of conormals at its smooth points. Those limits can be much smaller than the whole cotangent fibre over a singular point. We will prove that this conormal is analytic, explain why its regular Lagrangian geometry also gives the real involutivity condition at singular points, and cover any closed analytic isotropic cone by finitely many such conormals. The finite family can contain bases with infinitely many components.

Let XX be a complex manifold of complex dimension nn, Hausdorff and countable at infinity. Put P=T*XP=T^*X, π:P→X\pi:P\to X, and use

α=∑jξjdzj,Ω=dα=∑jdξj∧dzj,ω=Re⁡Ω.(1) \alpha=\sum_j\xi_j\,dz_j,\qquad \Omega=d\alpha=\sum_jd\xi_j\wedge dz_j,\qquad \omega=\operatorname{Re}\Omega. \qquad\text{(1)}

We use the real covector identification and analytic-piece convention of Complex conicity and analytic Lagrangian closures. Fibre conicity in this lesson means invariance under all ℂ*\mathbb C^* dilations. Isotropy of a subanalytic cone means vanishing of the canonical form in the singular one-form sense; on a closed complex analytic cone this is equivalent to vanishing of α\alpha on its regular locus. There are no coefficient or derived-shift assumptions in this geometric lesson.

The cover and generic-base statements below explicitly require isotropy. The full cotangent bundle gives a counterexample when that hypothesis is omitted. The source account below identifies the analytic-ideal, specialization and image theorems used in the constructions.

The exact analytic geometry inputs

We use the programme proofs of local finite generation of reduced analytic ideals, analyticity of the singular locus, locally finite global components, and dimension on an irreducible germ. The global component proof also gives connected dense regular loci, so each irreducible component has one dimension. Closure of an analytic set after deleting an analytic subset retains precisely the components not contained in the deleted set, by the deletion argument on connected regular loci. The complex deformation lesson proves the application of that component rule to real positive normal cones; in particular, those cones for analytic pieces are complex analytic and complex-conic.

The proper holomorphic image theorem is proved in the Weierstrass and component lesson. It applies to maps proper on the actual closed analytic carrier, including the compact Grassmann incidences used here. For a holomorphic image that is subanalytic, the connected maximal-rank regions and the dimension and fibre calculation identify its real dimension with twice the maximum generic complex rank on the regular irreducible source components. Peterzil–Starchenko, Theorem 6.1 and Claim 1 in its proof, manuscript PDF 16–17, give the broader closed-image theorem and its rank account. The image and dimension contracts used here thus have explicit programme proof suppliers.

The reduced-fibre theorem proved below, Theorem CS4, shows that intersecting a reduced involutive analytic set with the zero fibre of a holomorphic function preserves involutivity whenever its Hamiltonian field is tangent to the set. The finite ramified charts of Lemma CS3 supply the required central tangent planes. The Casimir case, where the Hamiltonian field is zero, is Kashiwara–Monteiro-Fernandes, Corollary 1.1.14, printed p. 397 / PDF 6. The argument below shows how this proved specialization gives the real singular condition; regular coisotropy alone is not used as a definition of that condition.

From regular coisotropy to the real normal-cone condition

Recall the Hamiltonian convention

ιHθω=−θ.(2) \iota_{H\theta}\omega=-\theta. \qquad\text{(2)}

For a locally closed real subset S⊂PS\subset P, involutivity at p∈Sp\in S means

Cp(S,S)⊂ker⁡θ⟹−Hθ∈Cp(S)(θ∈Tp*P real).(3) C_p(S,S)\subset\ker\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -H\theta\in C_p(S) \qquad(\theta\in T_p^*P\text{ real}). \qquad\text{(3)}

The first cone uses differences of two points of SS, and the second uses differences from the fixed point pp; both use positive real scales. Their precise definitions and signs are in Involutive subsets of subanalytic isotropic sets.

Theorem. A closed complex analytic subset S⊂PS\subset P whose regular tangent spaces are complex coisotropic satisfies (3). No fibre-conicity assumption is needed for this theorem.

Proof. First consider holomorphic functions g,hg,h vanishing on SS. At a regular point their differentials annihilate TSTS. Coisotropy makes their Poisson bracket zero there. Regular density and continuity give

{ℐS,ℐS}⊂ℐS,(4) \{\mathcal I_S,\mathcal I_S\}\subset\mathcal I_S, \qquad\text{(4)}

where ℐS\mathcal I_S is the reduced ideal. Conversely, (4) implies coisotropy at a regular point because differentials of its local defining functions span its conormal. This converse will also be used for a specialized cone.

Fix pp. Use canonical holomorphic coordinates on PP near pp, so that Ω\Omega has constant coefficients. On the open chart where p+tvp+tv stays in that coordinate neighborhood, take the accessible analytic closure

D={(v,t):t≠0,p+tv∈S}¯.(5) D=\overline{\{(v,t):t\ne0,\ p+tv\in S\}}. \qquad\text{(5)}

The component rule makes DD analytic and excludes components existing only at t=0t=0. Its central fibre is exactly the positive-real point cone,

D∩{t=0}=Cp(S)×{0}.(6) D\cap\{t=0\}=C_p(S)\times\{0\}. \qquad\text{(6)}

For (6), apply the earlier analytic pair-normal-cone argument to (S,{p})(S,\{p\}). Its positive-power reparameterization is what identifies the complex central fibre with the cone using positive real scales.

Give the vv coordinates the constant Poisson tensor associated with Ωp\Omega_p, and make tt a Casimir: its bracket with every function is zero. At t≠0t\ne0, the map v↦p+tvv\mapsto p+tv scales the symplectic form by t2t^2. Each slice of (5) is consequently coisotropic. For two local holomorphic functions vanishing on DD, compute their bracket using only their vv derivatives. It vanishes at every regular point of every nonzero slice. Those points are dense in DD, so the bracket vanishes on all of DD. Thus its reduced ideal is bracket-closed.

Apply Theorem CS4 to the Casimir tt. We conclude that

C=Cp(S)⊂TpPis complex analytic and coisotropic at its regular points(7) C=C_p(S)\subset T_pP \quad\text{is complex analytic and coisotropic at its regular points} \qquad\text{(7)}

for the constant form Ωp\Omega_p. This step concerns the point cone, including its singular points, rather than a guessed limiting tangent space of SS.

Suppose now that θ\theta satisfies the premise in (3). Since p∈Sp\in S, every sequence defining Cp(S)C_p(S) also defines a two-set sequence with second point pp. Thus θ\theta annihilates CC. The cone is complex-conic by the analytic normal-cone theorem. Therefore the complex-linear covector

ℓ(v)=θ(v)−iθ(iv)(8) \ell(v)=\theta(v)-i\theta(iv) \qquad\text{(8)}

vanishes on CC: both vv and iviv belong to that cone. Let uu be the constant complex Hamiltonian vector satisfying ιuΩp=−ℓ\iota_u\Omega_p=-\ell. Its underlying real vector is HθH\theta, by taking real parts of that equality and using nondegeneracy of ωp\omega_p.

At a regular point of CC, dℓd\ell annihilates its tangent space. By (7), uu belongs to that tangent space. Hence the constant derivation uu preserves the reduced analytic ideal of CC: for any ideal function its derivative is zero on the regular locus, then on all of CC. To verify flow preservation also at the vertex, choose local ideal generators g1,…,grg_1,\ldots,g_r near zero. There are holomorphic coefficients aija_{ij} with

u(gi)=∑jaijgj.(9) u(g_i)=\sum_j a_{ij}g_j. \qquad\text{(9)}

Along the real translation w(s)=suw(s)=su, the vector (gi(w(s)))i(g_i(w(s)))_i solves the corresponding linear ordinary differential equation with initial value zero. Uniqueness gives su∈Csu\in C for sufficiently small positive and negative ss. The local C1 uniqueness proof applies to this equation by adjoining the time variable: the real system is (s,g)′=(1,A(s)g)(s,g)'= (1,A(s)g), with real and imaginary parts of the holomorphic matrix coefficients. Its zero initial-value solution is therefore g=0g=0 in both time directions. Positive conicity then gives −u∈C-u\in C. As u=Hθu=H\theta in real coordinates, this is (3). If u=0u=0, the conclusion holds because the cone contains zero. All reasoning was local near the arbitrary point pp. ▫\square

For an analytic set with Lagrangian regular tangent spaces, coisotropy is available and the theorem gives real singular involutivity. In the conic case, regular symplectic isotropy makes α\alpha vanish as well: the Euler vector E=∑ξj∂ξjE=\sum\xi_j\partial_{\xi_j} is tangent and ιEΩ=α\iota_E\Omega=\alpha. Singular one-form calculus extends this vanishing from the dense regular locus. Thus a closed analytic conic set with Lagrangian regular locus is Lagrangian also in the earlier real singular sense.

The tangent graph and the analytic conormal closure

For a closed complex analytic S⊂XS\subset X, define

ΣS=TSreg*X¯T*X.(10) \Sigma_S=\overline{T^*_{S_{\mathrm{reg}}}X}^{\,T^*X}. \qquad\text{(10)}

We prove that ΣS\Sigma_S is closed complex analytic, complex-conic, and pure of complex dimension nn, unless it is empty. Its regular tangent spaces are Lagrangian, and the preceding theorem gives its real singular involutivity.

First assume SS is pure of complex dimension dd. In a coordinate chart U⊂XU\subset X, choose holomorphic generators f1,…,frf_1,\ldots,f_r for its reduced ideal. Let Gr⁡d(ℂn)\operatorname{Gr}_d(\mathbb C^n) be the compact complex Grassmannian. The incidence conditions

x∈S,dfj(x)|L=0(1≤j≤r)(11) x\in S,\qquad df_j(x)|_L=0\quad(1\le j\le r) \qquad\text{(11)}

define an analytic subset of U×Gr⁡d(ℂn)U\times\operatorname{Gr}_d(\mathbb C^n). Over SregS_{\mathrm{reg}}, the common kernel of the differentials is precisely TxST_xS, of dimension dd; thus (11) has the unique plane L=TxSL=T_xS there. Delete the analytic inverse image of SsingS_{\mathrm{sing}}, then take the component closure. The result is the analytic tangent graph

ΓS={(x,TxS):x∈Sreg}¯.(12) \Gamma_S=\overline{\{(x,T_xS):x\in S_{\mathrm{reg}}\}}. \qquad\text{(12)}

Its projection to SS is proper because the Grassmannian is compact. Each retained component has dimension dd and contains a dense regular tangent-graph part.

Over ΓS\Gamma_S, impose ξ|L=0\xi|_L=0. This is the restriction of a holomorphic vector bundle of rank n−dn-d; its total incidence set

ES={(x,L,ξ):(x,L)∈ΓS,ξ|L=0}(13) E_S=\{(x,L,\xi):(x,L)\in\Gamma_S,\ \xi|_L=0\} \qquad\text{(13)}

is analytic. The map q(x,L,ξ)=(x,ξ)q(x,L,\xi)=(x,\xi) is proper: over a compact cotangent set the omitted Grassmann coordinates remain compact. The proper holomorphic image theorem therefore makes q(ES)q(E_S) closed analytic.

This image equals (10). It is closed and contains the ordinary regular conormal. Conversely, take (x,L,ξ)∈ES(x,L,\xi)\in E_S. By (12), choose xk∈Sregx_k\in S_{\mathrm{reg}} with (xk,TxkS)→(x,L)(x_k,T_{x_k}S)\to(x,L). Continuity of the annihilator subspaces allows ξk∈(TxkS)⟂\xi_k\in(T_{x_k}S)^\perp with ξk→ξ\xi_k\to\xi. For example, in a fixed Hermitian metric project ξ\xi onto these annihilator spaces. Hence (x,ξ)(x,\xi) is a limit of ordinary conormals, as required.

Each component of (13) has dimension d+(n−d)=nd+(n-d)=n. On its dense regular conormal part, qq is one-to-one and identifies that part with an nn-dimensional ordinary conormal. Each proper analytic component image is therefore of dimension nn. Properness keeps the image family locally finite. This proves purity of ΣS\Sigma_S. For nonpure SS, apply this argument to the locally finite irreducible components, grouped by their finitely many possible dimensions. The closure of the whole regular conormal is the union of their conormal closures: intersections and smaller component singularities can be deleted without changing those closures. The same proper and local-finiteness argument proves analyticity and purity in this case.

On an ordinary conormal α\alpha vanishes, since its covector annihilates every projected tangent vector. It consequently vanishes on the closure by singular one-form calculus. At a regular point of the analytic closure, differentiation gives Ω|TΣS=0\Omega|_{T\Sigma_S}=0. Purity gives complex dimension nn, so that regular tangent space is Lagrangian. Fibre scaling preserves the ordinary conormal and its closure. The result about real singular involutivity now applies to ΣS\Sigma_S, proving every claimed property.

The Grassmann incidence retains limiting tangent planes. It does not replace them by the possibly larger tangent space obtained by linearizing equations at a singular point.

What the ideal-generator formula actually requires

With the reduced ideal generators used in (11), the exact formula is

ΣS∩T*U={(x,∑j=1rλjdfj(x)):x∈S∩U,λj∈ℂ}¯T*U.(14) \Sigma_S\cap T^*U =\overline{\left\{\left(x,\sum_{j=1}^r\lambda_jdf_j(x)\right): x\in S\cap U,\ \lambda_j\in\mathbb C\right\}}^{\,T^*U}. \qquad\text{(14)}

At a regular point, the differential span is exactly the conormal, which proves one inclusion after closure. At a singular point, fix the finitely many coefficients λj\lambda_j. Choose regular xk→xx_k\to x. Then ∑jλjdfj(xk)→∑jλjdfj(x)\sum_j\lambda_jdf_j(x_k)\to\sum_j\lambda_jdf_j(x). Thus every point of the set inside the bar already lies in the regular conormal closure, proving the other inclusion. Arbitrarily large coefficients are allowed before taking closure; they are sometimes necessary for a nonzero limiting covector.

Equations having the correct zero set do not suffice if their differentials fail to generate the regular conormal. For S={0}⊂ℂS=\{0\}\subset\mathbb C, the equation z2=0z^2=0 has differential zero on all of SS. Its differential-span closure is only (0,0)(0,0), whereas ΣS=T0*ℂ\Sigma_S=T_0^*\mathbb C. Generators of the reduced ideal, here zz, give the correct formula. Thus the differential-span construction requires generators of the reduced ideal, not merely equations with the right zero set.

Conicity makes the projected component family locally finite

Let Λ⊂P\Lambda\subset P be closed complex analytic and complex-conic. Each irreducible component Λi\Lambda_i is itself complex-conic. To check this, choose a regular point belonging to that component alone. For λ\lambda near 1, its image remains in the unique component near the point, so mλ(Λi)=Λim_\lambda(\Lambda_i)=\Lambda_i. More explicitly, the component image and Λi\Lambda_i have a common nonempty relatively open germ, so irreducibility identifies them. The stabilizer of Λi\Lambda_i is thus an open subgroup of the connected group ℂ*\mathbb C^*; it is the whole group.

Closedness and λ→0\lambda\to0 put the zero covector over every projected point into the same component. Consequently,

Ni=π(Λi)={x:(x,0)∈Λi}(15) N_i=\pi(\Lambda_i) =\{x:(x,0)\in\Lambda_i\} \qquad\text{(15)}

is closed complex analytic, by inverse image under the holomorphic zero section. Projection need not be proper here. The equality in (15), rather than a general nonproper image theorem, proves closedness and analyticity of this particular image.

The family (Ni)(N_i) is locally finite on XX. A neighborhood of (x0,0)(x_0,0) in PP meets only finitely many Λi\Lambda_i. Choose it to contain a product of a base neighborhood with a small fibre ball. If NiN_i meets that base neighborhood, (15) puts a point of Λi\Lambda_i at zero in the product. Thus only those finitely many projected components can occur. This zero-section argument prevents components escaping to infinity in the fibres from creating an accumulation of projected bases.

Let

di=max⁡rank⁡ℂ(dπ|TΛi,reg).(16) d_i=\max\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb C} (d\pi|_{T\Lambda_{i,\mathrm{reg}}}). \qquad\text{(16)}

The holomorphic rank-dimension prerequisite, applied to the closed analytic image (15), gives dim⁡ℂNi=di\dim_{\mathbb C}N_i=d_i. The image is irreducible: two proper analytic closed subsets covering it would pull back to two analytic subsets covering Λi\Lambda_i, and one would have to contain the whole component. Thus NiN_i is pure of that dimension. These uses of dimension are explicit; density alone would not prove (16) equals the image dimension.

A finite cover by closed analytic base conormals

Theorem. If Λ⊂T*X\Lambda\subset T^*X is closed complex analytic, complex-conic and isotropic, there are at most n+1n+1 closed complex analytic subsets Xd⊂π(Λ)X_d\subset\pi(\Lambda), indexed by 0≤d≤n0\le d\le n, such that

Λ⊂⋃d=0nΣXd.(17) \Lambda\subset\bigcup_{d=0}^n\Sigma_{X_d}. \qquad\text{(17)}

Empty bases can be omitted. The bases need not be smooth, connected, or strata of a partition.

Proof. Group the components above by did_i, and set

Λ(d)=⋃di=dΛi,Xd=⋃di=dNi.(18) \Lambda^{(d)}=\bigcup_{d_i=d}\Lambda_i, \qquad X_d=\bigcup_{d_i=d}N_i. \qquad\text{(18)}

Local finiteness makes both unions closed analytic; nonempty XdX_d is pure of dimension dd. The cone Λ(d)\Lambda^{(d)} is isotropic as a subset of Λ\Lambda.

On each Λi\Lambda_i, maximum-rank regular points are open dense. After deleting component intersections, these are also regular points of Λ(d)\Lambda^{(d)}. Points over (Xd)reg(X_d)_{\mathrm{reg}} are dense among them. Indeed, otherwise a rank-dd neighborhood would have its local dd-dimensional complex image inside the singular part of XdX_d, whose dimension is smaller than dd. The constant-rank theorem and dimension monotonicity contradict that possibility.

At such a good point p=(x,ξ)p=(x,\xi), the projection differential maps onto Tx(Xd)regT_x(X_d)_{\mathrm{reg}}. Given uu in that base tangent space, choose v∈TpΛ(d)v\in T_p\Lambda^{(d)} projecting to it. Then

ξ(u)=αp(v)=0.(19) \xi(u)=\alpha_p(v)=0. \qquad\text{(19)}

Thus the dense good subset lies in the ordinary conormal of (Xd)reg(X_d)_{\mathrm{reg}}. Its closure contains all of Λ(d)\Lambda^{(d)}, and (10) gives Λ(d)⊂ΣXd\Lambda^{(d)}\subset\Sigma_{X_d}. Taking the finite union proves (17). ▫\square

In particular, conic analyticity alone is insufficient: if Λ=T*ℂ\Lambda=T^*\mathbb C, its projection rank is 1 and its covectors need not annihilate the tangent of the one-dimensional base. The failed equality in (19) identifies precisely where isotropy is needed.

Analytic closures of the critical rank loci

The generic-base theorem requires analytic exceptional sets, so the real subanalytic Sard argument alone is not enough. Here is the additional rank-closure argument.

Let BB be pure-dimensional closed complex analytic in a complex manifold MM, and let H⊂BH\subset B be closed analytic and contain its singular locus. Assume R=B\HR=B\setminus H is dense in BB. Let g:M→Ng:M\to N be holomorphic. For an integer ee, put

Q={b∈R:rank⁡ℂ(dgb|TbB)<e}.(20) Q=\{b\in R:\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb C}(dg_b|_{T_bB})<e\}. \qquad\text{(20)}

Then Q¯M\overline Q^{\,M} is complex analytic. To prove it, build the analytic tangent graph ΓB\Gamma_B just as in (11)–(12), now with planes in TMTM. Its projection r:ΓB→Br:\Gamma_B\to B is proper. r(Z′)=Q¯M.(21) r(Z')=\overline Q^{\,M}. \qquad\text{(21)}

For one inclusion, lift every point of Z′Z' by its approximating graph points. For the other, a convergent sequence in QQ has a subsequence of tangent planes in the compact Grassmannian fibre. Reduced fibres and Casimir specialization

All analytic sets below carry their reduced structures. A holomorphic Poisson tensor on a complex manifold MM is denoted by Π\Pi, with {g,h}=Π(dg,dh)\{g,h\}=\Pi(dg,dh). A linear subspace L⊂TxML\subset T_xM is coisotropic when Πx(α,β)=0\Pi_x(\alpha,\beta)=0 for every α,β∈L⟂\alpha,\beta\in L^\perp. This definition permits a degenerate Poisson tensor.

We use the proper holomorphic image theorem proved in the Weierstrass and component lesson: the image of a closed analytic set under a proper holomorphic map is a closed analytic set. Its source may be a reduced analytic space; equivalently, one applies the assertion to the graph in local ambient charts. The proof below uses this theorem only for local proper maps with finite fibres and for holomorphic coordinate functions on compact analytic sets.

The remaining local inputs have programme proofs: finite local parametrization and dimension, coherence of the reduced ideal and the Jacobian description of singular points, and locally finite components and dense regular loci. The inverse and constant-rank theorems are used on complex manifolds.

Isolated fibres and one equation

Lemma CS1 (a proper finite representative). Let YY be an analytic set near a∈𝑪Na\in\mathbf C^N, and let F:Y→𝑪mF:Y\to\mathbf C^m be holomorphic. If aa is isolated in the germ of F−1(F(a))F^{-1}(F(a)), there is a neighbourhood of aa on which FF, restricted over a sufficiently small target neighbourhood, is proper and has finite fibres.

Proof. Translate so that F(a)=0F(a)=0. Choose a closed ball B¯\overline B centered at aa, contained in the domain of representatives, such that Y∩B¯∩F−1(0)={a}Y\cap\overline B\cap F^{-1}(0)=\{a\}. Compactness gives a target neighbourhood WW of zero for which F−1(W)∩Y∩∂B=∅F^{-1}(W)\cap Y\cap\partial B=\varnothing. Then

Y′=Y∩B∩F−1(W)⟶W(CS1) Y'=Y\cap B\cap F^{-1}(W)\longrightarrow W \qquad\text{(CS1)}

is proper: the inverse image of a compact subset of WW is closed in the compact set Y∩B¯Y\cap\overline B, and cannot meet its boundary.

Every fibre KK of (CS1) is a compact analytic subset of the open ball. Each ambient coordinate wj:K→𝑪w_j:K\to\mathbf C is a proper holomorphic map. The proper-image theorem makes wj(K)w_j(K) a compact analytic subset of 𝑪\mathbf C. Such a subset is finite: a proper analytic subset of the connected plane has isolated points, and a compact discrete analytic subset is finite. Thus KK lies in a finite product of finite coordinate sets, and is finite. □

We record two elementary dimension consequences. A holomorphic map with finite fibres from a pure rr-dimensional analytic set has rank rr somewhere on the regular locus of each local irreducible component. Otherwise choose a point where the rank on that component is maximal; on a neighbourhood of that regular point the rank is constant and less than rr. The constant-rank theorem supplies a positive-dimensional local fibre, a contradiction. In particular the target dimension is at least rr.

Also, the image of an analytic set of complex dimension at most ss under a holomorphic map cannot contain an open subset of 𝑪s+1\mathbf C^{s+1}. Here is a direct justification sufficient for its use below. Split each regular component into its open maximal-rank locus and the analytic rank-drop locus, and include the analytic singular locus in the latter stage. Repeat on the lower-dimensional sets. The dimension strictly decreases, so this gives a countable cover by complex manifolds on which the map has constant rank at most ss. Each is covered by countably many relatively compact constant-rank coordinate patches. The image of each such patch has real 2s+22s+2-dimensional measure zero, since locally it is contained in a smooth submanifold of real dimension at most 2s2s. A countable union still has measure zero and cannot contain an open set. This argument uses no analyticity assertion for a nonproper image.

Specialization of a reduced involutive ideal

Theorem CS4. Let (M,Π)(M,\Pi) be a smooth complex Poisson manifold. Let Y⊂MY\subset M be a closed reduced analytic subset satisfying

{ℐY,ℐY}⊂ℐY.(CS7) \{\mathcal I_Y,\mathcal I_Y\}\subset\mathcal I_Y. \qquad\text{(CS7)}

If f∈𝒪(M)f\in\mathcal O(M) has Hamiltonian field tangent to YY, then the reduced analytic zero fibre Y0=Y∩{f=0}Y_0=Y\cap\{f=0\} also satisfies (CS7). In particular this holds when ff is a Casimir, meaning that its Hamiltonian field is identically zero.

Proof. At a regular point of YY, (CS7) says exactly that TyYT_yY is coisotropic: differentials of reduced ideal generators span its conormal. Conversely, coisotropy on a dense set of regular points of every component implies (CS7), since the bracket of any two ideal functions is holomorphic and vanishes on that dense set. This equivalence also shows that each local irreducible component of YY is involutive: away from the other components its regular locus is part of the regular locus of YY, and density extends the bracket identity to the component. Tangency of HfH_f to each component follows in the same way by differentiating its local ideal functions on that dense locus.

It therefore suffices to treat one pure-dimensional local irreducible component. If ff vanishes identically on it, its zero fibre is the same component and there is nothing to prove. Otherwise take h=fh=f in CS3, and consider a central component E⊂Y0E\subset Y_0. At the nonzero-parameter points of the resulting chart, put

Lz,u=Tϕ(z,u)Y∩ker⁡df,u≠0.(CS8) L_{z,u}=T_{\phi(z,u)}Y\cap\ker df, \qquad u\ne0. \qquad\text{(CS8)}

Since df|TY≠0df|_{TY}\ne0, elementary linear algebra gives

Lz,u⟂=(Tϕ(z,u)Y)⟂+𝑪df.(CS9) L_{z,u}^{\perp}=(T_{\phi(z,u)}Y)^{\perp}+\mathbf C\,df. \qquad\text{(CS9)}

The Poisson tensor vanishes on pairs from the first summand by (CS7). It vanishes on a pair α,df\alpha,df, with α∈(TY)⟂\alpha\in(TY)^\perp, because HfH_f is tangent to YY. It vanishes on df,dfdf,df by alternation. Thus Lz,uL_{z,u} is coisotropic.

Fix zz. By CS3 the columns dzϕ(z,u)d_z\phi(z,u) are a holomorphic family of r−1r-1 independent vectors even at u=0u=0. Their spans tend to Tϕ(z,0)ET_{\phi(z,0)}E. Coisotropy is closed under this limit. Explicitly, an invertible minor of the column matrix remains invertible near u=0u=0; solving with that minor produces a continuous local basis of its annihilator. Evaluate Π\Pi on pairs of these covectors and pass to u=0u=0. All resulting pairings are zero. Hence EE is coisotropic at every central point provided by CS3, a dense set in EE.

For local holomorphic g1,g2g_1,g_2 vanishing on Y0Y_0, their differentials annihilate TETE at these points, so {g1,g2}=0\{g_1,g_2\}=0 there. Continuity makes the bracket zero on all of EE. Do this for every central component of each original component. There are finitely many in a sufficiently small representative, and they cover Y0Y_0. Therefore the bracket vanishes on Y0Y_0, which is exactly bracket closure of its reduced ideal. This argument includes mixed-dimensional YY by the initial component reduction. □

The proof does not say that the radical of an arbitrary bracket-closed ideal is bracket-closed. For example, in 𝑪2\mathbf C^2 with {x,y}=1\{x,y\}=1, the ideal (x,y)2(x,y)^2 is bracket-closed whereas its radical (x,y)(x,y) is not. CS4 uses reducedness of the original set, the Hamiltonian tangency condition, and the analytic family of tangent planes produced above.

Applying the theorem to the point cone

Use the canonical holomorphic coordinates and the accessible deformation

D={(v,t):t≠0,p+tv∈S}¯⊂TpP×𝑪 D=\overline{\{(v,t):t\ne0,\ p+tv\in S\}} \subset T_pP\times\mathbf C

from the complex deformation proof. It is analytic, has no component contained in t=0t=0, and its reduced central fibre is Cp(S)×{0}C_p(S)\times\{0\}. Give the vv-space its constant Poisson tensor and declare tt to be a Casimir. For t≠0t\ne0, the map v↦p+tvv\mapsto p+tv scales the symplectic form by t2t^2, so the regular nonzero slices are coisotropic. They are dense in DD, and the bracket of two functions vanishing on DD, computed in the vv variables, vanishes there and hence on DD. Thus CS4 applies.

Consequently D∩{t=0}D\cap\{t=0\} has bracket-closed reduced ideal in the product Poisson manifold. Functions vanishing on Cp(S)C_p(S) extend independently of tt, and their product Poisson bracket is their constant symplectic bracket in vv. It follows that the reduced ideal of Cp(S)⊂TpPC_p(S)\subset T_pP is bracket-closed. At its regular points the tangent spaces are coisotropic. This is precisely the assertion needed before the constant-Hamiltonian flow argument at the cone vertex; that flow argument requires no change.

Reduced equations and large coefficients

Difficulty: Introductory.

Compare formula (14) for S={0}⊂ℂS=\{0\}\subset\mathbb C using zz and z2z^2. For the cusp S={y2=x3}⊂ℂ2S=\{y^2=x^3\}\subset\mathbb C^2, explain why restricting the coefficient of d(y2−x3)d(y^2-x^3) to a bounded set loses nonzero conormal limits at the origin.

Solution. The reduced generator zz gives every covector λdz\lambda dz at zero. The equation z2z^2 gives only zero there and therefore fails despite having the same zero set. On the cusp, write (x,y)=(t2,t3)(x,y)=(t^2,t^3). Then

d(y2−x3)=(−3t4,2t3),λ=b2t3⟹λd(y2−x3)=(−32tb,b).(26) d(y^2-x^3)=(-3t^4,2t^3),\qquad \lambda=\frac{b}{2t^3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda d(y^2-x^3)=\left(-\frac32tb,b\right). \qquad\text{(26)}

For fixed nonzero bb, this tends to (0,b)(0,b), using unbounded coefficients. Bounded coefficients instead make both entries tend to zero. Formula (14) permits all finite coefficients at each nonzero tt, followed by closure; it imposes no uniform coefficient bound along a sequence.

(ΣS)0={a=0}∪{b=0}.(27) (\Sigma_S)_0=\{a=0\}\cup\{b=0\}. \qquad\text{(27)}

One finite base with infinitely many components

Difficulty: Introductory.

Let Z={m:m∈ℤ}⊂ℂZ=\{m:m\in\mathbb Z\}\subset\mathbb C and Λ=⋃m∈ℤTm*ℂ\Lambda=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb Z}T_m^*\mathbb C. Exhibit the rank-group cover and check local finiteness.

Solution. The integers are the zero set of the entire function sin⁡(πz)\sin(\pi z); they have no finite accumulation. Generic bases for a zero section and a fibre

Difficulty: Intermediate.

Take Λ={ξ=0}∪{z=0}⊂T*ℂ\Lambda=\{\xi=0\}\cup\{z=0\}\subset T^*\mathbb C. Give the cover (17). For Y=ℂY=\mathbb C and for Y={0}Y=\{0\}, find a valid Y0Y_0 and verify (22). Explain what happens if Λ\Lambda is replaced by all of T*ℂT^*\mathbb C.

Solution. The zero section has projection rank 1 and base X1=ℂX_1=\mathbb C; the vertical fibre has rank zero and base X0={0}X_0=\{0\}. Their base conormals give exactly the stated union. For Y=ℂY=\mathbb C, take Y0=ℂ\{0}Y_0=\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}. Over it, only the zero covector occurs, which annihilates its tangent. Including the origin would fail, since its nonzero vertical covectors do not annihilate the tangent of ℂ\mathbb C. For the zero-dimensional base Y={0}Y=\{0\}, take all of it: its tangent is zero, and its conormal is the full fibre.

If Λ=T*ℂ\Lambda=T^*\mathbb C, every nonempty open smooth base has arbitrary nonzero covectors above it. Its tangent is one-dimensional, so (22) fails on every possible open dense Y0⊂ℂY_0\subset\mathbb C. Nor can finitely many closed analytic base conormals cover it. A closed analytic subset of the connected curve ℂ\mathbb C is either all of ℂ\mathbb C, with zero-section conormal, or discrete, with full conormal fibres only over that discrete set. Finitely many discrete bases still leave some base point outside them, where only zero covectors have been covered. The full cotangent bundle is analytic and complex-conic but fails isotropy. It verifies the need for the hypothesis in both valid theorems.

Why projected components need conicity

Difficulty: Advanced.

Consider the discrete analytic set A={(1/m,m):m≥1}⊂T*ℂA=\{(1/m,m):m\ge1\}\subset T^*\mathbb C. Show that it is closed analytic and that its projected component family is not locally finite near zero. Identify the failure of the zero-section argument.

Solution. Its fibre coordinates tend to infinity, so every compact subset of T*ℂT^*\mathbb C contains only finitely many of these points. The set is closed and locally a finite union of isolated analytic points, hence complex analytic. Each point is an irreducible component. Their projected bases are {1/m}\{1/m\}, which accumulate at zero, so are not locally finite there; their union is not closed in ℂ\mathbb C. The set is not fibre-conic: scaling (1/m,m)(1/m,m) does not stay in its singleton component. In particular (1/m,0)(1/m,0) does not belong to it. Equation (15) therefore fails, and no neighborhood of (0,0)(0,0) detects those escaping components. Zero-dimensional regular tangent spaces have vanishing canonical form, so this example also shows that isotropy without conicity does not supply the projected local-finiteness argument.

What remains a prerequisite

The normal-cone, ideal, flow, tangent-graph, finite-cover and generic-base arguments use the linked programme proofs of local analytic algebra, proper holomorphic images, dimension and subanalytic geometry. The reduced-fibre specialization needed here is proved in CS1–CS4. The stronger Thom statement about every limiting sequence is not required by that proof. A complex microlocal stratification refining an analytic covering, the sheaf constructibility equivalences, and the nonproper curve-base direct-image theorem require their own arguments. The defining-equation and missing-isotropy counterexamples retain the precise limits of the constructions; full transitive proof closure is not claimed.
