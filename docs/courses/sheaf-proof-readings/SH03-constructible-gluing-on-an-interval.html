Sheaf proof readings

Constructible gluing on an interval

A constructible sheaf can change at a small set of points while remaining locally constant elsewhere. The change is not described by a list of stalks alone. We must also know how a section near a singular point restricts to each neighboring region. On an interval with one distinguished point, this produces a diagram of three modules. We will construct the sheaf from that diagram, calculate its cohomology and costalk, and extend the calculation to bounded complexes.

We assume the definition of a sheaf, exactness detected at stalks, the localization triangle for an open subset and its closed complement, and derived adjunction for extension by zero. We also use the elementary sheaf-cohomology calculation for a constant sheaf on a contractible interval. A prerequisite is Sheaves of modules and their derived categories; extension-by-zero adjunction is also required. The source account below identifies the classical operations and the exact scope of their use.

Restriction data at one point

Let I=(−1,1)I=(-1,1), I−=(−1,0)I_-=(-1,0), and I+=(0,1)I_+=(0,1). A sheaf of kk-modules is weakly constructible for this decomposition if its restrictions to I−I_- and I+I_+ are locally constant. They are constant because each interval is simply connected. This statement uses no finiteness condition on the modules and no global-dimension condition on the ring kk.

Choose trivializations of the two restrictions. Denote their values by LL and RR, and put V=F0V=F_0. A germ at zero has a representative on a sufficiently small interval. Its restrictions to the two half intervals give homomorphisms

L←r−V→r+R.(1) L\xleftarrow{r_-}V\xrightarrow{r_+}R. \qquad\text{(1)}

The arrows point away from the singular point. This is the direction in which a germ at zero determines nearby values. They need not be injective or surjective.

Theorem 1. Weakly constructible sheaves on II are equivalent to diagrams (1). Morphisms are triples of module homomorphisms commuting with both arrows. Kernels, cokernels and exact sequences are calculated at the three modules.

Proof. We first construct a sheaf EE from a diagram. On an open subset U⊂IU\subset I, a section consists of a locally constant LL-valued function on U∩I−U\cap I_-, a locally constant RR-valued function on U∩I+U\cap I_+, and, if 0∈U0\in U, an element v∈Vv\in V. Near zero the two functions must have the constant values r−(v)r_-(v) and r+(v)r_+(v). There is no condition on connected components of UU away from zero.

Restriction forgets parts of the two functions and, when necessary, the value at zero. Compatible local functions glue uniquely. If an open covering contains zero, at least one member contains a neighborhood of it; the values vv agree on overlaps containing zero. The near-zero condition therefore glues too. This proves the sheaf axiom.

At a point of I−I_- or I+I_+, the stalk is LL or RR. At zero, a section on a sufficiently small connected interval is uniquely determined by vv, because each half interval is connected and its value is prescribed. Thus E0=VE_0=V, and its two restriction maps are the chosen arrows.

Conversely, construct (1) from a sheaf FF. Independence of the representative of a germ follows by shrinking its neighborhood; a locally constant function on the connected half interval has one value. A section of FF determines the two locally constant functions and its germ at zero. This gives a natural sheaf morphism from FF to the constructed EE. It is an isomorphism on each of the three types of stalk, hence an isomorphism of sheaves. The two constructions also identify morphisms. Finally, sheaf exactness is stalkwise, so exactness is componentwise in (1). ▫\square

For example, L=R=kL=R=k and V=0V=0 gives a sheaf with nonzero values on both sides but no germ at zero. Taking V=kV=k and both arrows the identity gives the constant sheaf on all of II. These sheaves have the same values away from zero and different gluing.

Cohomology of the whole interval

For a diagram (1), every section on II is determined by its value in VV, so Γ(I;F)=V\Gamma(I;F)=V. The corresponding derived assertion needs a proof in the category of all sheaves, rather than only exactness within the diagram category.

Let j±:I±↪Ij_\pm:I_\pm\hookrightarrow I and i:{0}↪Ii:\{0\}\hookrightarrow I. The exact sequence separating the closed point from the two open pieces is

0⟶j−!LI−⊕j+!RI+⟶F⟶i*V⟶0.(2) 0\longrightarrow j_{-!}L_{I_-}\oplus j_{+!}R_{I_+} \longrightarrow F\longrightarrow i_*V\longrightarrow0. \qquad\text{(2)}

Exactness follows from stalks: the first map is the identity away from zero and has zero stalk at zero; the last map is the identity at zero and zero elsewhere.

Proposition 2. For every module diagram (1),

RΓ(I;F)≃V(3) R\Gamma(I;F)\simeq V \qquad\text{(3)}

with VV in degree zero.

Proof. The constant sheaf with value LL on the closed half interval (−1,0](-1,0], pushed into II, fits into

0⟶j−!LI−⟶L(−1,0]⟶i*L⟶0.(4) 0\longrightarrow j_{-!}L_{I_-} \longrightarrow L_{(-1,0]} \longrightarrow i_*L\longrightarrow0. \qquad\text{(4)}

The closed half interval is contractible, and its constant-sheaf cohomology is LL in degree zero. The point has the same cohomology. The map on degree-zero cohomology in (4) is the identity of LL. The long exact cohomology sequence gives RΓ(I;j−!LI−)=0R\Gamma(I;j_{-!}L_{I_-})=0. The right half interval gives the same statement for j+!RI+j_{+!}R_{I_+}. Apply derived global sections to (2). Both open-piece terms vanish, and RΓ(I;i*V)=VR\Gamma(I;i_*V)=V, proving (3). ▫\square

The constant-sheaf interval calculation used here holds for arbitrary coefficient modules. The programme proof is compact-interval acyclicity and its constant-module argument, followed by homotopy invariance through a proper interval. Each open, closed or half-open interval used here contracts inside itself to a point. Evaluation at that point and the constant-section map have one composite equal to the identity by definition; homotopy invariance identifies the other composite with the identity induced by the interval contraction. This gives the ordinary cohomology identification, naturally in the module. Finite truncation triangles give the same assertion for a bounded coefficient complex. No compact-support cohomology of an open half interval has been substituted for ordinary cohomology on II.

Bounded complexes of diagrams

We now assume that kk is a commutative ring of finite global dimension gg. Let 𝒜\mathcal A be the abelian category of diagrams (1). Let D𝒮b(kI)D^b_{\mathcal S}(k_I) denote bounded complexes of sheaves whose cohomology sheaves are weakly constructible for the three pieces.

Theorem 3. The exact sheaf construction in Theorem 1 induces an equivalence

Db(𝒜)→∼D𝒮b(kI).(5) D^b(\mathcal A)\xrightarrow{\sim}D^b_{\mathcal S}(k_I). \qquad\text{(5)}

Under this equivalence a bounded complex can be represented by three complexes and two cochain maps

L•←r−V•→r+R•.(6) L^\bullet\xleftarrow{r_-}V^\bullet\xrightarrow{r_+}R^\bullet. \qquad\text{(6)}

Proof. We give the homological-algebra argument because passing from sheaves to complexes must preserve extension data.

For a module MM, form the following diagrams:

P0(M)=(M←idM→idM),P−(M)=(M←0→0),P+(M)=(0←0→M). \begin{aligned} P_0(M)&=(M\xleftarrow{\mathrm{id}}M\xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}}M),\\ P_-(M)&=(M\leftarrow0\to0),\\ P_+(M)&=(0\leftarrow0\to M). \end{aligned}

Here the notation always lists left value, middle value, right value. A map from P0(M)P_0(M) to (1) is determined by a map M→VM\to V; maps from P−(M)P_-(M) and P+(M)P_+(M) are determined by maps to LL and RR. Consequently these diagrams are projective when MM is projective, and 𝒜\mathcal A has enough projectives.

For E=(L←V→R)E=(L\leftarrow V\to R) there is a functorial exact sequence

0→P−(V)⊕P+(V)→P0(V)⊕P−(L)⊕P+(R)→E→0.(7) 0\to P_-(V)\oplus P_+(V) \to P_0(V)\oplus P_-(L)\oplus P_+(R) \to E\to0. \qquad\text{(7)}

The last map is the identity at the middle module. On the left and right it sends (v,l)(v,l) to r−(v)+lr_-(v)+l, and (v,r)(v,r) to r+(v)+rr_+(v)+r. The first map on the left sends vv to (v,−r−(v))(v,-r_-(v)), and on the right sends vv to (v,−r+(v))(v,-r_+(v)). These formulas prove exactness at every module.

Each of V,L,RV,L,R has a projective resolution of length at most gg. The functors P0,P−,P+P_0,P_-,P_+ are exact and carry projective modules to projective diagrams. Resolving the terms of (7), and taking the mapping cone of a lifted resolution map, gives a projective diagram resolution of EE of length at most g+1g+1.

Next, these projective diagrams have no higher sheaf Ext into a weakly constructible sheaf GG. The sheaf associated to P0(M)P_0(M) is the constant sheaf MIM_I. Derived adjunction identifies its derived Hom into GG with

RHom⁡k(M,RΓ(I;G)). R\operatorname{Hom}_k(M,R\Gamma(I;G)).

By Proposition 2, the second argument is G0G_0 in degree zero. If MM is projective, this has no higher cohomology. The sheaves associated to P−(M)P_-(M) and P+(M)P_+(M) are j−!MI−j_{-!}M_{I_-} and j+!MI+j_{+!}M_{I_+}. Extension-by-zero adjunction gives instead RHom⁡k(M,RΓ(I±;G|I±))R\operatorname{Hom}_k(M,R\Gamma(I_\pm;G|_{I_\pm})). The restriction is constant on a contractible interval, so these complexes too have no higher cohomology for projective MM.

A finite projective diagram resolution can therefore calculate both diagram Ext and sheaf Ext into GG. In degree zero the Hom groups agree by Theorem 1; the resolution maps agree as well. Dimension shifting yields equality of Ext groups in every degree. The induced functor on bounded derived categories is fully faithful: apply these Ext comparisons successively to the truncation triangles of the two bounded complexes.

For essential surjectivity, take a bounded sheaf complex with constructible cohomology. If it has one nonzero cohomology sheaf, it is a shift of a sheaf in Theorem 1. Induct on the number of possibly nonzero cohomology degrees. A truncation triangle expresses the complex as an extension of its lower truncation by its highest cohomology sheaf with a shift. By the induction hypothesis these two objects come from Db(𝒜)D^b(\mathcal A); by full faithfulness the connecting morphism comes from that category too. Its cone produces a preimage of the original complex. This proves (5). Finally, a bounded complex in the diagram category is exactly the data (6). ▫\square

This proof concerns the one-point interval decomposition. It does not assert the corresponding theorem for an arbitrary stratification or triangulation.

For a cochain map u:A•→B•u:A^\bullet\to B^\bullet, our cone has Cone⁡(u)n=Bn⊕An+1\operatorname{Cone}(u)^n=B^n\oplus A^{n+1} and differential d(b,a)=(dBb+u(a),−dAa)d(b,a)=(d_Bb+u(a),-d_Aa). We use the connecting-arrow convention of Sheaves of modules and their derived categories. The fibre objects calculated below are cones shifted by [−1][-1]; their cohomology degrees are independent of negating a triangle’s connecting arrow.

Stalks and costalks

For the complex (6), the stalk at zero is V•V^\bullet. The costalk Ri!FRi^!F measures cohomology supported at zero. It is sensitive to the restriction maps.

Put j:I\{0}↪Ij:I\setminus\{0\}\hookrightarrow I. The localization triangle is

i*Ri!F⟶F⟶Rj*j−1F⟶i*Ri!F[1].(8) i_*Ri^!F\longrightarrow F\longrightarrow Rj_*j^{-1}F\longrightarrow i_*Ri^!F[1]. \qquad\text{(8)}

At zero the third term is the cohomology of two small punctured half intervals. Each is contractible, and the restrictions in (6) are constant complexes there. Hence its stalk is L•⊕R•L^\bullet\oplus R^\bullet, and the middle map in (8) is (r−,r+)(r_-,r_+).

Proposition 4. There is a natural isomorphism

Ri!F≃Cone⁡(V•→(r−,r+)L•⊕R•)[−1].(9) Ri^!F\simeq \operatorname{Cone}\bigl(V^\bullet\xrightarrow{(r_-,r_+)}L^\bullet\oplus R^\bullet\bigr)[-1]. \qquad\text{(9)}

For a sheaf in degree zero this gives

H0(Ri!F)=ker⁡(V→L⊕R),H1(Ri!F)=coker⁡(V→L⊕R),(10) \begin{aligned} H^0(Ri^!F)&=\ker(V\to L\oplus R),\\ H^1(Ri^!F)&=\operatorname{coker}(V\to L\oplus R), \end{aligned} \qquad\text{(10)}

and all other cohomology groups vanish.

Proof. Taking a stalk is exact and takes (8) to a distinguished triangle Ri!F→V•→L•⊕R•→Ri!F[1]Ri^!F\to V^\bullet\to L^\bullet\oplus R^\bullet\to Ri^!F[1]. The fibre of the middle map is its cone shifted by −1-1, giving (9). For degree-zero modules, the long exact sequence gives (10). ▫\square

In particular, the constant sheaf has diagonal restriction k→k⊕kk\to k\oplus k. Its costalk has kk in degree one, or k[−1]k[-1]. This agrees with the one-dimensional local orientation calculation after choosing an orientation of the interval. The skyscraper i*ki_*k has zero neighboring modules and costalk kk in degree zero. A stalk and a costalk can therefore occupy different degrees.

Perfect values and finite coefficients

A complex of kk-modules is perfect if it is quasi-isomorphic to a bounded complex of finitely generated projective modules. In this lesson a bounded weakly constructible complex is constructible if each stalk is perfect. For (6), this means precisely that V•,L•,R•V^\bullet,L^\bullet,R^\bullet are perfect.

This definition is valid without assuming that kk is Noetherian. Perfect complexes are closed under finite direct sums, shifts and cones. To see the cone statement, represent two perfect complexes by bounded complexes of finitely generated projectives. A derived morphism between them is represented by a cochain map because its source is a bounded projective complex. Its ordinary mapping cone is another bounded complex of finitely generated projectives. Formula (9) therefore shows that a constructible complex in this model also has perfect costalk at zero.

If kk is Noetherian as well as of finite global dimension, a bounded complex is perfect exactly when its cohomology modules are finitely generated. One direction follows because kernels and cokernels of maps between finitely generated modules are finitely generated. For the other, resolve a finitely generated cohomology module by finite free modules; Noetherianity keeps the successive kernels finitely generated, and finite global dimension makes a sufficiently high kernel projective. This gives a finite resolution by finitely generated projectives. Use the finitely many truncation triangles and closure under cones for a bounded complex.

Over a field, perfect simply means bounded with finite-dimensional cohomology. The diagram with V=⨁n≥0kV=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}k, L=R=0L=R=0 is weakly constructible but not constructible: its stalk at zero is infinite dimensional. The geometry of the decomposition alone supplies no finiteness of coefficients.

One diagram from sections to a closed-layer correction

Use this example throughout the diagram-to-heart route. Over ℤ\mathbb Z, take

L=ℤ,V=ℤ2,R=ℤ,r−(a,b)=2a+4b,r+(a,b)=a+2b.(B1) L=\mathbb Z,\qquad V=\mathbb Z^2,\qquad R=\mathbb Z,\qquad r_-(a,b)=2a+4b,\qquad r_+(a,b)=a+2b. \qquad\text{(B1)}

Write q(a,b)=a+2bq(a,b)=a+2b, u=(−2,1)u=(-2,1), and let EE be its sheaf. All three modules are finite free, but neither their ranks nor their perfection determines its local tests. The combined attachment is ρ=(2q,q):V→L⊕R\rho=(2q,q):V\to L\oplus R.

Proposition 2 gives RΓ(I;E)=ℤ2R\Gamma(I;E)=\mathbb Z^2 in degree zero. Under this identification the maps restricting a global section to either half interval are exactly 2q2q and qq. At zero, ordinary restriction also gives VV. The costalk, by Proposition 4, has the model

K=[ℤ2→(2q,q)ℤ2]in degrees 0,1.(B2) K=[\,\mathbb Z^2\xrightarrow{\ (2q,q)\ }\mathbb Z^2\,] \quad\text{in degrees }0,1. \qquad\text{(B2)}

Here and in the two directional models we identify the shifted cone with the displayed positive differential by negating its degree-one target. We make the same change for all three cones, so their projection maps commute. Its degree-zero cohomology is ℤu\mathbb Zu. The functional ℓ(x,y)=x−2y\ell(x,y)=x-2y induces an isomorphism coker⁡ρ→ℤ\operatorname{coker}\rho\to\mathbb Z: it kills (2,1)(2,1), is onto, and its kernel is precisely ℤ(2,1)\mathbb Z(2,1). Thus H1K=ℤH^1K=\mathbb Z.

The directional tests are

J+=[V→2qℤ],J−=[V→qℤ].(B3) J_+=[\,V\xrightarrow{\,2q\,}\mathbb Z\,],\qquad J_-=[\,V\xrightarrow{\,q\,}\mathbb Z\,]. \qquad\text{(B3)}

Both have H0=ℤuH^0=\mathbb Zu; their H1H^1 are respectively ℤ/2\mathbb Z/2 and zero. In particular both nonzero covector directions occur, even though the right attachment is onto. Absence requires a quasi-isomorphism, so its nonzero kernel matters too.

These models give more than three independent cohomology calculations. Projection to the left coordinate induces K→J+K\to J_+, with identity in degree zero and (x,y)↦x(x,y)\mapsto x in degree one. On H0H^0 it is the identity of ℤu\mathbb Zu. On H1H^1 it is reduction modulo two, because x≡ℓ(x,y)(mod⁡2)x\equiv\ell(x,y)\pmod2. Its kernel complex is R[−1]R[-1], included by y↦(0,y)y\mapsto(0,y). Consequently the cohomology sequence contains the actual maps

ℤ=R→−2ℤ=H1K→mod⁡2ℤ/2=H1J+⟶0.(B4) \mathbb Z=R\xrightarrow{\ -2\ }\mathbb Z=H^1K \xrightarrow{\ \bmod2\ }\mathbb Z/2=H^1J_+\longrightarrow0. \qquad\text{(B4)}

The sign comes from ℓ(0,y)=−2y\ell(0,y)=-2y. Projection to the right coordinate gives K→J−K\to J_-, again the identity on H0H^0, and zero on H1H^1. Keeping the attachment maps accounts for both the torsion and this comparison.

The ordinary smart lower cut of (B2) at zero is the subgroup ℤu\mathbb Zu in degree zero, with its inclusion into VV. Its upper cut starts with coker⁡ρ=ℤ\operatorname{coker}\rho=\mathbb Z in degree one. Deleting the target would leave all of VV, rather than its kernel.

Since EE is in ordinary degree zero there, its lower open cut is zero. Evaluation does not supply a natural splitting

The separately licensed pointwise abelian and natural-splitting treatment explains the distinction between evaluating a diagram and splitting it naturally. Its human source is Tom Leinster, Basic Category Theory, arXiv version 2, 26 August 2025; that component and its AI additions retain CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. This lesson applies its universal objects to the interval; it does not reproduce that adapted exposition or change its licence.

For a direct interval example, the usual sheaf sequence 0⟶j!ℤI\{0}⟶ℤI⟶i*ℤ⟶0(B5) 0\longrightarrow j_!\mathbb Z_{I\setminus\{0\}} \longrightarrow\mathbb Z_I\longrightarrow i_*\mathbb Z \longrightarrow0 \qquad\text{(B5)} is split after evaluating at every stratum. At zero its quotient is the identity of ℤ\mathbb Z; on an open half the quotient is zero. A section as a diagram would have to be the identity at zero and zero on both halves. Naturality along either attachment would say 1=01=0, because the middle constant sheaf has identity attachments. No such section exists. Stalkwise exactness and stalkwise splitness have different conclusions.

The hidden-differential exercise is a second check: the complex [k→1k][k\xrightarrow{1}k] is zero in the derived category even though its two terms are nonzero. Neither a termwise dimension list nor a family of unrelated splittings removes the need to retain all maps.

Exercises with solutions

Three boundary conditions

Difficulty: Introductory.

Let k≠0k\neq0. Give the diagrams and costalks for the constant sheaf kIk_I, the extension by zero j+!kI+j_{+!}k_{I_+}, and the ordinary direct image j+*kI+j_{+*}k_{I_+}.

Solution. The constant sheaf has V=L=R=kV=L=R=k and both arrows the identity. Its costalk is the fibre of the diagonal map, hence k[−1]k[-1]. Extension by zero has V=L=0V=L=0 and R=kR=k; its costalk is k[−1]k[-1] by (9), even though its stalk at zero vanishes. Ordinary direct image has L=0L=0, V=R=kV=R=k, and r+=idr_+=\mathrm{id}; its costalk is zero. On a small interval, sections of the latter sheaf are determined by the constant value on its positive half, which explains its nonzero stalk at zero. The higher direct-image stalks vanish because the punctured positive half interval is contractible.

Torsion from gluing

Difficulty: Intermediate.

Take k=ℤk=\mathbb Z, V=L=R=ℤV=L=R=\mathbb Z, and maps r−(v)=2vr_-(v)=2v, r+(v)=6vr_+(v)=6v. Calculate the stalk, global cohomology and costalk of the resulting sheaf.

Solution. The stalk at zero and the global cohomology are ℤ\mathbb Z in degree zero, by construction and Proposition 2. The map to the two neighboring values is v↦(2v,6v)v\mapsto(2v,6v), which is injective. Its cokernel is ℤ⊕ℤ/2\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/2: replace the second coordinate by the second minus three times the first, so the map becomes v↦(2v,0)v\mapsto(2v,0). Thus H0(Ri!F)=0H^0(Ri^!F)=0 and H1(Ri!F)=ℤ⊕ℤ/2H^1(Ri^!F)=\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/2. Every stalk module is finite free, while the costalk detects torsion created by the restriction maps.

A hidden differential

Difficulty: Intermediate.

Work over a field. Let L•=R•=0L^\bullet=R^\bullet=0, and let V•=[k→idk]V^\bullet=[k\xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}}k] in degrees zero and one. Compare this complex with the skyscraper complex having V=kV=k in degree zero.

Solution. The first V•V^\bullet is acyclic, so all stalks of its associated sheaf complex are acyclic. That sheaf complex is zero in the derived category, as is its costalk. The second has nonzero degree-zero stalk and costalk. The list of term dimensions is not a substitute for cohomology or for the differentials in a derived diagram.

Euler characteristic at the singular point

Difficulty: Intermediate.

Let kk be a field and assume the three complexes in (6) are perfect. Define χ(C)=∑j(−1)jdim⁡kHj(C)\chi(C)=\sum_j(-1)^j\dim_kH^j(C). Prove

χ(Ri!F)=χ(V•)−χ(L•)−χ(R•). \chi(Ri^!F)=\chi(V^\bullet)-\chi(L^\bullet)-\chi(R^\bullet).

Evaluate it for the constant sheaf and the skyscraper.

Solution. A distinguished triangle of bounded finite-dimensional complexes has additive Euler characteristic, by its finite long exact cohomology sequence. Apply this to the stalk triangle of (8). It gives the stated difference. For the constant sheaf the result is 1−1−1=−11-1-1=-1, agreeing with k[−1]k[-1]. For the skyscraper it is 1−0−0=11-0-0=1, agreeing with a degree-zero costalk. This identity describes the costalk in this decomposition; it has not defined a characteristic-cycle sign convention.
