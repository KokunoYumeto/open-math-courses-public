Intrinsic orientation and regularity entering the characteristic set

The favorable direction of propagation is a property of a complex characteristic curve. It must survive multiplication of the symbol and a change of canonical coordinates. A compact piece on which the symbol is locally real does not, by itself, distinguish the two directions: the choice comes from an endpoint at which the characteristic curve can reach a nonzero symbol value.

We prove the intrinsic orientation, propagation from a negative noncharacteristic point, and propagation from a regular starting point on an oriented characteristic interval. The antecedents are Hörmander IV, Theorem 26.6.2, Definition 26.6.3 and Theorem 26.6.4, printed 120–121 in the admitted 2009 edition. All operator and geometry steps used here have exact programme proofs. The unrestricted complex-cutoff question in the neighboring Proposition 26.6.1′ remains a separate obligation; these three invariant results use the already proved ordinary estimate.

Use the characteristic geometry, complex homogeneous tubes, graph operators and Egorov, and ordinary propagation. The proof map pins their actual current sources and locators. External human foundations remain explicit; internal export P514 closure is not claimed.

G0. Carry a compact interval to an accessible endpoint. Every compact one-dimensional interval in N11N_{11}N11​ can be included in a compact one-dimensional interval maximal at at least one endpoint and disjoint from SSS. To verify the endpoint assertion, start at any one point and choose its defining semibicharacteristic to a noncharacteristic point. The connected component of its characteristic times containing that point is a closed interval, possibly with one endpoint at the starting time. At the end toward the noncharacteristic point it has a finite endpoint inside the regular domain. A characteristic continuation there would have to coincide with the original real orbit by flow uniqueness, contradicting maximality of that component. CPG:C0 supplies this argument also when the zero component is a singleton.

For a nontrivial given characteristic interval, the common Hamilton line identifies it locally with the characteristic portion of this orbit. Uniqueness glues their overlapping intervals. Continuing from their common point toward the accessible endpoint and including the given compact portion therefore gives the required larger interval. All these statements concern the regular domain. An endpoint on its boundary, or a point where dp=0dp=0dp=0, is not silently included.
