Regularizing long-range coefficients

Written by GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI). Self-checked by the writing AI. Original exposition: CC0.

Working question: How much smoothing can a slowly varying coefficient tolerate? At distance RRR, an averaging radius RρR^\rhoRρ, with 0<ρ<10<\rho<10<ρ<1, grows but has relative size Rρ−1→0R^{\rho-1}\to0Rρ−1→0. Thus a derivative can be improved without averaging across a macroscopic part of the escaping ray. Moment cancellation decides the size of the remainder; a positive kernel alone cannot cancel every even moment.

A slowly decaying coefficient changes a classical trajectory over a long time. Constructing that trajectory requires differentiating the coefficient repeatedly, even if the original equation assumes only finitely many derivatives. The useful remedy is to separate a smooth long-range part from an integrable short-range remainder. The smoothing scale must grow with distance but remain small compared with that distance.

The derivative rules and Taylor remainder are proved in Section 1, and Section 4 gives the local inverse argument. We use the earlier finite linear algebra and compact scalar calculus, smooth cutoffs, and convolution differentiation. Endpoint spaces and flat energy shells explains the spatial scale of a short-range remainder. Hörmander [HW], Lemma 3.3, pp. 77–78, gives the dyadic regularization method; Yafaev [Y], Section 3, discusses the long-range scattering setting. We construct the signed moment kernel, prove the vanishing normalization and track the derivative bounds below.

1. A kernel that cancels Taylor terms

A positive averaging kernel cannot cancel all even moments. We instead use a smooth signed kernel. This causes no difficulty: only bounded integral norms and moment identities will enter the proof.

5. Why the remainder is short range

The elementary coefficient condition ∣fS(x)∣≤C⟨x⟩−1−δ|f_S(x)|\leq C\langle x\rangle^{-1-\delta}∣fS​(x)∣≤C⟨x⟩−1−δ, δ=ε−b>0\delta=\varepsilon-b>0δ=ε−b>0, has a direct dynamical consequence. Along a straight ray x=tvx=tvx=tv, v≠0v\ne0v=0, its absolute value is integrable for t≥1t\geq1t≥1:

∫1∞∣fS(tv)∣ dt≤C∫1∞⟨tv⟩−1−δ dt<∞. \int_1^\infty|f_S(tv)|\,dt \leq C\int_1^\infty\langle tv\rangle^{-1-\delta}\,dt<\infty. ∫1∞​∣fS​(tv)∣dt≤C∫1∞​⟨tv⟩−1−δdt<∞.

The original coefficient need not have that property. For example ⟨tv⟩−ε\langle tv\rangle^{-\varepsilon}⟨tv⟩−ε, 0<ε<10<\varepsilon<10<ε<1, has an integral growing like t1−εt^{1-\varepsilon}t1−ε. This is why the smooth part contributes to the modifier in a long-range wave operator while the remainder is estimated as an integrable error. Constructing such a modifier requires additional Hamiltonian and operator estimates; the coefficient decomposition alone is not an existence theorem for wave operators.
