Root data, Weyl chambers and the Bruhat decomposition

Written by GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI) in Codex, Ultra setting, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI, GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI). Public domain (CC0).

A root records how a torus moves a one-dimensional subgroup. Its coroot records the diagonal direction in the associated rank-one group. Keeping both, together with the integral character and cocharacter lattices, distinguishes groups that have the same root system. We first assemble these data, then use cocharacters to describe Borel subgroups and the Bruhat cells. The last part explains which of these constructions survive over an arbitrary base.

The preceding lesson supplies root groups, their canonical line-bundle parametrizations, coroots, and the limit subgroups $U_G(\lambda)$ and $P_G(\lambda)$. For the field arguments we also use the geometric Borel quotient and the simply transitive action of $N_G(T)/T$ on the Borels containing $T$, proved in the first two lessons. We prove the identification of this normalizer quotient with the reflection group here; Theorem 4.2 then proves arbitrary-base finiteness using this field calculation.

1. The integral data and their reflections

A root datum consists of finite free lattices $X$ and $X^\vee$, a perfect pairing

$$ \langle\ ,\ \rangle:X\times X^\vee\longrightarrow\mathbf Z, $$

finite subsets $\Phi\subset X\setminus\{0\}$ and $\Phi^\vee\subset X^\vee\setminus\{0\}$, and a bijection $\alpha\mapsto\alpha^\vee$ between them. Its axioms are

$$ \langle\alpha,\alpha^\vee\rangle=2, \qquad s_\alpha(\Phi)=\Phi, \qquad s_\alpha^\vee(\Phi^\vee)=\Phi^\vee, $$

where

$$ s_\alpha(x)=x-\langle x,\alpha^\vee\rangle\alpha, \qquad s_\alpha^\vee(y)=y-\langle\alpha,y\rangle\alpha^\vee. \tag{1.1} $$

We require the bijection to respect these reflections. The reflections square to the identity, are dual under the pairing, and send $\alpha$ and $\alpha^\vee$ to their negatives. These assertions follow by substituting $\langle\alpha,\alpha^\vee\rangle=2$ in (1.1). A datum is reduced if $\Phi\cap\mathbf Q\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}$ for every root. The empty root set is allowed: it describes a torus. Roots need not span $X_\mathbf Q$.

The Weyl group $W(\mathcal R)$ is the subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(X)$ generated by the $s_\alpha$. Here is a proof of its finiteness which also isolates the central directions. Let $V=\mathbf Q\Phi$. The image of $W(\mathcal R)$ on $V$ is finite, since it permutes a finite spanning set. Average a positive definite inner product on $V_\mathbf R$ over this image. Each $s_\alpha|_V$ is then an orthogonal reflection, and hence

$$ \langle v,\alpha^\vee\rangle =\frac{2(v,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\quad(v\in V). \tag{1.2} $$

Consequently these functionals span $V^*$. Set

$$ Z_\mathbf Q=\{x\in X_\mathbf Q:\langle x,\alpha^\vee\rangle=0 \text{ for every }\alpha\}. $$

The restriction map $X_\mathbf Q\to V^*$ is surjective and is an isomorphism on $V$, by (1.2). Thus $X_\mathbf Q=V\oplus Z_\mathbf Q$. Every reflection fixes $Z_\mathbf Q$ pointwise. The action on $V$ is therefore faithful on the entire Weyl group, proving finiteness. Equation (1.2) also shows that different roots have different coroots, and that proportional coroots come from proportional roots.

The dual datum interchanges $X$ with $X^\vee$ and $\Phi$ with $\Phi^\vee$. Its axioms are the same identities read in the other direction. Reducedness is preserved, by (1.2). A datum is semisimple if $\mathbf Q\Phi=X_\mathbf Q$; in that case

$$ \mathbf Z\Phi\subset X\subset \operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf Z\Phi^\vee,\mathbf Z) $$

inside $X_\mathbf Q$. The left and right lattices are the root and weight lattices. The intermediate lattice is information that a picture of the roots alone cannot retain.

2. The root datum of a reductive group

Let $G$ be split connected reductive over a field $k$, and let $T$ be a split maximal torus. Take

$$ X=X^*(T),\qquad X^\vee=X_*(T),\qquad \mathfrak g=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak g_\alpha. $$

The pairing is composition of a character and a cocharacter. The preceding lesson proves that every root space is a line, that there are only the two roots $\pm\alpha$ on its rational line, and that the rank-one map $\varphi_\alpha:\operatorname{SL}_2\to G$ provides an intrinsic coroot with pairing two.

Choose a nonzero vector in $\mathfrak g_\alpha$ and its linked dual in $\mathfrak g_{-\alpha}$. Write the corresponding root parametrizations as $x_\alpha$ and $x_{-\alpha}$. The element

$$ n_\alpha=x_\alpha(1)x_{-\alpha}(-1)x_\alpha(1) \tag{2.1} $$

is the image of $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$. It normalizes $T$. On the coroot torus it acts by inversion, and on the codimension-one torus killed by $\alpha$ it acts trivially. These statements follow in the rank-one group and remain valid after its central quotient. On the rational character lattice they give precisely the reflection (1.1), since the two rational torus directions form an isogeny decomposition. Equality on the rational lattice is equality on the integral lattice, which is torsion-free. Thus

$$ n_\alpha t n_\alpha^{-1} =t\,\alpha^\vee(\alpha(t)^{-1}). \tag{2.2} $$

Conjugation by any normalizer element permutes the root spaces and their root groups. The uniqueness of the rank-one coroot construction then permutes the coroots in the same way. Applied to (2.1), this proves both reflection axioms and their compatibility.

Theorem 2.1. $(X^*(T),\Phi,X_*(T),\Phi^\vee)$ is a reduced root datum. Its formation commutes with extension of the field.

Proof. Write \(T\simeq\mathbf G_m^r\). The coefficient proof for diagonalizable groups in AG-GS, Theorem 2.2, identifies its characters with integer exponent vectors and its cocharacters with the dual vectors. Thus \(X=X^*(T)\) and \(X^\vee=X_*(T)\) are finite free of rank \(r\), and their composition pairing is perfect. The finite-dimensional adjoint representation has finitely many weights by the full grading proof in RG01. The root-line reduction following RG03 Proposition 2.3 proves that every nonzero weight space is a line and that \(\Phi\cap\mathbf Q\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\}\). RG03 Theorem 7.1 supplies the intrinsic coroot \(\beta=\alpha^\vee\) and the identity \[ \alpha\circ\beta(z)=z^2. \] In particular \(\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=2\) as an integer, in every characteristic, and \(\beta\ne0\). If \(\Phi\) is empty, the reflection axioms are vacuous; the scalar-extension argument below still applies.

We make the torus decomposition in the reflection calculation precise. Write \(\alpha=\nu\alpha_0\), where \(\nu>0\) and \(\alpha_0\) is a primitive exponent vector. Since \[ \nu\langle\alpha_0,\beta\rangle=2, \qquad m:=\langle\alpha_0,\beta\rangle=2/\nu, \] both \(\nu\) and \(m\) are either one or two. Primitive means that the integer coordinates of \(\alpha_0\) have greatest common divisor one. The integer Euclidean algorithm gives integers \(b_i\) with \(\sum_i a_i b_i=1\): each division replaces a pair by its remainder pair, decreases the nonnegative remainder, and preserves its expression as a linear combination of the original coordinates. The cocharacter \(\lambda_0(z)=(z^{b_1},\ldots,z^{b_r})\) therefore satisfies \(\alpha_0\circ\lambda_0(z)=z\). Consequently \[ T\simeq T_0\times\mathbf G_m,\quad T_0=\ker\alpha_0, \qquad t\longmapsto \bigl(t\lambda_0(\alpha_0(t))^{-1},\alpha_0(t)\bigr). \] The inverse sends \((u,z)\) to \(u\lambda_0(z)\). The basis operations are integral and invertible. For a nonzero coordinate pair \((a,b)\), put \(d=\gcd(a,b)>0\) and choose \(u a+v b=d\) by that algorithm. Right multiplication of the row by \[ \begin{pmatrix}u&-b/d\\v&a/d\end{pmatrix} \] sends \((a,b)\) to \((d,0)\), and this matrix has determinant one. Repeat with the remaining coordinates, skipping zero pairs and adjusting a final sign. This sends the primitive row \(\alpha_0\) to \((1,0,\ldots,0)\). The corresponding monomial maps and their integer inverses are torus automorphisms by the coefficient classification, and identify \(T_0\) with \(\mathbf G_m^{r-1}\). In these coordinates \(\ker\alpha=T_0\times\mu_\nu\). For \(\nu=1\) or \(2\), the reduced identity component of \(\mu_\nu\) is the point, also when the characteristic is two. Hence the previously defined \(T_\alpha=(\ker\alpha)^0_{\mathrm{red}}\) is exactly \(T_0\).

Consider \[ p:T_\alpha\times\mathbf G_m\longrightarrow T, \qquad (u,z)\longmapsto u\beta(z). \] In the preceding product coordinates, \(\beta(z)=(c(z),z^m)\) for a cocharacter \(c\) of \(T_\alpha\). Changing the first source coordinate by \(u\mapsto u c(z)\) identifies \(p\) with the identity on \(T_\alpha\) times the power map \(z\mapsto z^m\). This is finite free of positive rank \(m\): over any test ring and any unit \(a\), adjoining \(z\) with \(z^m=a\) gives the free algebra with basis \(1,z,\ldots,z^{m-1}\), and \(z^{-1}=a^{-1}z^{m-1}\). It is therefore an fppf cover. This proves the torus product statement with its full scheme structure; no invertibility of two is required.

Choose linked nonzero vectors in the two root lines. The rank-one homomorphism of RG03 Theorem 7.1 sends \[ \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} \quad\hbox{to}\quad n_\alpha=x_\alpha(1)x_{-\alpha}(-1)x_\alpha(1). \] Multiplying the three elementary matrices verifies this identity over every ring. Conjugation by the displayed matrix inverts \(\operatorname{diag}(z,z^{-1})\), so \(n_\alpha\beta(z)n_\alpha^{-1}=\beta(z^{-1})\). Each of the two root groups lies in \(G_\alpha=C_G(T_\alpha)\), by its construction in RG03 Theorem 4.1; hence \(n_\alpha\) centralizes \(T_\alpha\) schematically. After the fppf cover \(p\), write \(t=u\beta(z)\). Then \(\alpha(t)=z^2\) and \[ \begin{aligned} n_\alpha t n_\alpha^{-1}&=u\beta(z^{-1}),\\ t\beta(\alpha(t)^{-1}) &=u\beta(z)\beta(z^{-2})=u\beta(z^{-1}). \end{aligned} \] Equality of morphisms descends through this faithfully flat cover. Thus (2.2) holds over every test algebra. Its right side defines a torus automorphism: its value under \(\alpha\) is \(\alpha(t)^{-1}\), and applying it twice gives \(t\). Therefore \(n_\alpha\) normalizes \(T\) as a group scheme.

Applying an arbitrary character \(x\) to (2.2) gives exactly \[ x\longmapsto x-\langle x,\beta\rangle\alpha. \] Applying (2.2) to a cocharacter \(y\) gives exactly \[ y\longmapsto y-\langle\alpha,y\rangle\beta. \] These are identities on the integral lattices themselves. Substitution of \(\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle=2\) shows that both maps square to the identity and that their paired values equal \(\langle x,y\rangle\). Conjugation transports adjoint weight spaces by this character automorphism and transports their root groups by the uniqueness in RG03 Theorem 4.1. It therefore permutes \(\Phi\). Transporting the full rank-one multiplication identity of RG03 Theorem 7.1, whose coroot and pairing are unique, gives \[ (s_\alpha\delta)^\vee=s_\alpha^\vee(\delta^\vee). \] Thus the coroot image set is preserved and the correspondence respects both reflections.

We also verify that this correspondence is a bijection onto its image set. Put \(V=\mathbf Q\Phi\). The group generated by the restricted reflections on \(V\) is finite because it permutes the finite spanning set \(\Phi\). Average any positive definite inner product on \(V_\mathbf R\) over that finite group. Each restricted reflection fixes \(\ker\langle\ ,\alpha^\vee\rangle\), sends \(\alpha\) to \(-\alpha\), and is orthogonal. Decomposing a vector into its fixed part and its component on the normal line gives \[ \langle v,\alpha^\vee\rangle =\frac{2(v,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}. \] This argument uses only the root permutation and integer pairing already proved, so it does not assume injectivity of the coroot correspondence. If \(\alpha^\vee=\delta^\vee\), their displayed normal vectors agree, making \(\delta=c\alpha\). The two pairings with this common coroot are both two, so \(2c=2\) in \(\mathbf Q\), giving \(c=1\). Thus \(\delta=\alpha\). All the root-datum axioms now hold, and the already proved root-line assertion makes the datum reduced.

Finally let \(L/k\) be any field extension. The exponent-vector coefficient classification leaves both lattices and their pairing unchanged. The coaction weight projectors commute with scalar extension, and a nonzero weight space remains nonzero after faithfully flat extension of a field, so the root set and its lines extend with no added or lost weights. RG03 Theorems 4.1 and 7.1 prove scalar compatibility of the root maps, perfect pairings and unique coroots. Consequently all the constructed data, including both reflection identities, are the scalar extensions of the original data. This proves every assertion. $\square$

For $\operatorname{GL}_n$, both lattices are $\mathbf Z^n$, with dual bases $e_i$ and $e_i^\vee$. The roots and coroots are

$$ \alpha_{ij}=e_i-e_j,\qquad \alpha_{ij}^\vee=e_i^\vee-e_j^\vee\quad(i\ne j). \tag{2.3} $$

For $\operatorname{SL}_2$, write $T=\{\operatorname{diag}(t,t^{-1})\}$. In the resulting copies of $\mathbf Z$, the positive root is $2$ and its coroot is $1$. For $\operatorname{PGL}_2$, use $t\mapsto\operatorname{diag}(t,1)$ modulo scalars: the positive root is $1$ and its coroot is $2$. These two data are dual. Their root systems have the same two-point shape, but their integral data distinguish the groups even in characteristic two.

3. Reflections and chambers

In $X^\vee_\mathbf R$, remove the hyperplanes $\langle\alpha,\lambda\rangle=0$. The connected components are Weyl chambers. They are open convex cones, possibly with an unrestricted central factor. A chamber determines the positive system

$$ \Phi^+_\lambda=\{\alpha:\langle\alpha,\lambda\rangle>0\}. \tag{3.1} $$

Conversely its signs determine the chamber: a prescribed choice of strict signs is an intersection of open half-spaces, hence is convex and connected whenever nonempty. The hyperplanes are defined by integer linear forms on the cocharacter lattice. Approximate any interior point by rational coordinates closely enough to retain its finitely many strict inequalities, then clear their positive common denominator. Thus every chamber contains an integral cocharacter. The central factor imposes no additional inequality. If \(\Phi\) is empty there is one chamber, no simple root, and the reflection group is the identity; all assertions below then have their empty-set interpretation.

We first prove transitivity on chambers. Choose interior points \(p,q\) of two prescribed chambers so that their straight segment meets no intersection of distinct root hyperplanes. Here is the required choice argument. For two nonproportional defining forms \(f,g\), a simultaneous zero on the segment would imply \[ f(p)g(q)-g(p)f(q)=0. \] This is a nonzero polynomial in \(p,q\), since \(f,g\) are independent. Finitely many such zero sets cannot fill the open product of the two chambers: their product is a nonzero polynomial, and a polynomial vanishing on an open box is zero. The latter assertion follows by induction on the number of variables, regarding it as a polynomial in the last variable and using that a nonzero one-variable polynomial has at most its degree many roots. Endpoints lie on no wall, so the chosen segment crosses finitely many hyperplanes, one at a time.

At a crossing point on just one hyperplane, take a sufficiently small ball meeting no other hyperplane. The corresponding orthogonal reflection fixes the crossing point, reverses the two half-balls, and permutes the full hyperplane arrangement by the root-datum axioms. It therefore interchanges the two adjacent chambers. The successive crossed-wall reflections carry the first chamber to the last, proving transitivity.

Fix a chamber and its positive roots. A positive root is simple if it is not a sum of two positive roots. We establish the simple-root assertions directly, before using a rank-two classification. For nonparallel roots \(a,b\) with \((a,b)>0\), put \[ A=\langle b,a^\vee\rangle=\frac{2(a,b)}{(a,a)}, \qquad B=\langle a,b^\vee\rangle=\frac{2(a,b)}{(b,b)}. \] These are positive integers, by (1.2) and the lattice pairing, and \(AB=4(a,b)^2/((a,a)(b,b))<4\). Hence their product is one, two or three, and at least one of \(A,B\) equals one. If \(A=1\), then \(s_a(b)=b-a\) is a root; if \(B=1\), then \(s_b(a)=a-b\) is a root. This proves the difference assertion without importing a classification.

Distinct simple roots have nonpositive inner product: otherwise the positive one of these differences decomposes one of them. They are linearly independent. A dependence would separate into an equality \(v=\sum_i c_i a_i=\sum_j d_j b_j\) of nonnegative real combinations with disjoint supports. Their mutual inner product is nonpositive, while \((v,v)\) is positive unless \(v=0\). The chamber functional is strictly positive on a nonempty positive combination, excluding \(v=0\). A dependence with only one sign is excluded by the same functional. Thus there is no nonzero dependence.

Every positive root is a nonnegative integral combination of simple roots. Indeed, decompose a nonsimple root into two positive roots and continue with each nonsimple summand. If \(h\) is the chamber functional and \(c=\min_{\delta\in\Phi^+}h(\delta)>0\), each step down a branch lowers its value by at least \(c\). Every branch therefore terminates after at most \(h(\gamma)/c\) steps for the original root \(\gamma\); the finitely branching decomposition is finite. Its terminal summands are simple. Since all roots are positive or negative, the simple roots are consequently a basis of \(\mathbf R\Phi\), and every root has integral coefficients all of the same sign in that basis.

For a simple root \(a\), reflection changes only its coefficient in this expansion. A different positive root has a nonzero coefficient at another simple root: parallel lattice vectors have rational ratio, so reducedness excludes any other positive root on the line of \(a\). The unchanged positive coefficient forces the reflected root to remain positive. Thus \(s_a\) permutes \(\Phi^+\setminus\{a\}\).

The chamber inequalities are now exactly positivity on the simple roots. Each simple inequality defines a wall: in the dual basis, take its coordinate zero and all other coordinates positive. Every other positive root has a positive value there, so this is an interior point of that wall. Conversely all other inequalities follow from the nonnegative expansions. Let \(H\) be generated by these simple reflections. The preceding segment argument shows inductively that \(H\) is transitive on chambers: a wall of the current chamber \(vC\), for \(v\in H\), is \(v\) applied to a simple wall, and its reflection is \(v s_a v^{-1}\). Every root hyperplane is a wall of some chamber, by choosing a point on it avoiding the other finitely many hyperplanes. Transporting that chamber to \(C\) shows that its root reflection lies in \(H\). Here the conjugation identity \(v s_a v^{-1}=s_{va}\) follows directly from (1.1), the dual pairing and coroot compatibility. Hence the simple reflections generate the full reflection group.

We can now classify any two-dimensional subsystem entirely within these axioms. For a plane \(P\) spanned by roots, \(\Phi_P=\Phi\cap P\) is finite, spans \(P\), is reduced, and is stable under its root reflections. Restrict (1.2) to \(P\); its Cartan pairings remain integers. Choose a functional nonzero on its finitely many roots. The argument just proved supplies precisely two simple roots \(a,b\). Set \[ k=-\langle b,a^\vee\rangle, l=-\langle a,b^\vee\rangle. \] Both are nonnegative integers, their product is less than four, and one vanishes exactly when both vanish, since their common numerator is \(-2(a,b)\). Interchanging \(a,b\) if necessary gives \[ (k,l)=(0,0),(1,1),(2,1),(3,1). \] In the last two cases \((b,b)/(a,a)=k/l\), so \(a\) is the short root. On coefficient pairs the two reflections are exactly \[ s_a(n,m)=(-n+km,m),\qquad s_b(n,m)=(n,ln-m). \]

Every root of \(\Phi_P\) is in the orbit of \(a\) or \(b\) under these two reflections. For a nonsimple positive root \(\gamma=na+mb\), both coefficients are positive by reducedness. Since \[ (\gamma,\gamma)=n(\gamma,a)+m(\gamma,b)>0, \] one of its inner products with the simple roots is positive. Reflection in that simple root subtracts a positive integer multiple of it, preserves positivity by the simple-root permutation already proved, and strictly lowers the positive integral height \(n+m\). Repetition ends at a simple root. Negative roots follow by sign.

Here is the full orbit calculation. For each row below, write \(R\) for the ordered positive-root list; the displayed lists give the image of each entry under the indicated reflection, in the same order: \[ \begin{array}{c|l} A_1\times A_1&R=(a,b)\\ &s_aR=(-a,b),\quad s_bR=(a,-b)\\[2pt] A_2&R=(a,b,a+b)\\ &s_aR=(-a,a+b,b),\quad s_bR=(a+b,-b,a)\\[2pt] B_2&R=(a,b,a+b,2a+b)\\ &s_aR=(-a,2a+b,a+b,b)\\ &s_bR=(a+b,-b,a,2a+b)\\[2pt] G_2&R=(a,b,a+b,2a+b,3a+b,3a+2b)\\ &s_aR=(-a,3a+b,2a+b,a+b,b,3a+2b)\\ &s_bR=(a+b,-b,a,2a+b,3a+2b,3a+b). \end{array} \tag{3.2} \] These images follow by substituting \((n,m)\) into the two displayed coefficient maps. Each set \(R\cup(-R)\) is therefore stable under both reflections and contains \(a,b\), so it contains their entire orbits. Conversely, all entries are reached: in \(A_2\), \(s_a b=a+b\); in \(B_2\), \(s_b a=a+b\) and \(s_a b=2a+b\); in \(G_2\), use \(s_b a=a+b\), \(s_a(a+b)=2a+b\), \(s_a b=3a+b\), and \(s_b(3a+b)=3a+2b\). Negatives are in the same orbits because \(-a=s_a a\), \(-b=s_b b\) and the maps are linear. The descent just proved shows that these are exactly all roots, with no missing orbit.

Finally this calculation supplies the wall angles used below. If \(\varphi\) is the obtuse or right angle between \(a,b\), their positive chamber has angle \(\theta=\pi-\varphi\), with \(0<\theta\le\pi/2\) and \(4\cos^2\theta=kl\). The four products zero, one, two and three give respectively \(\theta=\pi/2,\pi/3,\pi/4,\pi/6\); these cosine values follow from a right isosceles triangle and an equilateral triangle, and cosine is strictly decreasing on this interval. The product of the two wall reflections rotates their plane by \(2\theta\), up to orientation, as is seen by reflecting a direction angle first about one line and then about the other. If \(m=2,3,4,6\) respectively, it has order \(m\). Its rotations and the two wall reflections produce the \(m\) equally spaced reflecting lines, separated by \(\pi/m\). There are exactly \(m\) root lines in the verified lists (3.2), so these are every line of the subsystem. This proves the full rank-two and simple-root calculations used in the chamber and word arguments.

Length and standard parabolic cosets

We first prove the reflection assertion entirely in the root space, before using any group normalizer. This also makes the length and word results available for every reduced root datum.

Lemma 3.A (a chamber has trivial stabilizer). The reflection group acts freely on chambers.

Proof. For an empty root set the group is the identity, so the assertion holds. Otherwise work in the Euclidean root space, discarding the central factor, on which all reflections act trivially. Reflection across a wall carries either adjacent chamber to the other. Starting with a fixed chamber $C$, a path crossing one wall at a time therefore transports $C$ by a product of the crossed-wall reflections. If the current transport is $v$, a wall of $vC$ is $v$ applied to a simple wall of $C$; crossing it replaces $v$ by $vs_i$. Thus words in the simple reflections and such wall galleries give the same transports.

We show that a closed gallery has identity transport. Realize it by a polygonal loop avoiding intersections of two hyperplanes at its crossings. Fill the loop by a polygonal disk in the root space; for example join each of its edges to an auxiliary interior vertex. Subdivide and perturb the interior vertices slightly. Because there are finitely many hyperplanes, the vertices can be chosen so that the disk avoids their intersections of codimension at least three, meets each codimension-two intersection transversely in finitely many interior points, and crosses single hyperplanes transversely away from these points. These choices exclude finitely many proper algebraic conditions on the perturbations. Intersections of codimension greater than two can be avoided by a two-dimensional disk; the same statement in dimensions one and two has no such intersections to avoid.

The inverse images of the walls now divide the disk into regions. Passing a path across an ordinary wall arc and back inserts two copies of the same reflection, whose product is the identity. Passing across an intersection point changes its gallery by the small circuit around that point. Its walls have normals in the two-dimensional orthogonal complement of the intersection. The roots in this plane form exactly one of the rank-two systems (3.2). By the calculation preceding that list, consecutive reflecting lines have angle $\pi/m$, for $m=2,3,4,6$. The $2m$ successive crossings around a small full circle have product the identity: consecutive pairs rotate this plane by $2\pi/m$, and $m$ such pairs make a full rotation. Every factor fixes the common orthogonal complement, so the product is the identity on the whole root space. The orientation-reversed circuit has the inverse product and gives the same conclusion.

To see that these are all changes needed, cut the disk along disjoint arcs from its finitely many intersection points to its boundary. In the cut polygon any closed path can be reduced by successively removing its innermost crossings of wall arcs. Restoring each cut inserts the corresponding small circuit or its inverse. Hence the boundary gallery has identity transport, by the two calculations just given. In dimension one a loop consists directly of back-and-forth crossings and has the same conclusion.

Suppose $wC=C$. Choose a word for $w$ in simple reflections. Its successive chambers give a gallery from $C$ back to $C$. Join the two endpoint representatives inside the convex chamber, producing a closed gallery without an additional crossing. Its transport is $w$, so the preceding argument gives $w=1$ on the root space. Section 1 proved that this action determines $w$ on the full lattice. Thus $w=1$. $\square$

The cell dimensions below now follow from this independent chamber proof. No normalizer identification is used in the length calculation.

Proposition 3.1. Let $\ell(w)$ be the minimum number of simple reflections in an expression for $w$. Then $\ell(w)=|\Psi_w|$, where $\Psi_w=\{\alpha\in\Phi^+:w^{-1}\alpha<0\}$. For $J\subset\Delta$, each right coset of $W_J=\langle s_\alpha:\alpha\in J\rangle$ has a unique element $w$ of minimum length. It is characterized by $w(\Phi_J^+)\subset\Phi^+$. For this $w$ and every $z\in W_J$,

$$ \Psi_{wz}=\Psi_w\sqcup w\Psi_z, \qquad \ell(wz)=\ell(w)+\ell(z). $$

Proof. Fix the positive chamber $C$. A word in simple reflections gives a gallery of adjacent chambers from $C$ to $wC$: if the current chamber is $vC$, its wall corresponding to a simple root leads to $vs_\alpha C$. Every such gallery must cross each hyperplane separating $C$ from $wC$. There are $|\Psi_w|$ of these, since the sign of a positive root on $wC$ is the sign of $w^{-1}\alpha$ on $C$.

Conversely choose interior points of $C$ and $wC$ generically, so the straight segment between them never meets an intersection of distinct root hyperplanes. A hyperplane with endpoints on the same side is not crossed; each separating hyperplane is crossed exactly once. The segment therefore supplies a gallery with $|\Psi_w|$ steps. Write its successive chambers as $vC$ by the preceding adjacent-wall rule. At the end, $vC=wC$; freeness from Lemma 3.A gives $v=w$. Thus the gallery supplies a word of that length and proves the length formula. The same wall calculation gives $\ell(ws_\alpha)=\ell(w)+1$ or $\ell(w)-1$ according as $w\alpha$ is positive or negative.

Choose a minimum-length element $w$ of a right coset, which exists because $W$ is finite. The last calculation gives $w\alpha>0$ for every $\alpha\in J$. Every root in $\Phi_J^+$ is a nonnegative integral combination of those simple roots; its image is consequently positive. Thus $w(\Phi_J^+)\subset\Phi^+$.

Each generator of $W_J$ changes only the coefficient of its own simple root in a root's simple-root expansion. A positive root outside $\Phi_J$ has a positive coefficient outside $J$, which remains unchanged. Since all coefficients of a root have the same sign, every element of $W_J$ preserves its positivity. The corresponding assertion holds for negative roots outside $\Phi_J$. In particular $\Psi_z\subset\Phi_J^+$ for $z\in W_J$.

Now suppose $w$ sends $\Phi_J^+$ to positive roots. If $\alpha\in\Psi_w$, then $w^{-1}\alpha$ is negative and outside the Levi subsystem: a negative Levi root would have negative image under $w$, whereas $\alpha$ is positive. Applying $z^{-1}$ therefore leaves it negative. If $\alpha\notin\Psi_w$ is positive, then $w^{-1}\alpha$ is positive, and $z^{-1}w^{-1}\alpha$ is negative precisely when $w^{-1}\alpha\in\Psi_z$. These two cases prove the displayed disjoint inversion-root identity. Its cardinalities and the length formula prove length additivity. They also show that any element with the positive-Levi condition is a minimum in its right coset.

Finally, if two such minima satisfy $w'=wz$, applying additivity in both directions gives $\ell(w')=\ell(w)+\ell(z)$ and $\ell(w)=\ell(w')+\ell(z^{-1})$. Since $\ell(z^{-1})=\ell(z)$, we get $\ell(z)=0$ and $z=1$. This proves uniqueness. $\square$

Relations between reflection words

The same length calculation determines all relations between simple reflections. This is the Coxeter presentation used in the pinned isomorphism argument of the next lesson. Its inputs are the rank-two root calculation (3.2), chamber freeness in Lemma 3.A, and the standard-parabolic coset calculation in Proposition 3.1. The proof below applies to every reduced root datum, including its central lattice directions.

For distinct simple roots $\alpha_i,\alpha_j$, put $c_{ij}=\langle\alpha_i,\alpha_j^\vee\rangle\langle\alpha_j,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle$. The invariant positive definite form gives $c_{ij}=4\cos^2\theta$, where $\theta$ is the obtuse angle between these roots. The rank-two calculation (3.2) gives $c_{ij}=0,1,2,3$ and $m_{ij}=2,3,4,6$, respectively. On the plane spanned by these roots, the product $s_i s_j$ is a rotation through $2\pi/m_{ij}$, up to orientation, and on its orthogonal complement it is the identity. Hence it has order $m_{ij}$. The group $W_{ij}=\langle s_i,s_j\rangle$ consists of the $m_{ij}$ rotations $(s_i s_j)^a$ and the $m_{ij}$ reflections $(s_i s_j)^a s_i$.

After cancelling adjacent equal letters, words in two involutions alternate. Write $R=s_i s_j$. Alternating words of length $2a$ are $R^a$ and $R^{-a}$, while those of length $2a+1$ are $R^a s_i$ and $R^{-a-1}s_i$. Comparing these exponents modulo $m_{ij}$ shows that all alternating words of length less than $m_{ij}$ are distinct and reduced. The two alternating words of length $m_{ij}$ agree and give the unique longest element. These words exhaust the $2m_{ij}$ elements: there is one empty word, two words at each length $1,\ldots,m_{ij}-1$, and one element at length $m_{ij}$.

A braid move replaces either alternating block of length $m_{ij}$ by the other. With the involution relations, it is equivalent to $(s_i s_j)^{m_{ij}}=1$.

Lemma 3.2 (two left descents). If both $s_i$ and $s_j$ shorten $w$ on the left, there is a length-additive factorization $w=v_0u$, where $v_0$ is the longest element of $W_{ij}$ and $u$ has minimum length in $W_{ij}w$.

Proof. Invert the unique minimum representative assertion of Proposition 3.1 to obtain the left-coset version. It gives $w=vu$, $v\in W_{ij}$, with $u^{-1}(\Phi_{ij}^+)\subset\Phi^+$ and $\ell(vu)=\ell(v)+\ell(u)$. A positive root outside $\Phi_{ij}$ has a positive coefficient on some other simple root; neither $s_i$ nor $s_j$ changes that coefficient. Such a root remains positive under $W_{ij}$, so the ambient length of $v$ equals its rank-two length.

For $k=i,j$, the root $v^{-1}\alpha_k$ belongs to $\Phi_{ij}$, and $u^{-1}$ preserves its sign. Thus $w^{-1}\alpha_k<0$ implies $v^{-1}\alpha_k<0$. Every positive root in $\Phi_{ij}$ is a nonnegative combination of $\alpha_i,\alpha_j$, so $v^{-1}$ makes all of them negative. Its length is consequently the number of rank-two positive roots. This is the rank-two longest element just calculated. $\square$

Lemma 3.3 (reduced words). Any two reduced words for the same element are connected by braid moves.

Proof. Induct on the length $k$. If the first letters agree, cancel them, apply induction to the two tails of length $k-1$, and restore the first letter. Otherwise the two first letters are $s_i\ne s_j$, so both shorten $w$ on the left. Lemma 3.2 gives $w=v_0u$, with $\ell(w)=m_{ij}+\ell(u)$. Fix a reduced word for $u$. Prefix it with either alternating length-$m_{ij}$ word for $v_0$. Both resulting words are reduced; one starts with $s_i$, the other with $s_j$. After cancelling the first $s_i$, induction connects the first original word to the first constructed word. The same induction with $s_j$ treats the second original word. A single braid move connects the two constructed prefixes. Composing these moves proves the assertion. $\square$

Theorem 3.4 (Coxeter presentation). For the Weyl group of the root datum under consideration,

$$ W=\langle s_i\mid s_i^2=1,\ (s_i s_j)^{m_{ij}}=1\ (i\ne j)\rangle. $$

Proof. The relations hold by the reflection and rank-two calculations, so the presented group surjects onto $W$. Reduce an arbitrary word by induction on its number of letters. First reduce its prefix to a reduced word for its value $a$. If the final letter $s_i$ increases length, the word is reduced. If it decreases length, choose a reduced word $A$ for $as_i$. Then $A s_i$ is reduced for $a$, by Proposition 3.1. Lemma 3.3 changes the reduced prefix to $A s_i$ by braid moves. The entire word becomes $A s_i s_i$, and the involution relation cancels the last two letters. Thus the given relations turn every word into a reduced word for its actual value. A word representing $1$ reduces to the empty word. The kernel is zero. $\square$

The argument applies to the full character lattice, including its central summand: reflections fix that summand, and the faithful action on the root span in Section 1 determines their relations.

4. Borel subgroups are positive systems

We first work over an algebraically closed field. $$ L_G(\lambda)=T,\qquad P_G(\lambda)=T\ltimes U_G(\lambda),\qquad \operatorname{Lie}U_G(\lambda) =\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\lambda\rangle>0}\mathfrak g_\alpha. \tag{4.2} $$

Here \(P_G(\lambda)\) is reduced and irreducible by the complete component argument in S08 Theorem G.4.2. Interchanging the two gives equality. Different chambers give different root summands in (4.2), so give different Borels.

Every Borel containing \(T\) is obtained. This proves the correspondence over the algebraically closed field.

Theorem 4.1. For a split reductive group over any field, Borels containing its split maximal torus correspond bijectively to positive systems. The normalizer Weyl group equals the reflection Weyl group and acts simply transitively on these systems.

Theorem 4.2 (the relative Weyl scheme). \(W_G(T)\) is represented by a finite étale group scheme over \(S\).

Proof. Here \(G/S\) is reductive, \(T\) is a maximal \(S\)-torus, and \(W_G(T)\) denotes the fppf sheaf quotient \(N_G(T)/T\). A test point of the normalizer must preserve the entire subgroup \(T\) after that base change. We construct this quotient and its scheme structure over the original base.

On an affine neighbourhood, a finite generating set of the finite locally free module \(\operatorname{Lie}G\) has coactions involving only finitely many characters of \(X\). Choose a positive base and trivialize its finitely many simple root lines. The perfect opposite pairings give the linked negative frames.

5. Coordinates on the unipotent radicals

We need actual scheme coordinates. The following complete graded argument works over an arbitrary base ring, including one with nilpotents.

Lemma 5.1. Suppose a smooth affine $R$-scheme $Y$ of finite presentation has a section $e$ and a $\mathbf G_m$-action which extends to $\mathbf A^1$ and sends $0\times Y$ to $e$. Suppose the tangent module at $e$ is free with positive weights $d_1,\ldots,d_N$. A $\mathbf G_m$-equivariant map from $\mathbf A_R^N$, with these weights, to $Y$ whose differential at zero is an isomorphism is an isomorphism.

Proof. Put \(A=\Gamma(Y,\mathcal O_Y)\). The full torus coefficient grading in RG01 gives \(A=\bigoplus_{d\in\mathbf Z}A_d\). Extension of the coaction from \(R[t,t^{-1}]\) to \(R[t]\) forces every negative-degree component to be zero: these monomials are linearly independent even over a ring with nilpotents. Evaluation at \(t=0\) is the map factoring through \(e\). For \(f\in A_0\) it gives \(f=e^*(f)\) in \(A\), so \(A_0=R\), with the inclusion split by the section. Its augmentation ideal is \(I=\bigoplus_{d>0}A_d\). The fixed subscheme is precisely this section, so equivariance sends the zero section to \(e\) and intertwines the two augmentation maps.

Replace a finite algebra generating list by all of its finitely many homogeneous components, and discard the degree-zero components, already in \(R\). If none remain, \(A=R\); the tangent isomorphism gives \(N=0\) when \(R\ne0\), and the assertion is immediate. For \(R=0\) both schemes are empty, also giving the assertion. Otherwise let \(q>0\) and \(D\) be the minimum and maximum of these positive generator degrees. For \(F_hA=\bigoplus_{d\ge h}A_d\), the monomial calculation gives \[ I^n\subset F_{nq}A,\qquad F_{(n-1)D+1}A\subset I^n. \] Indeed a product of \(n\) positive generators has degree at least \(nq\), whereas a monomial of length less than \(n\) has degree at most \((n-1)D\). Homogeneous expressions in the chosen generators span each component, so these inclusions survive all algebra relations. The two filtrations are cofinal. Consequently \[ \widehat A_I=\prod_{d\ge0}A_d, \] with multiplication defined by the finite sums of component products in each degree. In particular \(A\) injects into its completion, and a homogeneous element of completed weight \(d\) is exactly an element of \(A_d\). The polynomial algebra \(P=R[x_1,\ldots,x_N]\), with \(\deg x_i=d_i\) and augmentation \(J\), has the same properties.

Write \(\phi:A\to P\) for the given map. Its differential is invertible along the entire zero section. Both schemes are smooth, so the complete differential proof in S02 Lemma 3.A makes the morphism étale on an open neighbourhood \(V\) of that section. We give the full completion inverse. For \(n=1\), the zero section is a map \(\operatorname{Spec}(A/I)=\operatorname{Spec}R\to V\) over \(Y\). Inductively, \[ A/I^{n+1}\longrightarrow A/I^n \] has square-zero kernel \(I^n/I^{n+1}\). Formal étaleness lifts the preceding map uniquely to \(\operatorname{Spec}(A/I^{n+1})\to V\), over the natural map to \(Y\). All these thickenings have the same underlying space as the section, so the chosen étale neighbourhood applies at every stage. The complete infinitesimal criterion in AG-FSE, Infinitesimal lifting and invariance under thickenings, Theorem 4.1 supplies this formal property. The compatible maps send every element of \(J\) into \(I/I^n\) at stage \(n\) and therefore factor through \(P/J^n\to A/I^n\). Taking inverse limits gives \(\widehat\psi:\widehat P_J\to\widehat A_I\).

Their being maps over \(Y\) gives \(\widehat\psi\widehat\phi=1\). To check the other composite, test on every \(P/J^n\): the identity map and the composite are two lifts of the same zero section, over the same \(A\)-map, through the étale \(V\to Y\). Uniqueness makes them equal. Thus the completions are mutually inverse. Equivariance follows from this same uniqueness after adjoining the universal Laurent variable for \(\mathbf G_m\). The completed inverse therefore preserves each integer weight. By the displayed product description it carries \(P_d\) into \(A_d\); the forward map does likewise. Restricting the two inverses to their direct sums gives inverse algebra maps \(P\leftrightarrows A\). This proves the assertion over the original arbitrary \(R\). \(\square\)

Work locally where the split torus, the root datum and the finitely many root lines are trivialized, and choose an integral chamber cocharacter \(\lambda\). RG01 Lemma 3.3 gives the smooth closed \(U_G(\lambda)\), its contraction and its positive Lie summands. It also gives \(L_G(\lambda)\). The inclusion \(T\to L_G(\lambda)\) has invertible differential because no root has zero pairing. S02 Lemma 3.A and the étale-monomorphism argument of S04 Lemma E7.2 make it an open immersion. On each geometric fibre the full proof of Section 4 identifies this centralizer with \(T\); hence the open image is all of \(L_G(\lambda)\). Thus (4.2) holds over this base, with \(P_G(\lambda)=T\ltimes U_G(\lambda)\).

For a supplied Borel subgroup scheme \(B\) containing \(T\) with this positive system, its Lie weight summands are exactly \(\mathfrak t\) and the positive root lines. This follows on the original base from their geometric fibre identities: the inclusions of the finite projective weight modules have invertible matrices on every fibre, hence are isomorphisms locally. Apply RG01 Lemma 3.3 also inside the smooth affine \(B\). Its \(P_B(\lambda)\) has the whole Lie algebra of \(B\), so its closed inclusion is étale and open by the same differential and monomorphism proofs. On every geometric fibre its nonempty open-and-closed image is all of the connected Borel; hence \(P_B(\lambda)=B\). Functoriality of the limit now gives a closed inclusion \(B\subset P_G(\lambda)\). Their displayed Lie gradings agree, so this inclusion is again étale and open, and the full field correspondence of Section 4 makes it surjective on all geometric fibres. Its open image is therefore the whole target. We have proved \(B=P_G(\lambda)=T\ltimes U^+\) as schemes over this base. This includes nilpotents and is the relative Borel identity used in Theorem 8.1.

For any ordering \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_N\) of \(\Phi^+\), multiplication is equivariant for \(\lambda\), on the source by the positive weights \(\langle\alpha_i,\lambda\rangle\). Its differential at the identity is the direct sum of these root lines onto \(\operatorname{Lie}U_G(\lambda)\). This proves (5.1) for root line bundles as well. Changing the ordering changes coordinates on the same \(U_G(\lambda)\). Changing the cocharacter within the positive chamber gives the same subgroup: the product image of the same root groups is the whole subgroup in both cases. We denote it by \(U^+\). Applying the same proof to \(-\lambda\), the full open-cell immersion of RG01 Lemma 3.3 gives \[ U^-\times T\times U^+\hookrightarrow G. \tag{5.2} \] Each construction is defined by the canonical root maps, multiplication and grading, and thus commutes with every base change.

The inverse of (5.1) supplies root coordinates. For nonparallel roots \(a,b\), a positive system containing both exists: the metric vector \(a/\|a\|+b/\|b\|\) pairs positively with each, since \(1+(a,b)/(\|a\|\|b\|)>0\). In the resulting \(U^+\), the commutator has the form \[ [x_a(u),x_b(v)] =\prod_{ia+jb\in\Phi,\ i,j>0} x_{ia+jb}(C_{ij}u^iv^j). \tag{5.3} \] Here a fixed ordering is used. To prove it, take the coordinate belonging to any root \(\gamma\). It is a polynomial in \(u,v\), because (5.1) is an isomorphism of affine schemes. Setting \(u=0\) or \(v=0\) gives the identity commutator, so its polynomial is divisible by both variables, by coefficient comparison over the base ring. This proves every coordinate of (5.3) over all test algebras; Laurent character comparison includes nilpotents and every characteristic.

More generally let \(C\) be a subset of the real root space closed under addition, and put \(\Psi=\Phi^+\cap C\). Set every root coordinate outside \(\Psi\) equal to zero. This defines a smooth closed subscheme parametrized by the root lines in \(\Psi\). It is a subgroup. Indeed the coordinate polynomials for multiplication and inversion are torus-equivariant and vanish at the identity. Each nonconstant monomial has target character equal to the sum of its input characters, with their positive integer multiplicities. If all those characters lie in \(C\), so does their sum; hence every coordinate outside \(\Psi\) is zero after either operation. These are polynomial identities over the base, proving the subgroup assertion schematically.

It is independent of the ordering. The product in a second ordering has no coordinate outside \(\Psi\) in the first ordering, by the same weight calculation, and has invertible identity differential. Both sides are contracted by \(\lambda\), so Lemma 5.1 makes the resulting coordinate map an isomorphism after trivializing the lines. These isomorphisms descend. Write the resulting closed subgroup as \(U_\Psi\). If the same root subset occurs in two positive systems, the same ordered multiplication of its canonical root groups is an isomorphism onto both images. They are consequently the same subgroup of \(G\), independently of the ambient positive system. For \[ \Psi_w=\Phi^+\cap w\Phi^-, \qquad \Theta_w=\Phi^+\cap w\Phi^+, \tag{5.4} \] the relevant additive sets are the intersections of two strict positive or negative half-spaces, so are closed under addition. The two root subsets partition \(\Phi^+\). Ordering all roots in \(\Psi_w\) before those in \(\Theta_w\) in (5.1) proves the scheme product \[ U_{\Psi_w}\times U_{\Theta_w}\simeq U^+. \] The factors need not commute; this statement specifies their multiplication order.

6. The Bruhat cells from a projective limit

We give the projective limit argument, including its smoothness and its scheme structures over the field of definition.

Lemma 6.1. Let a $\mathbf G_m$ act on a smooth projective variety with an equivariant projective embedding. Suppose its fixed points are isolated. The points whose limit at zero is a fixed point $p$ form an affine locally closed scheme $Y_p$, contracted to $p$, and its tangent space at $p$ is the positive-weight part of $T_pY$. An equivariant map from a positive-weight affine space to $Y_p$, with invertible differential at zero, is an isomorphism.

Proof. First take \(p\) rational over the field in use. Diagonalize the vector space of the given linear projective action and write the weights as \(m_i\). A fixed point is represented by a line in a single weight space, say of weight \(m\). Choose a coordinate of that weight nonzero at \(p\), and normalize it to one. Its affine chart \(U\) is invariant under \(\mathbf G_m\) and is smooth affine. The other coordinates \(z_i\) have relative weights \(m_i-m\). The limit equals \(p\) precisely when \[ z_i=0\quad(m_i<m),\qquad z_i=z_i(p)\quad(m_i=m). \] The remaining coordinates have positive weights and vanish at \(p\). Intersect these equations with \(Y\cap U\). They give an affine closed subscheme of this chart, hence an affine locally closed \(Y_p\subset Y\), with nonnegative coordinate grading, degree-zero field and contraction to \(p\).

This also represents the limit condition on arbitrary test algebras. A Laurent orbit \(z_i(t)=t^{m_i-m}z_i\) extends to \(t=0\) in the chart exactly when every negative coefficient is zero, and its value at zero gives exactly the displayed zero-weight equations. Laurent monomials are independent over every test ring. Conversely the equations give the polynomial orbit extension with that value. If an orbit has limit \(p\) in \(Y\), its extension lies in this chart: its inverse image of the invariant closed complement misses both \(D(t)\) and \(V(t)\), since the latter maps to \(p\). These two subsets cover the underlying affine line on every scheme test. Thus the extension factors through the chart. The construction is consequently intrinsic and independent of the chosen nonzero coordinate.

We prove smoothness by full equivariant square-zero lifting. Let \(C\twoheadrightarrow\bar C=C/J\), with \(J^2=0\), and let \(y_0\in Y_p(\bar C)\). Its polynomial orbit is an equivariant map \(F_0:\mathbf A^1_{\bar C}\to U\) with \(F_0(0)=p\). Formal smoothness of the affine \(U\), by the complete AG-FSE infinitesimal criterion, supplies a scheme lift \(F:\mathbf A^1_C\to U\). Differences of lifts are derivations into \(J[t]\). If \[ E=\Gamma(\mathbf A^1_{\bar C},F_0^*T_U),\qquad M=E\otimes_{\bar C[t]}J[t], \] this module of differences is \(M\): the smooth cotangent module is finite projective, and duality identifies its homomorphisms to the square-zero ideal with the displayed tangent tensor. The calculation takes place also on \(C[t,a,a^{-1}]\) and on the analogous two-parameter ring; these polynomial and Laurent extensions are flat, so their kernel ideals are exactly the corresponding extensions of \(J\).

Evaluation \(M\to T_pU\otimes J\) is surjective, with kernel \(M_0=E\otimes_{\bar C[t]}tJ[t]\), because the finite projective \(E\) preserves the exact polynomial evaluation sequence. Correct \(F\) by such a derivation to make \(F(0)=p\). On lifts use the action \[ (aF)(t)=a\cdot F(a^{-1}t). \] Its reduction fixes \(F_0\). The difference \(c(a)=(aF)-F\) is therefore a regular section with values in \(M_0\), and \[ c(ab)=c(a)+a c(b). \] This follows by subtracting \(F\) through the intermediate \(aF\); differences add because \(J^2=0\). The module \(M_0\) is stable under this action and is a quasi-coherent \(\mathbf G_m\)-module over \(\bar C\). The full coefficient-integral homotopy in AG-GS, Diagonalizable groups, Proposition 7.14 applies even with infinitely many weights and supplies \(w\in M_0\) with \(c(a)=w-aw\). The corrected map \(F'=F+w\) is then equivariant, since \[ (aF')-F'=c(a)+aw-w=0. \] Its value at zero is still \(p\). Evaluation at one gives a lift \(y\in Y_p(C)\) by the universal coordinate condition already proved. Thus \(Y_p\) is formally smooth on every affine square-zero test, and it is of finite presentation by its finite chart equations. The same infinitesimal criterion makes it smooth.

For the tangent calculation, a first-order orbit at \(p\) is \(\sum_n t^n v_n\), where \(v_n\) is the weight-\(n\) part of a tangent vector. Extension permits exactly \(n\ge0\), and value zero at the fixed section excludes \(n=0\). Hence \(T_pY_p=\bigoplus_{n>0}(T_pY)_n\). Its positive-weight contraction and smoothness now meet all hypotheses of the complete Lemma 5.1, which proves the last assertion.

The equations, orbit functor and lifting calculations commute with field extension. For a nonrational closed isolated fixed point, put \(K=\kappa(p)\). The fixed scheme is smooth by the entire fixed-scheme proof in AG-GS, Diagonalizable groups, Theorem 3.4. At an isolated point its smooth cotangent rank is zero; the full unramified field classification in AG-FSE, Unramified morphisms, Lemma 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 consequently makes \(K/k\) finite separable. In the same projective chart impose the ideal of this \(\operatorname{Spec}K\) point in the weight-zero coordinates and set the negative coordinates to zero. The resulting degree-zero algebra is \(K\). As a \(K\)-scheme it is exactly the preceding chart construction in \(Y_K\) with the fixed coordinate values of \(p\). It is affine over \(K\) and a locally closed \(k\)-subscheme of \(Y\); the smoothness and tangent conclusions just proved over \(K\) give the asserted stratum, and finite separability adds no tangent directions. Geometric fixed points are handled over their field of definition; in the last assertion the affine space and its zero point are over that same field. This proves the original geometric and field versions. \(\square\)

Let \(B\) correspond to \(\Phi^+\), put \(X=G/B\), and choose an integral chamber cocharacter \(\lambda\). The full quotient proof in AG-GS, Quotients and torsors, Theorem 11.1b gives the fppf \(B\)-torsor \(G\to X\), and Theorem 11.7 gives smoothness. Geometrically the complete flag proof in RG01 Lemma 2.1 realizes \(X\) in a linear \(G\)-projective space. We use that embedding over an algebraic closure for the projective limit calculation; all cells will also have the explicit charts over \(k\) constructed below.

The \(T\)-fixed cosets are precisely \(n_wB\), \(w\in W\). Geometrically a fixed coset corresponds to a Borel containing \(T\), because its full stabilizer is the conjugate Borel. The earlier Borel normalizer theorem identifies these cosets with the Borels themselves, and Theorem 4.1 gives their simply transitive Weyl action. The representatives are all defined over \(k\).

There are no further \(\lambda\)-fixed points. On the geometric fibre, the closed fixed scheme \(X^\lambda\) is smooth by AG-GS, Diagonalizable groups, Theorem 3.4 applied to the stable affine charts of that projective embedding. Its connected components are \(T\)-stable: \(T\) is connected and commutes with \(\lambda\), so its action cannot change the finite component label. Every such component contains a \(T\)-fixed point. To see this directly, choose an integral cocharacter \(\eta\) separating the finitely many \(T\)-characters of the embedding, by the integer separating-cocharacter construction in RG03 Lemma 2.1. For any point of that closed projective component, take its least nonzero \(\eta\)-weight projective limit. Separation leaves a single \(T\)-character space, so its line is \(T\)-fixed and remains in the closed component.

At \(n_wB\), the tangent quotient for the smooth \(B\)-torsor is \[ T_{n_wB}X=\mathfrak g/ \operatorname{Lie}(n_wBn_w^{-1}). \] The toral summand disappears, and its root weights are \(\Phi\setminus w\Phi^+\). None is zero on \(\lambda\). The fixed tangent space is therefore zero. Smoothness makes a neighbourhood in \(X^\lambda\) zero-dimensional and étale over the geometric field; its points are open reduced points by the complete unramified field classification. The point \(n_wB\) is also closed. Connectedness consequently makes its fixed component exactly this point. Thus the entire fixed scheme is the finite reduced set \(\{n_wB\}\); all of these points are \(k\)-rational.

We identify its attracting strata with charts defined over \(k\), retaining their full scheme structures. The open immersion (5.2) is right \(B\)-stable and is \(U^-\times B\). Invariant-open descent and the product torsor, proved in S04 Lemma A1.6 and AG-GS Quotients and torsors, give an affine open \(V_1\simeq U^-\) in \(X\). Its translate \[ V_w=n_wV_1\simeq n_wU^-n_w^{-1} \] is an affine \(T\)-stable chart about \(n_wB\). The ordered root coordinates of this group have characters \(w\Phi^-\); this follows from the canonical root equivariance and uniqueness in RG03 Theorem 4.1. The roots on which \(\lambda\) is positive are exactly \(\Psi_w\). The coordinate argument of Lemma 6.1 therefore cuts its attracting subscheme out by setting every negative-weight coordinate to zero. By the complete closed-root-subgroup proof in Section 5, it is precisely \(U_{\Psi_w}\).

It is also the global attracting subscheme. A point with limit \(n_wB\) must lie in \(V_w\): otherwise its orbit lies in the invariant closed complement, and its limit would lie in that complement too. This argument holds on every geometric point of a scheme test; an open immersion contains the whole test map when it contains all its underlying points. Within the chart, the polynomial orbit criterion of Lemma 6.1 works on every test algebra. Thus \[ U_{\Psi_w}\xrightarrow{\sim}X_{n_wB}^+, \qquad u\longmapsto un_wB. \tag{6.1} \] Its tangent map is exactly \(\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Psi_w}\mathfrak g_\alpha\), the positive part of the displayed tangent quotient. These identities and charts are defined over \(k\) and commute with field extension.

Every geometric point of \(X\) has a unique projective \(\lambda\)-limit: choose the least nonzero weight among its homogeneous coordinates, divide the orbit coordinates by that power of \(t\), and evaluate at zero. The defining homogeneous equations of the closed projective variety still vanish on this polynomial orbit extension. Thus its limit is in \(X\) and is fixed. Separatedness makes the extension unique; equivalently the normalized least-weight line fixes the limit. Consequently (6.1) gives a finite disjoint locally closed partition of \(X\).

These strata are exactly the \(B\)-orbits as schemes. In (5.4), every root of \(\Theta_w\) is in \(w\Phi^+\), so its full root group, as well as \(T\), belongs to the stabilizer \(n_wBn_w^{-1}\). The torus normalizes both coordinate subgroups and \(U^+=U_{\Psi_w}U_{\Theta_w}\) in the specified order. Hence \(B\)'s orbit map at \(n_wB\) factors through \(U_{\Psi_w}\), and its image is exactly (6.1), including on nonreduced tests. In particular its schematic stabilizer inside \(B\) is \(U_{\Theta_w}\rtimes T\): the remaining \(U_{\Psi_w}\) coordinate must be the identity, by the injectivity of (6.1).

Pull the full right \(B\)-torsor \(G\to X\) back to this stratum. The morphism \(u\mapsto un_w\) is a section. It gives mutually inverse maps \[ U_{\Psi_w}\times B\xrightarrow{\sim}Bn_wB,\qquad (u,b)\longmapsto un_wb,\qquad g\longmapsto\bigl(u,(un_w)^{-1}g\bigr), \tag{6.2} \] where \(u\) is the inverse image of \(gB\) under (6.1). The torsor fibre-product identity proves that the second coordinate lies in \(B\) on every test scheme. The displayed target is the locally closed inverse image of the stratum in \(G\), so these are isomorphisms with that exact scheme structure.

Theorem 6.2 (Bruhat decomposition). For a split connected reductive group over any field $k$,

$$ G=\coprod_{w\in W}Bn_wB \quad\text{as a locally closed stratification}, \qquad G(k)=\coprod_{w\in W}B(k)n_wB(k). \tag{6.3} $$

Moreover $Bn_wB$ has dimension $\dim B+|\Psi_w|$, and its quotient by the right $B$-action is an affine space of dimension $|\Psi_w|$.

Proof. The full preceding construction gives (6.1), (6.2), and the disjoint geometric covering. These schemes and maps are defined over \(k\). Membership in a locally closed \(k\)-subscheme can be tested after \(k\subset\bar k\): the open condition descends by its underlying inverse image, and its closed equations descend by faithful scalar extension. Thus every \(g\in G(k)\) has its coset in exactly one of the \(k\)-defined strata. The inverse of (6.1) gives \(u\in U_{\Psi_w}(k)\), and the full torsor relation gives \((un_w)^{-1}g\in B(k)\). Conversely (6.2) places every such product in that stratum. Since \(U_{\Psi_w}\subset B\), these are exactly \(B(k)n_wB(k)\). This proves the point decomposition for every field.

By (5.1), \(U_{\Psi_w}\) is affine space of dimension \(|\Psi_w|\). The right \(B\)-action in (6.2) is translation on its \(B\)-factor, so its fppf quotient is exactly that affine space, and its dimension is \(\dim B+|\Psi_w|\). The closures of the \(B\)-orbits in \(X\), and of the \(B\times B\)-orbits in \(G\), are invariant under those actions: translation preserves the orbit and hence its closure. Each closure is therefore a union of the finitely many orbits already identified. This gives the frontier property of the stated stratification as well. \(\square\)

The disjoint-union notation in (6.3) describes strata and points. It does not assert that the cells are open-and-closed components, or that a morphism from a general scheme lands in one cell globally. In \(\operatorname{SL}_2\) the two cells have dimensions two and three, and the latter is open dense.

7. Central quotients and their root coordinates

Theorem 7.1. Let $G$ be reductive over $S$ and $H\subset Z(G)$ a closed subgroup of multiplicative type of finite presentation. Its affine reductive quotient $G/H$, constructed in the preceding lesson, Theorem 1.1, commutes with every base change. For a split maximal torus $T$ its maximal torus is $T/H$, and its root groups are the images of those of $G$.

Proof. RG03 Theorem 1.1 supplies the affine reductive quotient \(q:G\to Q\), its full \(H\)-torsor identity, finite presentation and arbitrary base change. We prove the additional torus and root-group assertions as scheme statements. By RG02 Theorem 2.2, \(C_G(T)=T\). Centrality therefore puts \(H\) inside \(T\) on every test scheme.

Work on an étale affine cover on which \(T=D_R(X)\) and \(H=D_R(M)\). This simultaneous cover exists by the complete relative character theorem in AG-GS, Diagonalizable groups, Theorem 7.7. On a further open neighbourhood the homomorphism is given by one map \(\rho:X\to M\): choose the images of finitely many generators of \(X\), using the group-like coefficient calculation of Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 2.2 there. The closed embedding gives a surjection \(R[X]\to R[M]\). Its image has precisely the exponent basis in \(\rho(X)\), so for \(R\ne0\) linear independence forces \(\rho(X)=M\). The empty affine chart has nothing to prove. Put \(X'=\ker\rho\).

The subgroup \(X'\) is a finite-rank free abelian group. Here is the integer argument: project a subgroup of \(\mathbf Z^r\) onto the first coordinate. If its image is \(d\mathbf Z\ne0\), choose an element with first coordinate \(d\); subtracting its multiples splits the subgroup as its cyclic span plus a subgroup of \(\mathbf Z^{r-1}\). If the image is zero, use only that smaller subgroup. Induction proves freeness and finite generation, without requiring \(X'\) to be saturated. Thus \(T'=D_R(X')\) is a split torus.

The \(H\)-grading on \(R[X]\) has degree \(m\) consisting of the exponents with \(\rho(x)=m\); its degree-zero algebra is \(R[X']\). Choose, for each \(m\), one lift \(x_m\in X\). Then its degree-\(m\) component is the free rank-one \(R[X']\)-module \(e^{x_m}R[X']\), and multiplication between components is an isomorphism. Consequently \(R[X]\) is faithfully flat over \(R[X']\). The full torsor algebra map is

$$ R[X]\otimes_{R[X']}R[X]\longrightarrow R[X]\otimes_RR[M], \qquad a\otimes c_m\longmapsto ac_m\otimes e^m. $$

Its inverse on the exponent basis sends \(a\otimes e^m\) to \(ae^{-x_m}\otimes e^{x_m}\). These formulas are mutually inverse; the forward map is an algebra map, so its bijective inverse is an algebra map too, independently of the lifts. The morphism \(T\to T'\) is of finite presentation: both are finite Laurent polynomial algebras over \(R\); add their finitely many defining Laurent relations and the images of the finitely many source generators to a relative presentation. It is therefore an fppf \(H\)-torsor and represents \(T/H\).

Write \(A=\Gamma(G,\mathcal O_G)\). The closed inclusion \(T\subset G\) gives a surjective equivariant algebra map \(A\to R[X]\). Exactness of taking degree zero, proved in AG-GS Theorem 3.1, gives a surjection

$$ A^H\longrightarrow R[X']. $$

It embeds \(T'\) as a closed subgroup of \(Q=\operatorname{Spec}A^H\). The resulting map \(T\to G\times_QT'\) is a morphism of \(H\)-torsors over \(T'\) and is an isomorphism. Indeed, after an fppf cover with a section of the first torsor, equivariance translates that section to a section of the second; both torsors become \(H\), and the map is translation by that section. The inverse descends uniquely. This also proves the exact inverse-image assertion \(q^{-1}(T')=T\).

Now work where the root datum is constant and its root lines are trivialized. Section 5 proves the relative open cell \(G_0=U^-\times T\times U^+\). Since \(H\subset T\) is central, its right action changes only the middle factor. This open is saturated for the \(H\)-torsor. S04 Lemma A1.6 descends it to an open \(Q_0\subset Q\). The displayed product torsor \(G_0\to U^-\times T'\times U^+\) has the same quotient functor, so uniqueness gives

$$ Q_0=U^-\times T'\times U^+\subset Q. \tag{7.1} $$

For any root \(\alpha\), choose a positive system containing it. Its Borel is a closed subgroup \(B=T\ltimes U^+\), as proved in Sections 4 and 5. The surjection \(A\to\Gamma(B,\mathcal O_B)\) is \(H\)-equivariant, so exact invariants give a closed subgroup

$$ B'=B/H=T'\ltimes U^+\subset Q. $$

The product identifies this quotient because \(H\) again acts only on \(T\). As for \(T'\), the morphism of \(H\)-torsors \(B\to G\times_QB'\) is an isomorphism, so \(q^{-1}(B')=B\). In its scheme coordinates \(T'\times\prod_{\beta>0}U_\beta\), the map from \(U_\alpha\) sets the torus coordinate to the identity and every other root coordinate to zero. It is a closed immersion into \(B'\), hence into \(Q\). Its inverse image under \(q\) is exactly \(H\times U_\alpha\) in the corresponding \(T\times U^+\) coordinates, by the same torsor identity. In particular \(H\cap U_\alpha=1\), including on nonreduced test schemes.

Centrality makes the restriction of \(\alpha\) to \(H\) trivial: in a local root frame, conjugation multiplies the root coordinate by that restricted character, whereas centrality makes this multiplier one on the universal \(H\)-algebra. Hence \(\alpha\in X'\). The image root group has exactly that character for conjugation by \(T'\). This can be checked after the faithfully flat cover \(T\to T'\), where it is the original identity. None of these characters becomes zero, since \(X'\hookrightarrow X\) is injective.

At the identity, (7.1) now gives the whole Lie decomposition

$$ \operatorname{Lie}(Q)=\operatorname{Lie}(T') \oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak g_\alpha. $$

All the displayed root summands retain their one-dimensional nonzero \(T'\)-characters. Thus the invariant tangent submodule is precisely \(\operatorname{Lie}(T')\), using the full character grading, rather than character differentials. On a geometric fibre, any torus \(A'\) containing \(T'\) commutes with it, so \(\operatorname{Lie}(A')\) injects into this invariant part. The inclusion of \(T'\) gives the reverse inclusion of tangent spaces, so its differential is an isomorphism. S02 Lemma 3.A and the étale-monomorphism proof in S04 Lemma E7.2 make \(T'\to A'\) an open immersion. An open subgroup is also closed: every other coset is open, so their union is its open complement. Connectedness of \(A'\) therefore makes its nonempty subgroup all of \(A'\). This proves that \(T'\) is maximal on every geometric fibre, hence is a maximal \(S\)-torus.

There are no additional roots in \(Q\), because the displayed Lie decomposition is exhaustive. Apply the uniqueness assertion of RG03 Theorem 4.1 to \(Q\): each closed smooth image just constructed has the required character and identity differential on its root line, so it is exactly the canonical root group of \(Q\). This proves the assertion about all root groups.

All character algebras, grading projectors, torsor identities, product maps and coordinate-zero immersions above commute with arbitrary base change. On overlaps the maps are the original quotient map restricted to \(T\) and to each canonical root group, so their identifications agree. The complete morphism descent proof in S04 Lemma A1.5 glues them. This proves the theorem over the original base without a smoothness assumption on \(H\). \(\square\)

8. Splittings and subgroup constructions in families

Over a scheme, a split maximal torus need not trivialize its root lines. A reductive group is split with specified root datum here when its maximal torus is identified with \(D_S(X)\), its datum is the specified constant datum, and its root lines are trivializable. A pinning chooses a nowhere-vanishing section of each simple root line. These are the objects used in the next lesson.

Every reductive group acquires such data étale locally. RG02 Theorem 2.3 gives a maximal torus. A torus has an étale splitting cover by its definition in RG01; the equivalent fpqc definition is justified by the complete relative character theorem, AG-GS Theorem 7.7. Work on an affine member with \(T=D_R(X)\). The coefficient grading in RG01 gives

$$ \mathfrak g=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\ne0}\mathfrak g_\alpha $$

with only finitely many nonzero weight modules locally. Each is a finite projective direct summand. Its rank is locally constant: on a local basis chart it is a fixed free rank, by S01 Lemma 1.2. After restricting around any chosen point the finite weight list and all its ranks are therefore fixed. RG03 Theorem 4.1 identifies its nonzero summands as root lines. The root-system assertion and the chamber construction of Sections 2 and 3 give the finite constant root system and a positive system.

The coroots are homomorphisms \(\mathbf G_m\to D_R(X)\). By the same group-like coefficient proof in AG-GS Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 2.2, their finitely many character images are fixed integers on a further open neighbourhood. There are only finitely many coroots, so one common neighbourhood makes the whole datum constant. Trivialize all the finitely many root lines on a further common open cover. Choose the simple frames there; these are the pinning. Choosing all lines also verifies the stated split condition before using any later pinned classification theorem. This argument uses finite projective modules and universal characters over \(R\), including its nilpotents.

The full product proof in Section 5 then applies. Its local inverses agree on overlaps by uniqueness, so without any choice of frames the product of positive root line bundles is \(U^+\), the supplied positive Borel is \(T\ltimes U^+\), and (5.2) is an open immersion. Choosing frames identifies these line bundles with affine coordinates.

For an arbitrary cocharacter \(\lambda\), RG01 Lemma 3.3 gives smooth closed subgroups \(P_G(\lambda)\), \(L_G(\lambda)\) and \(U_G(\lambda)\), their exact Lie weight decompositions, and the limit homomorphism. Its section is the inclusion of \(L_G(\lambda)\); on all test schemes its kernel is \(U_G(\lambda)\). The explicit mutually inverse maps

$$ L_G(\lambda)\times U_G(\lambda)\longrightarrow P_G(\lambda), \qquad(l,u)\longmapsto lu, $$ $$ g\longmapsto\bigl(l(g),\,l(g)^{-1}g\bigr) $$

give \(P_G(\lambda)=L_G(\lambda)\ltimes U_G(\lambda)\). Here \(L_G(\lambda)\) is the fixed subgroup for conjugation by \(\lambda\). On a geometric fibre, a nonzero cocharacter has torus image: in integer coordinates divide its vector by its greatest common divisor; the resulting primitive vector is a closed one-dimensional subtorus and the map onto it is a finite free power map. The zero cocharacter has trivial image. RG02 Theorem 2.1 makes the centralizer of this torus image connected reductive. Thus all geometric fibres of the already smooth affine finite-presentation \(L_G(\lambda)\) are connected reductive, proving that it is reductive over the base. Its roots have zero pairing; those of \(P_G(\lambda)\) have nonnegative pairing and those of \(U_G(\lambda)\) positive pairing, directly from the proved Lie grading. Relative parabolic quotients will be constructed in the last lesson.

For the quotient of Theorem 7.1, the lattice formula is \(X'=\ker(X\to X^*(H))\). Its root characters lie in \(X'\), as the universal centrality calculation in that proof shows. The quotient of the rank-one homomorphism in RG03 Theorem 7.1 sends the original coroot to \(q_T\circ\alpha^\vee:\mathbf G_m\to T/H\). For every \(\chi\in X'\),

$$ \langle\chi,q_T\circ\alpha^\vee\rangle =\langle\chi,\alpha^\vee\rangle. $$

In particular its pairing with \(\alpha\) is two. Characters change from \(X\) to \(X'\), and cocharacters by this dual map; the root system stays the same. The free subgroup \(X'\) need not be saturated, so central quotients can change the integral lattice even when \(H\) is finite.

These constructions are local on the base and are compatible with base change and the canonical root maps. They make no assertion of one constant lattice on a disconnected base. For example, \(\mathbf G_m\) on one component and \(\mathbf G_m^2\) on another is a reductive group of different torus ranks. Constant-datum assertions apply componentwise or on the covers just constructed.

Bruhat cells over a base

The field theorem also gives actual cells over the integers; the following argument identifies their scheme structures before using their fibres.

Theorem 8.1 (relative Bruhat cells). Let $G/S$ be a pinned split reductive group of constant reduced root datum. Fix its split torus $T$, positive Borel $B$, Weyl group $W$, and the pinning representatives $n_w$. Let $N=|\Phi^+|$, $r=\operatorname{rank}X^*(T)$, and $\Psi_w=\{\alpha\in\Phi^+:w^{-1}\alpha\in-\Phi^+\}$. Multiplication gives locally closed immersions

$$ U_{\Psi_w}\times B\longrightarrow G,\qquad (u,b)\longmapsto un_wb, $$

whose images $C_w$ form a finite, disjoint stratification of $G$. With the root frames and a basis of $X^*(T)$, they have coordinates

$$ C_w\simeq\mathbf A_S^{\ell(w)}\times\mathbf G_{m,S}^{r}\times\mathbf A_S^N. $$

The construction commutes with arbitrary base change, including to nonreduced schemes. For $S=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf Z$, each stratum is a product of a split torus and an affine space over the integers. The assertion is a stratification of schemes; it does not identify $G$ with their coproduct as schemes or partition $G(R)$ for an arbitrary ring $R$.

Proof. The ordered root-product theorem of Section 5, applied relatively as above, identifies $U_{\Psi_w}$ with the product of its root lines. The set $\Psi_w$ is closed under addition of roots. Conjugation by $n_w^{-1}$ identifies it with a closed root-product subscheme of $U^-$: complete its root ordering by the remaining negative roots, and set those remaining coordinates to zero. This is a closed immersion over $S$, since the ordered product identifies the whole $U^-$ with a product of root lines. After left translation by $n_w^{-1}$, the displayed map is the composite

$$ n_w^{-1}U_{\Psi_w}n_w\times B\ \hookrightarrow\ U^-\times B\ \hookrightarrow\ G. $$

The second map is the relative open-cell immersion already proved. Thus the original map is a locally closed immersion, with precisely the stated product as its source and image. This argument is over the base, rather than an inference of an immersion from geometric fibres. Inversion applied to the cell indexed by $w^{-1}$ also gives the opposite-order coordinates $B\times U_{\Psi_{w^{-1}}}\to C_w$, $(b,u)\mapsto bn_wu$. A different pinning representative of the same Weyl element differs by an element of $T$ and only changes the torus coordinate. Thus one can equally use $U^+\times T\times U_{\Psi_{w^{-1}}}$. The inversion convention in this order is $\Psi_{w^{-1}}=\{\alpha>0:w\alpha<0\}$.

On each geometric fibre, Theorem 6.2 identifies these images with the Bruhat cells. Hence their underlying sets cover $G$ and are pairwise disjoint: every point lies over a point of $S$, and a geometric point above it tests both assertions. The length formula of Section 3 gives $|\Psi_w|=\ell(w)$. Finally $B=T\ltimes U^+$ as a group and $T\times U^+$ as a scheme, with $U^+\simeq\mathbf A_S^N$ after choosing the root frames. These identifications prove the coordinate formula. All maps, coordinate-zero immersions and representatives base change, proving the last assertion. $\square$

For a finite field $\mathbf F_q$ this gives the useful count

$$ |G(\mathbf F_q)|=(q-1)^r q^N\sum_{w\in W}q^{\ell(w)}. $$

For a torification of the integral scheme, partition each affine coordinate by zero versus invertible. On a cell with $d=N+\ell(w)$ affine coordinates, a subset $I$ of invertible coordinates gives a locally closed torus $\mathbf G_m^{r+|I|}$, with every other coordinate zero. These finitely many pieces partition geometric points and points over every field. They do not make each point over a ring belong to exactly one such piece. Thus the construction supplies the relative cells and counting hypotheses used in the lessons on finite-field counting and torified varieties without a claim about general ring-valued Bruhat double cosets.

A global equation for the longest cell

The longest cell is already open. An integral equation for this open will also control denominators in its coordinates.

Proposition 8.2 (the normalized longest-cell equation). Let \(G/\mathbb Z\) be a pinned split reductive group with constant root datum and split torus \(T=D_{\mathbb Z}(X)\). Fix positive roots \(\Phi^+\), put \(s=|\Phi^+|\), and choose integral root frames \(E_\alpha=dx_\alpha(1)\) for all roots. Write \[ \mathfrak g=\mathfrak t\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathbb ZE_\alpha, \qquad V=\bigwedge^s\mathfrak g, \qquad \eta=\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^+}\alpha\in X. \] Let \(v_+\) and \(v_-\) be the ordered wedges of the positive and negative root frames. Choose a basis of \(\mathfrak t\) and let \(\varphi_-:V\to\mathbb Z\) extract the \(v_-\)-coefficient in the resulting wedge basis. If \(n_0\) represents the longest Weyl element, write \[ (\bigwedge^s\operatorname{Ad})(n_0)v_+=\epsilon v_-, \qquad\epsilon\in\{\pm1\}. \] Then the regular function \[ d(g)=\epsilon^{-1}\varphi_-\bigl((\bigwedge^s\operatorname{Ad})(g)v_+\bigr) \] satisfies \(d(n_0)=1\), and the opposite-order longest-cell map \[ \theta:\mathbb A^{\Phi^+}\times T\times\mathbb A^{\Phi^+}\longrightarrow G, \qquad(a,t,b)\longmapsto\psi(a)t n_0\psi(b) \] is an isomorphism onto \(D(d)\). This holds for every reduced root datum, including rootless tori and every isogeny type.

Proof. AG-RG-03 Theorem 4.1 supplies the root line bundles and their canonical parametrizations; the split integral frames give the displayed decomposition. Its Theorem 7.1 supplies the linked negative frame, the rank-one homomorphism and its Weyl representative. For each simple root, the image of $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is the integral element $n_\alpha$ of (2.1), and (2.2) gives its reflection action on $T$. Choose one word in simple reflections for each $w$, as allowed by Section 3, and multiply the corresponding $n_\alpha$. This produces $n_w\in N_G(T)(\mathbf Z)$ with the required action. Conjugation carries each integral root line isomorphically to the reflected line. Its coefficient relative to two $\mathbf Z$-frames has an integral inverse, so is $1$ or $-1$. These facts prove the signed-wedge assertion used below. Choose an integral cocharacter $\lambda$ strictly positive on $\Phi^+$. There are exactly $s$ positive-weight basis vectors of $\mathfrak g$, all remaining vectors having weight zero or negative. Consequently the unique maximal-weight line of $V$ is $\mathbf Zv_+$, and its unique minimal-weight line is $\mathbf Zv_-$. Their torus characters are $\eta$ and $-\eta$.

For a representation \(R\) on a finite free torus-graded module \(M\) and a vector \(m\) of weight \(\nu\), write \[ R(x_\alpha(z))m=m+\sum_{j>0}z^j m_j. \] Conjugation by the universal torus and comparison of Laurent characters give \(m_j\in M_{\nu+j\alpha}\). This comparison is an integral polynomial identity; it does not differentiate characters or divide by a prime. Positive root groups therefore fix \(v_+\), and preserve the coefficient extracted by \(\varphi_-\). By the ordered root-product theorem, for \(u\in U\) these identities give \[ R(u)v_+=v_+, \qquad\varphi_-\circ R(u)=\varphi_-. \]

Take \(R=\bigwedge^s\operatorname{Ad}\). Integral normalizer representatives permute the integral root lines by units, so \(R(n_w)v_+\) is a signed wedge indexed by \(w\Phi^+\). Its \(v_-\)-coefficient is zero unless \(w\Phi^+=\Phi^-\), which is precisely \(w=w_0\). In the opposite-order coordinates of AG-RG-04 Theorem 8.1, the preceding identities thus show \[ d(utn_wu')=0\quad(w\ne w_0), \qquad d(utn_0u')=\eta(t)^{-1}. \] These are identities on the coordinate schemes, and remain true after every base change. The last expression is a unit and equals one at \(n_0\).

The relative open-cell immersion of AG-RG-04 (5.2), translated and inverted as in Theorem 8.1, makes \(\theta\) an open immersion. Its image is the longest Bruhat stratum. The strata cover all scheme points, so the computed restrictions show that this image and \(D(d)\) have exactly the same underlying open set. Open subschemes carry the restricted structure sheaf and are determined by that set. They are therefore equal as schemes, including after nonreduced base changes.

If \(\Phi\) is empty, set \(V=\bigwedge^0\mathfrak g=\mathbb Z\) and \(d=1\). Then \(G=T\), and the assertion remains true. \(\square\)

The sum \(\eta\) lies in the root lattice, hence in \(X\), for every isogeny type. The proof requires neither individual fundamental weights in \(X\), a perfect Killing form, nor a factorial coordinate ring or trivial Picard group. Orthogonal examples are understood as the smooth split reductive models of the stated root data; reductivity of every geometric fibre is among the hypotheses.

For \(\operatorname{PGL}_2\), take the class of \(n_0=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). Pull back the function to \(\operatorname{GL}_2\) and write \(g=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&e\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), \(\delta=ae-bc\). Then \[ gE_{12}g^{-1}=\delta^{-1} \begin{pmatrix}-ac&a^2\\-c^2&ac\end{pmatrix}, \qquad d\big|_{\operatorname{GL}_2}=c^2/\delta. \] Here \(\epsilon=-1\). The formula is scalar invariant and is the pullback of the already constructed regular function on \(\operatorname{PGL}_2\). Its value at \(n_0\) is one. The boundary can have multiplicity two in this equation; a principal open does not require a reduced boundary equation. This calculation is a pullback formula: it does not assert that every \(\operatorname{PGL}_2(A)\)-point has a \(\operatorname{GL}_2(A)\)-lift.

9. Exercises and solutions

Exercise 9.1 (easy). Write the complete root datum of $\operatorname{GL}_n$ and identify its central rational direction.

Solution. The lattices and roots are (2.3), with $\langle e_i,e_j^\vee\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. Reflection in $e_i-e_j$ exchanges coordinates $i,j$. The root span is $\{(a_i):\sum a_i=0\}$, while its orthogonal central direction is $\mathbf Q(1,\ldots,1)$. The centre has character group $\mathbf Z^n/\mathbf Z\Phi\simeq\mathbf Z$, the quotient map being summation. For $n=1$ the root set is empty and the entire lattice is central.

Exercise 9.2 (medium). Verify integrally that the data of $\operatorname{SL}_2$ and $\operatorname{PGL}_2$ are dual. Explain why identifying their rational root systems does not identify their groups.

Solution. In the coordinates of Section 2, the pairs (positive root, positive coroot) are $(2,1)$ and $(1,2)$ in dual copies of $\mathbf Z$. Interchanging lattices interchanges these pairs. The reflection is multiplication by $-1$ in both. The quotient of the character lattice by the root lattice is $\mathbf Z/2$ for the first and zero for the second, so their centres are $\mu_2$ and the trivial group. This distinction holds scheme-theoretically in characteristic two.

Exercise 9.3 (medium). Show that the Weyl group of $\operatorname{GL}_n$ is $S_n$, generated by its root reflections.

Solution. Formula (1.1) for $e_i-e_j$ sends $e_i$ to $e_j$, $e_j$ to $e_i$, and fixes the other basis vectors. Thus its reflection is the transposition $(ij)$. Transpositions generate every permutation, by breaking each cycle into transpositions. Conversely all products of these reflections are coordinate permutations. The lattice representation is faithful, so the reflection group is exactly $S_n$; Theorem 4.1 identifies it with the normalizer quotient.

Exercise 9.4 (hard). Prove $\operatorname{GL}_n(k)=\coprod_{w\in S_n}B(k)wB(k)$ by upper triangular row and column operations, where $B$ is upper triangular. Prove uniqueness of the permutation.

Solution. In the bottom row choose its leftmost nonzero entry, in column $j$. Scale that row to make the entry one. Add multiples of column $j$ to columns to its right, making the other bottom-row entries zero; these are permitted right upper triangular operations. Entries to its left were already zero. Add multiples of the bottom row to higher rows to clear column $j$; these are permitted left upper triangular operations. Delete the bottom row and column $j$. The remaining square matrix is invertible, by expansion of the determinant along the cleared row. Repeat with its bottom row, retaining the original order of the surviving columns. Operations on these rows and columns extend to upper triangular operations on the original matrix and preserve every previously cleared pivot. Induction gives one nonzero pivot in every row and column, all scaled to one, hence a permutation matrix. This proves existence over every field.

For uniqueness consider, for every $i,j$, the rank $r_{ij}$ of the submatrix in rows $i,\ldots,n$ and columns $1,\ldots,j$. Left upper triangular operations mix these bottom rows only with one another, and right upper triangular operations mix these first columns only with one another. Hence all $r_{ij}$ are invariant. In a permutation matrix they count pivots in that rectangle. The difference

$$ r_{ij}-r_{i+1,j}-r_{i,j-1}+r_{i+1,j-1} $$

is one exactly at a pivot $(i,j)$ and zero otherwise, with empty boundary rectangles having rank zero. These invariants recover the permutation uniquely. The double cosets are therefore disjoint.

Exercise 9.5 (families). Let $L$ be a nontrivial line bundle. For $G=\operatorname{SL}(\mathcal O\oplus L)$, describe the positive unipotent radical and decide whether the displayed diagonal torus has a global pinning.

Solution. Choose the upper root as positive. Its group is the additive line bundle $\mathbf V(L^{-1})$, and the triangular subgroup is $T\ltimes\mathbf V(L^{-1})$. A pinning would choose a nowhere-vanishing section of $L^{-1}$ and therefore trivialize $L$. Hence this pair has no global pinning, although it has one on every open set trivializing $L$. Its relative big cell is $\mathbf V(L)\times T\times\mathbf V(L^{-1})$.
