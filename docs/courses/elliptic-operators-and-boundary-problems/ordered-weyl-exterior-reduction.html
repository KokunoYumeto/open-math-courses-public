From ordered Weyl errors to the exterior index form

Written and dedicated to the public domain by Codex, September 2026 (CC0).

The scaled index construction leaves one finite expression: the part of two ordered error powers that contains exactly 2n2n phase derivatives. This lesson evaluates that expression directly. Independent coordinate scales force one derivative in every phase coordinate. Alternation then cancels every term that differentiates one atomic factor twice. What remains is the first Poisson bracket in each selected error factor, and its exact alternating count is the exterior form Tr⁡[(db∧da)n]\operatorname{Tr}[(db\wedge da)^n].

Read Scaled Weyl parametrices and the surviving differential degree for the finite coefficient and its trace remainder, When a moving symbol scale controls an operator for norm-continuous symbol paths, and Relative Weyl projectors and the Chern cutoff form for the matrix exterior identities and cutoff geometry. We prove the coordinate-scaling step here, so the calculation does not use the later source-theorem evaluation of the index.

Scaling selects the one-derivative multi-degree. Alternation supplies the cancellation, and the exterior count fixes the coefficient. Equations (1)–(18) prove every reduction in the figure with the original ordered errors and full coefficients. The reproducible source is ordered_weyl_exterior_reduction.py.

1. The finite coefficient for every error power

Let

gz=(1+|z|2)−1|dz|2,a∈S(1,g;End⁡ℂν),z=(x,ξ)∈ℝ2n, g_z=(1+|z|^2)^{-1}|dz|^2, \qquad a\in S(1,g;\operatorname{End}\mathbb C^\nu), \qquad z=(x,\xi)\in\mathbb R^{2n},

and suppose that a(z)a(z) has a uniformly bounded inverse outside a compact set. Choose a smooth scalar cutoff ψ\psi supported in the invertibility region and equal to one outside a larger compact set. Put

b=ψa−1,t=1−ψ, b=\psi a^{-1},\qquad t=1-\psi,

and retain the two original matrix orders

r1=I−b#a,r2=I−a#b.(1) r_1=I-b\# a,\qquad r_2=I-a\# b. \tag{1}

The matrix products stay in their written order. The two error symbols belong to S(h,g)S(h,g), where h(z)=(1+|z|2)−1h(z)=(1+|z|^2)^{-1}. For each integer N>nN>n, their NN-fold Weyl powers have integrable order hNh^N, and the trace-power index identity is

ind⁡aw=Tr⁡((r1#N)w−(r2#N)w).(2) \operatorname{ind}a^w =\operatorname{Tr}\bigl((r_1^{\#N})^w-(r_2^{\#N})^w\bigr). \tag{2}

To recall why the finite coefficient exists, scale both phase variables by ε\varepsilon. Expanding each ordered product only through degree nn gives matrix coefficients Fj,k(N)F_{j,k}^{(N)}, where degree kk means 2k2k actual phase differentiations. The omitted symbol lies uniformly in S(hεn+1,gε)S(h_\varepsilon^{n+1},g_\varepsilon); its full trace norm is O(ε2)O(\varepsilon^2) by IP22–IP28, with both original factors and their output domains. The original phase-space integral has the same order:

∫ℝ2nhεn+1(z)dz=O(ε2). \int_{\mathbb R^{2n}}h_\varepsilon^{n+1}(z)\,dz =O(\varepsilon^2).

For k≤nk\leq n, every retained word contains NN error factors. If all factors had positive intrinsic degree, their total degree would be at least N>n≥kN>n\geq k. Thus one factor is the compactly supported scalar tIνtI_\nu, and every Fj,k(N)F_{j,k}^{(N)} is compactly supported. Define

Ak(N)=(2π)−n∫ℝ2ntr⁡(F1,k(N)−F2,k(N))dz(3) A_k^{(N)}=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb R^{2n}} \operatorname{tr}\bigl(F_{1,k}^{(N)}-F_{2,k}^{(N)}\bigr)\,dz \tag{3}

The exact scaled trace expansion is

ind⁡aw=∑k=0nAk(N)ε2k−2n+O(ε2). \operatorname{ind}a^w =\sum_{k=0}^{n}A_k^{(N)}\varepsilon^{2k-2n} +O(\varepsilon^2).

Its left side is independent of ε>0\varepsilon>0. Starting with the smallest nonzero negative power and multiplying by the power that makes it constant shows successively that every coefficient below nn vanishes. Letting ε\varepsilon tend to zero then gives

A0(N)=⋯=An−1(N)=0,ind⁡aw=An(N).(4) A_0^{(N)}=\cdots=A_{n-1}^{(N)}=0, \qquad \operatorname{ind}a^w=A_n^{(N)}. \tag{4}

The rest of the lesson evaluates this same An(N)A_n^{(N)} for arbitrary N>nN>n.

2. Independent scales select one derivative per coordinate

Expand the degree-nn expression in (3) down to the original factors a,b,ta,b,t. Every word contains exactly 2n2n differentiations. Group the words by the multi-degree

α=(α1,…,α2n)∈ℕ2n,|α|=2n, \alpha=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{2n})\in\mathbb N^{2n}, \qquad |\alpha|=2n,

where αj\alpha_j counts the differentiations with respect to zjz_j. Let IαI_\alpha be the complete integrated group with that multi-degree, including every matrix order and numerical factor.

For positive numbers ρ1,…,ρ2n\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_{2n}, set

Dρz=(ρ1z1,…,ρ2nz2n),aρ=a∘Dρ,bρ=b∘Dρ,tρ=t∘Dρ. D_\rho z=(\rho_1z_1,\ldots,\rho_{2n}z_{2n}), \qquad a_\rho=a\circ D_\rho,\qquad b_\rho=b\circ D_\rho,\qquad t_\rho=t\circ D_\rho.

The transformed symbol remains in S(1,g)S(1,g) and remains uniformly invertible off a compact set. Join DρD_\rho to the identity by a positive diagonal path. The chain rule gives uniform symbol bounds along the path, so the corresponding Weyl operators vary continuously in operator norm. Each is Fredholm by the construction in Section 1; therefore its index is constant along the path.

For a word of multi-degree α\alpha, the chain rule contributes ρα\rho^\alpha, while the change of variables w=Dρzw=D_\rho z contributes ∏jρj−1\prod_j\rho_j^{-1}. Equations (3)–(4) therefore give the finite Laurent identity

ind⁡aw=(2π)−n∑|α|=2nIα∏j=12nρjαj−1(ρj>0).(5) \operatorname{ind}a^w =(2\pi)^{-n}\sum_{|\alpha|=2n} I_\alpha\prod_{j=1}^{2n}\rho_j^{\alpha_j-1} \qquad(\rho_j>0). \tag{5}

Distinct Laurent monomials are linearly independent. For an explicit proof, choose an integer vector vv that separates the finitely many exponents α−𝟏\alpha-\mathbf1, and put ρj=esvj\rho_j=e^{s v_j}. Equation (5) becomes a finite exponential polynomial that is constant for every real ss. Its distinct exponential coefficients vanish. Hence

Iα=0(α≠𝟏),ind⁡aw=(2π)−nI𝟏,𝟏=(1,…,1).(6) I_\alpha=0\quad(\alpha\ne\mathbf1), \qquad \operatorname{ind}a^w=(2\pi)^{-n}I_{\mathbf1}, \quad \mathbf1=(1,\ldots,1). \tag{6}

This is a statement about each complete multi-degree group. Individual words inside a group can still cancel one another.

The same result gives the reflection sign without an exterior formula. Replacing one coordinate zjz_j by −zj-z_j changes the sign of every surviving word, because that word has exactly one zjz_j-derivative. The absolute Jacobian is one. Thus

ind⁡(a∘Rj)w=−ind⁡aw.(7) \operatorname{ind}(a\circ R_j)^w=-\operatorname{ind}a^w. \tag{7}

An even coordinate permutation lies in the positive-determinant component and preserves the index. An odd permutation is a positive-determinant map followed by one reflection. Therefore

ind⁡(a∘Pτ)w=sgn⁡(τ)ind⁡aw.(8) \operatorname{ind}(a\circ P_\tau)^w =\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\operatorname{ind}a^w. \tag{8}

No boundary index theorem has entered this argument.

3. Alternation creates a sign-reversing involution

Let S1S^1 be the full matrix differential expression that contributes to I𝟏I_{\mathbf1}. Every coordinate appears exactly once. If Sτ1S^1_\tau denotes the expression obtained by permuting all differentiation labels by τ∈S2n\tau\in S_{2n}, equations (6) and (8), followed by the change of variables back to zz, give

ind⁡aw=(2π)−n(2n)!∫ℝ2ntr⁡S2(z)dz,S2=∑τ∈S2nsgn⁡(τ)Sτ1.(9) \operatorname{ind}a^w ={(2\pi)^{-n}\over(2n)!} \int_{\mathbb R^{2n}}\operatorname{tr}S^2(z)\,dz, \qquad S^2=\sum_{\tau\in S_{2n}} \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)S^1_\tau. \tag{9}

Fully expand a word and regard its 2n2n differentiations as labeled slots assigned to occurrences of the atomic factors a,b,ta,b,t. Suppose two slots act on one atomic occurrence. Swap the two coordinate labels assigned to those slots. Mixed partial derivatives commute, so the matrix word does not change, while the permutation sign reverses. This fixed-point-free pairing proves

every word in which one atomic factor is differentiated twice vanishes from S2.(10) \text{every word in which one atomic factor is differentiated twice vanishes from }S^2. \tag{10}

The argument removes more than visibly repeated derivatives in one two-factor product. The finite differential coefficients of an NN-fold Weyl product are generated by

exp(12i∑1≤r<s≤N∑u,v=12nJuv∂zr,u∂zs,v)∏r=1Nfr(zr)|z1=⋯=zN=z,(11) \left. \exp\!\left({1\over2i} \sum_{1\le r<s\le N}\sum_{u,v=1}^{2n} J^{uv}\partial_{z_{r,u}}\partial_{z_{s,v}} \right) \prod_{r=1}^{N}f_r(z_r) \right|_{z_1=\cdots=z_N=z}, \tag{11}

read only through the finite degree in question. The constant Poisson tensor is JuvJ^{uv}. Derivatives act entrywise, and the matrix product remains in the displayed factor order. Iterating the two-factor Weyl formula proves (11); its constant-coefficient contraction operators commute, so the result is independent of parenthesization.

An internal coefficient Ck(b,a)C_k(b,a) or Ck(a,b)C_k(a,b) with k≥2k\geq2 differentiates both atomic factors more than once and disappears by (10). An internal C1C_1 differentiates its two atomic factors once each. Any later contraction that reaches either factor differentiates it again and also disappears.

Only contractions joining scalar tt-occurrences could remain. Under (10), their contraction graph is a matching, because every vertex has degree at most one. Each edge contains

{t,t}=∑j=1n(tξjtxj−txjtξj)=0. \{t,t\}=\sum_{j=1}^{n} (t_{\xi_j}t_{x_j}-t_{x_j}t_{\xi_j})=0.

The scalar tt commutes with all matrix factors, so this zero factor can be gathered without changing an order. Every outer contraction vanishes. This proves that alternation keeps only the undifferentiated product of first internal Weyl brackets and scalar tt-factors.

4. The first brackets are the only survivors

Use the fixed convention

{b,a}=∑j=1n(bξjaxj−bxjaξj),C1(b,a)=12i{b,a}=−i2{b,a}. \{b,a\}=\sum_{j=1}^{n} (b_{\xi_j}a_{x_j}-b_{x_j}a_{\xi_j}), \qquad C_1(b,a)={1\over2i}\{b,a\}=-{i\over2}\{b,a\}.

Inside the alternating differential-degree-2n2n projection, the two errors in (1) may therefore be replaced by

r1=tIν+i2{b,a},r2=tIν+i2{a,b},(12) r_1=tI_\nu+{i\over2}\{b,a\}, \qquad r_2=tI_\nu+{i\over2}\{a,b\}, \tag{12}

Each bracket contributes two atomic differentiations. Exactly nn of the NN error factors must supply a bracket, and the remaining N−nN-n factors supply tIνtI_\nu. Since tt is scalar, all choices of the nn bracket positions give the same ordered matrix power. Their complete contribution is

(Nn)tN−n(i2)n({b,a}n−{a,b}n).(13) \binom Nn t^{N-n}\left({i\over2}\right)^n \bigl(\{b,a\}^n-\{a,b\}^n\bigr). \tag{13}

The two matrix brackets have not been replaced by negatives of one another. That identity is generally false before the matrix trace.

5. Counting the exterior word and fixing the orientation

First order the coordinates as

Ω̃=dξ1∧dx1∧⋯∧dξn∧dxn. \widetilde\Omega =d\xi_1\wedge dx_1\wedge\cdots\wedge d\xi_n\wedge dx_n.

A term in b,an{b,a}^n that uses each coordinate once must use every canonical pair jj once. There are n!n! ways to order those pairs among the nn matrix factors and 2n2^n ways to choose one of the two bracket terms in every pair. Thus there are exactly

2nn!(14) 2^n n! \tag{14}

base words. The sign of each base word is its coordinate-permutation sign relative to Ω̃\widetilde\Omega: exchanging the two entries of a pair contributes the bracket minus sign, while permuting two blocks of length two is even. For any coordinate word in (db∧da)n(db\wedge da)^n, every base word has one unique relabeling to it, and the relabeling sign times the base sign is the target exterior sign. Hence every exterior word occurs with the common multiplicity 2nn!2^n n!.

Applying this count to (13) gives the untraced identity

S2Ω̃=n!in(Nn)tN−n((db∧da)n−(da∧db)n).(15) S^2\widetilde\Omega =n!\,i^n\binom Nn t^{N-n} \bigl((db\wedge da)^n-(da\wedge db)^n\bigr). \tag{15}

For matrix-valued forms of degrees pp and qq, cyclicity of the finite matrix trace gives

Tr⁡(α∧β)=(−1)pqTr⁡(β∧α). \operatorname{Tr}(\alpha\wedge\beta) =(-1)^{pq}\operatorname{Tr}(\beta\wedge\alpha).

Rotate the first one-form in (db∧da)n(db\wedge da)^n past the other 2n−12n-1 one-forms. The sign is −1-1, so

Tr⁡[(da∧db)n]=−Tr⁡[(db∧da)n]. \operatorname{Tr}[(da\wedge db)^n] =-\operatorname{Tr}[(db\wedge da)^n].

The course orientation is

Ω=dx1∧dξ1∧⋯∧dxn∧dξn,Ω̃=(−1)nΩ. \Omega=dx_1\wedge d\xi_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_n\wedge d\xi_n, \qquad \widetilde\Omega=(-1)^n\Omega.

Insert (15) into (9). Since (−1)nin=i−n(-1)^ni^n=i^{-n}, the direct coefficient is

An(N)=(2π)−n2n!in(2n)!(Nn)∫ℝ2nΩtN−nTr⁡[(db∧da)n](N>n).(16) \boxed{ A_n^{(N)} =(2\pi)^{-n}{2\,n!\over i^n(2n)!}\binom Nn \int_{\mathbb R^{2n}}^{\Omega} t^{N-n}\operatorname{Tr}[(db\wedge da)^n] } \qquad(N>n). \tag{16}

The numerator 2n!2\,n! means twice n!n!; the denominator (2n)!(2n)! is the factorial of 2n2n. Choosing N=2nN=2n gives

(2nn)2n!(2n)!=2n!, \binom{2n}{n}{2\,n!\over(2n)!}={2\over n!},

and therefore

ind⁡aw=An(2n)=(2π)−n2inn!∫ℝ2nΩ(1−ψ)nTr⁡[(db∧da)n].(17) \operatorname{ind}a^w =A_n^{(2n)} =(2\pi)^{-n}{2\over i^n n!} \int_{\mathbb R^{2n}}^{\Omega} (1-\psi)^n\operatorname{Tr}[(db\wedge da)^n]. \tag{17}

This is the exterior index formula derived directly from the two ordered error powers.

6. The beta moment and the boundary coefficient

On the region where aa is invertible, put θ=a−1da\theta=a^{-1}da. Differentiating a−1a=Ia^{-1}a=I gives d(a−1)=−a−1(da)a−1d(a^{-1})=-a^{-1}(da)a^{-1}, so

dθ=−θ2,db∧da=dψ∧θ−ψθ2. d\theta=-\theta^2, \qquad db\wedge da=d\psi\wedge\theta-\psi\theta^2.

Graded cyclicity gives Tr⁡(θ2n)=0\operatorname{Tr}(\theta^{2n})=0, because rotating one degree-one factor through the other 2n−12n-1 factors changes the sign. It also gives

dTr⁡(θ2n−1)=0. d\operatorname{Tr}(\theta^{2n-1})=0.

In the expansion of (dψ∧θ−ψθ2)n(d\psi\wedge\theta-\psi\theta^2)^n, two copies containing dψd\psi multiply to zero. The term without dψd\psi has trace zero, and the nn terms with one copy agree after the trace. Therefore

Tr⁡[(db∧da)n]=n(−1)n−1ψn−1dψ∧Tr⁡(θ2n−1). \operatorname{Tr}[(db\wedge da)^n] =n(-1)^{n-1}\psi^{n-1}d\psi\wedge \operatorname{Tr}(\theta^{2n-1}).

Gn,q(s)=n∫0sun−1(1−u)qdu. G_{n,q}(s)=n\int_0^s u^{n-1}(1-u)^q\,du.

8. Exercises with solutions

Exercise 1. Starting from (16), prove in one line that the choice N=2nN=2n gives the coefficient in (17).

Solution. Use the complete central binomial coefficient:

2n!(2n)!(2nn)=2n!(2n)!(2n)!(n!)2=2n!.(22) {2\,n!\over(2n)!}\binom{2n}{n} ={2\,n!\over(2n)!}{(2n)!\over(n!)^2} ={2\over n!}. \tag{22}

The cutoff power is t2n−n=tnt^{2n-n}=t^n, so every factor in (17) follows.

Exercise 2. Let a fully expanded alternating word have two derivative slots assigned to the same atomic factor. Prove that its complete alternating sum is zero even when the other factors are matrices.

Solution. Pair each coordinate-label permutation with the permutation obtained by transposing the labels in those two slots. The two mixed partial derivatives on the common factor commute. All other derivatives and the order of every matrix factor remain fixed. The transposition reverses the permutation sign, so each pair sums to zero. No matrix commutation is used.

Exercise 3. Why can an outer Weyl contraction between two scalar tt-factors not survive after the repeated-derivative terms have been removed?

Solution. The surviving contraction graph has degree at most one at every scalar occurrence, so it is a matching. Every edge is the Poisson contraction

{t,t}=∑j(tξjtxj−txjtξj)=0.(23) \{t,t\}=\sum_j(t_{\xi_j}t_{x_j}-t_{x_j}t_{\xi_j})=0. \tag{23}

Because tt is scalar, the zero edge factor can be moved next to the remaining factors without changing their matrix order.

Exercise 4. For n=3n=3 and N=6N=6, simplify the scalar coefficient in (16) before the integral.

Solution. Since N=2nN=2n, equation (17) applies. As i3=−ii^3=-i,

(2π)−32i33!=i3(2π)3.(24) (2\pi)^{-3}{2\over i^3\,3!} ={i\over3(2\pi)^3}. \tag{24}

The integral retains the full factor (1−ψ)3Tr⁡[(db∧da)3](1-\psi)^3\operatorname{Tr}[(db\wedge da)^3] and the original six-dimensional orientation.
