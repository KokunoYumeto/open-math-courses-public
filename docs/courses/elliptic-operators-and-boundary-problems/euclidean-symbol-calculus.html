Symbols, operators and Sobolev scales

Frequency differentiation improves a symbol, while differentiation in the base variable may worsen it. The balance between those two effects controls an expansion. Boundedness requires a different argument: a decomposition into frequency bands continues to work when the two effects exactly balance. We develop these mechanisms separately, then combine them to construct inverses and act on Sobolev and Besov spaces.

We use the Fourier conventions proved in Fourier transforms, finite spectra and convex separation and applied in Quadratic Fourier multipliers at a moving scale: the forward kernel is e−ix⋅ξe^{-ix\cdot\xi}, inverse transformation has factor (2π)−n(2\pi)^{-n}, and D=−i∂D=-i\partial. Fourier inversion and Plancherel, including their extension to L2L^2, are explicit prerequisites. The additional entry contracts are completeness of L2L^2, density of Schwartz functions in L2L^2, the usual integration and dominated-convergence theorems for Lebesgue measure, Taylor’s formula and the multivariable product rule. Distributional Fourier transformation, invertible linear pullback on tempered distributions, and the tempered Schwartz kernel theorem are the same explicit contracts as in Section 4 of Two measuring scales, one Weyl product. In particular the kernel theorem means that every continuous linear map 𝒮(ℝn)→𝒮′(ℝn)\mathcal S(\mathbb R^n)\to\mathcal S'(\mathbb R^n) has exactly one kernel in 𝒮′(ℝ2n)\mathcal S'(\mathbb R^{2n}), and conversely. Its full proof is Sections4.1–4.5 of the Weyl-product lesson, including the exact global kernel and strong-dual maps. Sections8.1–8.4 of the Fourier lesson prove partial transforms and the full original distributional and L2 extensions. The Banach and measure lesson supplies completeness and density; the metric and calculus lesson supplies the complete Taylor and product maps.

The multiplier results in Sections 7–8 of Quadratic Fourier multipliers at a moving scale and the classical metric verification in Section 8 of Two measuring scales, one Weyl product are available here. We use those results with specified metrics below. All statements are for a fixed finite dimension. Scalar symbols may be replaced by matrices between fixed finite-dimensional complex Hermitian spaces: norms replace absolute values, products keep their displayed order, and adjoints are conjugate transposes. The inverse theorem uses square matrices and a lower bound for their smallest singular value. No infinite-dimensional operator-valued extension is implicit.

11. Worked examples

For a bounded smooth matrix function B(x)B(x) with bounded derivatives and a constant matrix MM, take a(ξ)=Mξja(\xi)=M\xi_j, b(x,ξ)=B(x)b(x,\xi)=B(x). Formula (E21) terminates because aa is frequency-linear: a∘b=MξjB+MDjB,b∘a=BMξj.(E46) a\circ b=M\xi_jB+M D_jB, \qquad b\circ a=BM\xi_j. \tag{E46} One can also prove termination directly from MDj(Bu)=MBDju+M(DjB)uMD_j(Bu)=MB D_ju+M(D_jB)u; kernel injectivity identifies the symbols. The leading commutator contains (MB−BM)ξj(MB-BM)\xi_j, which has order one. Scalar Poisson-bracket cancellation cannot remove it.

For a scalar example, a(x,ξ)=2+14sin⁡x1+⟨ξ⟩2a(x,\xi)=2+\tfrac14\sin x_1+\langle\xi\rangle^2 is uniformly elliptic of order two. The initial inverse is its ordinary reciprocal, of order minus two, but it is usually not the inverse symbol for composition. The loss of gain at equality is visible in one dimension. Fix 0<r<10<r<1, let Rj=2LjR_j=2^{Lj} for a fixed large integer LL, and put λj=Rjr\lambda_j=R_j^r. 12. Exercises and solutions

Problem 1. Let m2j=−jm_{2j}=-j, m2j+1=3−jm_{2j+1}=3-j, and choose arbitrary aj∈Sρ,δmja_j\in S^{m_j}_{\rho,\delta}. What order is guaranteed for the remainder after the first four terms? Does summing the even terms first preserve the same meaning?

Solution. The remaining orders start with m4=−2m_4=-2, m5=1m_5=1, m6=−3m_6=-3, m7=0m_7=0; their maximum is one. Thus (E6) gives remainder order one after four terms. A genuine permutation of the natural numbers preserves the expansion modulo smoothing. The instruction “all even terms first, then all odd terms” is not a permutation indexed by the natural numbers, since no odd term occurs at a finite position. One may separately sum both subsequences and add the resulting two symbols; each tail still has orders tending to minus infinity, and (E6) verifies that the resulting sum has the original expansion. This is an additional two-sum construction, not a nonexistent enumeration.

Problem 2. In one dimension compute the exact left symbol of D2Me3ixD^2 M_{e^{3ix}}, where MfM_f denotes multiplication by ff. Check the coefficient of the first derivative term.

Problem 3. Show explicitly why the proof of (E28) gives no uniform constant as δ↑1\delta\uparrow1, even when δ≤ρ\delta\leq\rho.

Solution. With fixed Taylor order KK, the error norms in (E32) sum to at most a constant times ∑j≥12−jK(1−δ)=2−K(1−δ)/(1−2−K(1−δ))\sum_{j\geq1}2^{-jK(1-\delta)} =2^{-K(1-\delta)}/(1-2^{-K(1-\delta)}). This is a precise failure of this proof’s uniform bound; it is not a proof that every particular type-(1,1)(1,1) operator is unbounded.

Problem 4. Construct a distribution in B2,∞0B^0_{2,\infty} that does not belong to H0H^0, and explain why Schwartz density must not be used in the B2,∞0B^0_{2,\infty} proof.

Solution. For every j≥1j\geq1, choose a smooth Fourier function fjf_j supported strictly inside AjA_j, normalized so that the inverse Fourier transform has L2L^2 norm one. The distribution with Fourier transform ∑jfj\sum_jf_j is tempered: on each annulus Cauchy–Schwarz bounds its pairing with a Schwartz function by that function’s annular L2L^2 norm, and those norms form a summable rapidly decreasing sequence. Every dyadic L2L^2 norm is one, so its B2,∞0B^0_{2,\infty} norm is one, while its squared H0H^0 norm is ∑j1=∞\sum_j1=\infty. The tails of its finite annular sums all have Besov norm one. For any Schwartz function the annular norms tend to zero, so its distance from this distribution in that norm is at least one. The proof after (E39) instead uses convergence in a strictly weaker Sobolev space and estimates each fixed output band; it remains valid at the sequence endpoint.

Problem 5. Suppose a∈Sρ,δma\in S^m_{\rho,\delta}, δ<ρ\delta<\rho, is square-matrix elliptic, b∈Sρ,δ−mb\in S^{-m}_{\rho,\delta}, and a∘b−Ia\circ b-I is smoothing. Solution. Matrix factors in (E21) do not commute, and the derivatives fall on different factors in the two orders. Even their leading products need not agree. Use (E43) to obtain a uniform inverse bound for the pointwise matrix aa, construct a two-sided parametrix cc by (E44)–(E45), and compute b−c=c∘(a∘b−I)−(c∘a−I)∘bb-c=c\circ(a\circ b-I)-(c\circ a-I)\circ b. Both terms are smoothing because it is a two-sided ideal. Hence b∘a−I=(b−c)∘a+(c∘a−I)b\circ a-I=(b-c)\circ a+(c\circ a-I) is smoothing. Associativity and the ideal property replace commutativity.

Problem 6. Let a(x,ξ)=f(x)g(ξ)a(x,\xi)=f(x)g(\xi), where f∈Cn+1(ℝn)f\in C^{n+1}(\mathbb R^n) and all its derivatives through order n+1n+1 are integrable, while gg is only bounded and measurable. Prove L2L^2 boundedness and state whether (E21) follows.

Further questions

The next structural questions have distinct hypotheses: coordinate changes introduce a lower bound on δ\delta relative to 1−ρ1-\rho; positivity estimates depend on more than boundedness; and boundary kernels require additional structure near the normal-frequency directions. The endpoint example (E47) is a useful test for any proposed extension that claims a decreasing symbolic error at δ=ρ\delta=\rho.
