The Jacquet–Langlands correspondence

Written by GPT-6.1 Sol (OpenAI) in Codex, Ultra setting, October 2026. Self-checked by the writing AI. Public domain (CC0).

A quaternion division algebra has fewer conjugacy classes than a matrix algebra, but it retains exactly the regular elliptic ones. Its representations therefore offer a compact setting in which to study part of the spectrum of \(\mathrm{GL}_2\). We prove the local Jacquet–Langlands correspondence over every nonarchimedean local field and over \(\mathbb R\), with its elliptic character sign, twisted factors, formal degrees and smooth transfer. We then prove the global quaternion correspondence over \(\mathbb Q\) by a trace comparison, including quotient volumes, singular and continuous terms, residual characters and multiplicities. The calculations at levels eleven and thirty-seven connect the correspondence to explicit weight-two eigenforms. Section 6 states the distinct higher-degree extensions and their remaining proof scope.

We use Lesson 17 for quaternion groups and Brandt operators, Lesson 18 for their trace formula, and Lesson 15 for multiplicity one. A discrete series means an irreducible unitary representation square-integrable modulo the centre. Removing the unitary condition requires the term essentially discrete series: a character twist of a discrete series. At a finite place the discrete series of \(\mathrm{GL}_2\) are unitary Steinberg twists and unitary supercuspidals. At a real place they are the discrete series described in Lesson 11.

1. Match classes by their quadratic polynomial

Let \(F\) be a nonarchimedean local field, or \(\mathbb R\), and let \(D\) be the quaternion division algebra over \(F\). Set \(G=\mathrm{GL}_2(F)\), \(G'=D^\times\). An element of \(G\) is regular elliptic when its characteristic polynomial is separable and irreducible over \(F\). For \(b\in D\), use the reduced polynomial \[ P_b(T)=T^2-\operatorname{trd}(b)T+\operatorname{nrd}(b). \tag{1.1} \] Regular means that this polynomial has distinct roots over an algebraic closure. In positive characteristic, irreducibility alone does not ensure separability.

We import two algebraic facts. Every separable quadratic extension of a nonarchimedean local field embeds in its quaternion division algebra, by the quadratic-embedding lemma in Lesson 18, Section 5; it uses the written finite splitting criterion and the local Brauer invariant and degree-two restriction rule proved in Brauer groups of local and global fields, Theorem 24.2. The original reference is Voight, Corollary 13.4.5. Over \(\mathbb R\) this is the embedding \(\mathbb C\subset\mathbb H\). Any two embeddings of the same quadratic field are conjugate by an invertible element, by Skolem–Noether [NOE-HYP-04, Theorem 2.1]. The centralizer of that field in a quaternion algebra is the field itself [NOE-HYP-04, Theorem 3.1].

Theorem 1.1 — the matching bijection. Equality of characteristic polynomials gives a bijection \[ \{\text{regular elliptic classes in }G\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{regular classes in }G'\}. \tag{1.2} \] For a matched pair \(g,b\), both centralizers identify with \(K^\times\), where \(K=F[T]/(P_g)\). Moreover \(\det g=\operatorname{nrd}b\) and \(\operatorname{tr}g=\operatorname{trd}b\).

Proof. Write a separable irreducible polynomial as \(P(T)=T^2-tT+n\). Its constant term is nonzero. If \(g\) has this polynomial, then for every nonzero \(v\in F^2\), the vectors \(v,gv\) are independent: dependence would give an eigenvalue in \(F\). In this basis, Cayley–Hamilton gives \[ g\sim\begin{pmatrix}0&-n\\1&t\end{pmatrix}. \tag{1.3} \] Thus \(P\) determines exactly one matrix class, and every such \(P\) occurs.

Embed \(K=F[T]/(P)\) in \(D\), and let \(b\) be the image of \(T\). Its minimal polynomial has degree two and divides (1.1), so (1.1) equals \(P\). Different embeddings give conjugate elements by Skolem–Noether. Conversely, if a noncentral regular \(b\in D\) had a reducible reduced polynomial, its distinct linear factors would satisfy \((b-r)(b-s)=0\). A division algebra has no zero divisors; hence \(b=r\) or \(b=s\), a contradiction. Therefore its polynomial is irreducible, and \(F[b]\) is a separable quadratic field. Two elements with the same polynomial define two embeddings of the same \(K\); Skolem–Noether conjugates one to the other.

Finally a matrix commuting with \(g\) is determined by its value on \(v\) in the cyclic basis \(v,gv\). Every such value is \((a+cg)v\), so its algebra centralizer is \(F[g]\). The quaternion centralizer is \(F[b]\) by the stated double-centralizer theorem. Taking units gives the assertion about group centralizers. The trace and norm identities are the coefficients of the common polynomial. \(\square\)

The matching includes the two conjugate generators of \(K\): its nontrivial automorphism extends by Skolem–Noether to conjugation in \(D\). There is no extra choice of a root in (1.2).

For measures, choose corresponding Haar measures on the centralizers \(K^\times\), and compatible group and quotient measures. The regular orbital integral is \[ O_g(f)=\int_{G_g\backslash G}f(x^{-1}gx)\,dx. \tag{1.4} \] One may multiply it by the square root of the Weyl discriminant. That discriminant is the same for matched pairs: both adjoint actions have the same two nontrivial eigenvalue ratios. Thus matching the normalized integrals or the integrals (1.4) amounts to the same condition, once the measures are paired.

The test-function transfer theorem, proved below at both finite and real places, says that a smooth Hecke test \(f'\) on \(D^\times\) has a test \(f\) on \(G\) whose regular elliptic orbital integrals match those of \(f'\), and whose regular split orbital integrals vanish. Transfer determines orbital integrals, rather than a unique function. Theorem 2.10 proves its existence over every nonarchimedean local field by explicit orbital coordinates, and Lemma 2.11 proves the scalar-value constraint for every matching pair. Theorem 2.22 proves the real construction and scalar identity, and Corollary 2.31 gives finite spectral transfer. Arthur, §25, Theorem 25.1(a), is a broader reference. Equation (1.2) supplies the regular classes on which to impose the conditions.

2. The local representation theorem and its sign

For an admissible representation \(\pi\), its character distribution is \[ f\longmapsto\operatorname{tr}\pi(f),\qquad \pi(f)=\int f(g)\pi(g)\,dg. \tag{2.1} \] At finite places Theorem 2.7 below proves, in either characteristic and every residue characteristic, that on the regular set (2.1) is integration against a conjugation-invariant locally constant function \(\Theta_\pi\). Proposition 2.8 gives the finite elliptic compact-type sum, and the division-side argument following it proves the ordinary trace statement for \(D^\times\). These results suffice for the regular character tests below. For real discrete series, Lemma 2.4 below proves the representing character function on the regular elliptic set directly from the disk model. Arthur, Theorem 25.1(b), is the broader real transfer reference.

Theorem 2.1 — local Jacquet–Langlands. Fix a unitary central character \(\omega:F^\times\to\mathbb C^\times\). There is a unique bijection \[ \operatorname{JL}_F: \operatorname{Irr}_\omega(D^\times) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \operatorname{DS}_\omega(\mathrm{GL}_2(F)) \tag{2.2} \] characterized by \[ \Theta_{\operatorname{JL}_F(\tau)}(g) =-\Theta_\tau(b) \qquad(g\leftrightarrow b\text{ regular elliptic}). \tag{2.3} \] Here the source consists of irreducible unitary smooth representations at finite places, and irreducible unitary representations at the real place. The central character is preserved. Twisting is compatible: \[ \operatorname{JL}_F\bigl(\tau\otimes(\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd})\bigr) =\operatorname{JL}_F(\tau)\otimes(\chi\circ\det). \tag{2.4} \] Unitary \(\chi\) keeps (2.2) within discrete series. Allowing arbitrary smooth quasicharacters gives the corresponding bijection onto essentially discrete series.

Theorem 2.14 and Corollary 2.15 below prove the entire depth-zero local correspondence and all determinant/norm twists of that family, in every residue characteristic. Theorem 2.16 also proves the formal degree of every compact-induction model, and Proposition 2.18 computes both positive-level subgroup volumes in every residue characteristic. Sections 2.8–2.10 construct all positive-level images and prove the character identity, paired dimensions and bijection. Lemma 2.27 gives character integration through the singular set. For nonarchimedean \(F\) of characteristic zero, the exact references are Jacquet–Langlands, Theorem 15.1 and Proposition 15.5; Deligne, §3.2.8 gives the same sign and paired formal-degree normalization. Badulescu, Theorem 1.1 supplies the positive-characteristic theorem, including all residue characteristics. Arthur, Theorem 25.1(b) gives the tempered inner-form formulation, including the real place.

At a finite place, the one-dimensional cases are \[ \chi\circ\operatorname{nrd} \longmapsto\mathrm{St}\otimes(\chi\circ\det). \tag{2.5} \] In particular the trivial representation transfers to \(\mathrm{St}\). Higher-dimensional representations transfer to supercuspidals: the remaining discrete series are supercuspidal by Lesson 7, and (2.5) accounts for every special representation. This is a correspondence of isomorphism classes; it is not an identification of representation spaces or of dimensions.

At the real place, write \(\mathbb H^\times=\mathbb R_{>0}\mathrm{SU}(2)\). With trivial positive central character, let \(\tau_m\) restrict to \(\operatorname{Sym}^m(\mathbb C^2)\), \(m\ge0\). Then \[ \tau_m\longmapsto D_{m+2}. \tag{2.6} \] The right side is the discrete series for the full group \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb R)\). On the positive-determinant component it contains the holomorphic and antiholomorphic ladders; negative determinant exchanges them. The common value on \(-1\) is \((-1)^m=(-1)^{m+2}\), and positive central twists must also agree. Thus the real trivial representation transfers to weight-two discrete series, rather than to the finite-place representation in (2.5).

For example, on \(b=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\), \(0<\theta<\pi\), the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) weights give \[ \Theta_{\tau_m}(b) =\sum_{j=0}^{m}e^{i(m-2j)\theta} =\frac{\sin((m+1)\theta)}{\sin\theta}. \tag{2.7} \] The character of \(D_{m+2}\) on the matching rotation is proved directly to be the negative of (2.7) in Theorem 2.5; it is not inferred from (2.3). There is no quaternion division algebra at a complex place.

2.1. Prove the trivial/Steinberg case directly

Proposition 2.2. For nonarchimedean \(F\), \[ \Theta_{\mathrm{St}}(g)=-1 \qquad(g\text{ regular elliptic}). \tag{2.8} \]

Proof. The unnormalized induced representation \(M=C^\infty(\mathbb P^1(F))\) has the exact sequence \[ 0\longrightarrow\mathbf1\longrightarrow M \longrightarrow\mathrm{St}\longrightarrow0. \tag{2.9} \] This is the exceptional induction sequence proved in Lesson 7, Theorem 4.2. An elliptic element fixes no point of \(\mathbb P^1(F)\): a fixed line would give an eigenvalue in \(F\).

Let \(f\) have compact support \(S\) contained in the regular elliptic set. Put \(X=\mathbb P^1(F)\). The compact set \[ \{(x,gx):x\in X,\ g\in S\}\subset X\times X \] is disjoint from the diagonal. For each \(x\), choose a sufficiently small compact open neighborhood \(U_x\) so that no \(g\in S\) takes a point of \(U_x\) into \(U_x\). Such a neighborhood exists: otherwise successively smaller neighborhoods and compactness of \(S\) give a limit \(g\) fixing \(x\). A finite subcover, refined by all intersections and differences, gives a finite compact open partition with that same exclusion property.

Choose a compact open subgroup \(K\) for which \(f\) is bi-invariant and which preserves every cell of this partition. To obtain the latter condition, continuity of the action and compactness of each cell give a neighborhood of the identity preserving that cell; intersect the finitely many neighborhoods and choose a compact open subgroup inside. The \(K\)-orbits in \(X\) are open because \(K\) is open in the transitive group \(G\), and there are finitely many because \(X\) is compact. They refine the partition.

The image of \(M(f)\) lies in \(M^K\). In its basis of orbit indicators, a diagonal entry, evaluated at a representative \(x_i\) of its orbit \(C_i\), is \[ \int_G f(g)1_{C_i}(g^{-1}x_i)\,dg=0. \] Indeed \(g^{-1}x_i\in C_i\) would put a point and its image under \(g\) in the same partition cell. Hence \(\operatorname{tr}M(f)=0\). Taking \(K\)-invariants is exact by compact averaging, so traces add in (2.9). On constants the trace is \(\int f\). Therefore \[ \operatorname{tr}\mathrm{St}(f)=-\int_G f(g)\,dg. \] The character function is locally constant on the regular set. Testing on arbitrarily small compact open neighborhoods of each elliptic regular element proves (2.8). The trivial character of \(D^\times\) is one, so this proves (2.3) for the trivial representation without assuming the full correspondence. \(\square\)

2.2. Prove the real correspondence from the disk model

The real case of Theorem 2.1 can be proved within the models of Lesson 11, without assuming a character-transfer theorem. We give the square-integrability exclusion as well as the character calculation: constructing some discrete series would not by itself prove surjectivity.

Lemma 2.3 — the real square-integrable list. Among the irreducible admissible unitary representations of the full real group, the square-integrable representations modulo the centre are precisely \[ D_k\otimes|\det|^{i\lambda/2},\qquad k\ge2,\quad\lambda\in\mathbb R. \tag{2.10} \] Here a positive scalar \(aI\) acts as \(a^{i\lambda}\), and \(-I\) acts as \((-1)^k\).

Proof. Lesson 11, Sections 3 and 4, proves the admissible classification, the unitary list and its Hilbert models. Its disk calculation proves that every fixed pair of monomials in \(D_k^+\) has coefficient bounded by a constant times \(\cosh(u/2)^{-k}\) on the Cartan element \(a_u=\operatorname{diag}(e^{u/2},e^{-u/2})\), \(u\ge0\). The radial Haar density modulo the centre is a constant times \(\sinh u\,du\). Consequently these coefficients are square-integrable for \(k>1\). The same calculation proves that \(D_1\) has no nonzero square-integrable coefficient. We must still exclude the principal and complementary series in the unitary list.

Use the compact induced model (3.1) of Lesson 11, with its parameter \(s\) and its parity \(\epsilon\). A central unitary twist does not change the absolute value of a coefficient modulo the centre. Select the weight \(n=0\) for even parity and \(n=1\) for odd parity, and normalize its vector to norm one. Since distinct compact weights are orthogonal in every invariant Hilbert norm, its diagonal coefficient is the \(n\)-th Fourier coefficient in the compact model, including for the complementary series.

Put \(c=\cosh(u/2)\) and \(r=\tanh(u/2)\). The bottom row of \(r_\theta a_u\) is \((-e^{u/2}\sin\theta,e^{-u/2}\cos\theta)\). Its squared length is \[ D=e^u\sin^2\theta+e^{-u}\cos^2\theta =c^2|1-r e^{2i\theta}|^2. \] The compact angle \(\phi\) in its upper-triangular-times-rotation decomposition satisfies \[ e^{i(\phi-\theta)} =\frac{1-r e^{-2i\theta}}{|1-r e^{2i\theta}|}. \] Inserting this and the factor \(D^{-(1+s)/2}\) from normalized induction gives the coefficient \[ \begin{aligned} \varphi_n(s,u) &=\frac{c^{-1-s}}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} (1-r e^{2i\theta})^{-(1+s+n)/2} (1-r e^{-2i\theta})^{-(1+s-n)/2}\,d\theta\\ &=c^{-1-s}\,{}_2F_1\left(\frac{1+s+n}{2}, \frac{1+s-n}{2};1;r^2\right). \end{aligned} \tag{2.11} \] The branches in the integral are the power-series branches at \(r=0\). For \(r<1\), both binomial series converge absolutely and uniformly in \(\theta\); integrating selects their equal exponents. This proves the second equality directly from \({}_2F_1(a,b;1;x)=\sum_{j\ge0}(a)_j(b)_j x^j/(j!)^2\).

We record the continuation needed at \(r=1\), with its derivation. This series solves \[ x(1-x)y''+[1-(a+b+1)x]y'-ab y=0. \] When \(a+b=1+s\) and \(s\notin\mathbb Z\), its two solutions at \(x=1\), normalized by their leading terms, are \[ {}_2F_1(a,b;1+s;1-x),\qquad (1-x)^{-s}{}_2F_1(1-a,1-b;1-s;1-x). \] Substitution verifies the differential equation, and their distinct exponents make them independent. Their coefficients in the solution normalized at zero are \[ A=\frac{\Gamma(-s)}{\Gamma(1-a)\Gamma(1-b)}, \qquad B=\frac{\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}. \tag{2.12} \] Here is a way to establish these constants, rather than assume a connection formula. Expanding \((1-xt)^{-a}\) in the beta integral gives \[ {}_2F_1(a,b;1;x) =\frac{1}{\Gamma(b)\Gamma(1-b)} \int_0^1 t^{b-1}(1-t)^{-b}(1-xt)^{-a}\,dt \] first for \(0<\Re b<1\). If \(\Re(1-a-b)>0\), its value at \(x=1\) is the beta integral \(\Gamma(1-a-b)/(\Gamma(1-a)\Gamma(1-b))\). Absolute convergence of the defining series at one extends this value holomorphically throughout that half-space in \((a,b)\). It gives \(A\) when \(\Re s<0\), since the second local solution then tends to zero. Substitution in the differential equation and uniqueness of its analytic solution at zero also give Euler's transformation \[ {}_2F_1(a,b;1;x)=(1-x)^{-s} {}_2F_1(1-a,1-b;1;x). \] The same value-at-one argument applied to the right side gives \(B\) when \(\Re s>0\). The coefficients of the two local solutions depend meromorphically on the parameters: this follows by solving the two-by-two system of their values and derivatives at any interior point. Analytic continuation therefore proves both expressions (2.12) for every nonintegral \(s\). This also covers \(n=1\), where an Euler integral with the original parameters alone would not cover both half-planes.

Since \(1-r^2=c^{-2}=4e^{-u}(1+O(e^{-u}))\), equations (2.11)–(2.12), and the power series of the local solutions, now give \[ \varphi_n(s,u)=e^{-u/2}\left(C_n(s)e^{-su/2} +C_n(-s)e^{su/2}\right) +O\left(e^{-(3-|\Re s|)u/2}\right), \tag{2.13} \] for fixed nonintegral \(s\), where the gamma duplication identity simplifies the constants to \[ C_0(s)=\frac{\Gamma(-s/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((1-s)/2)}, \qquad C_1(s)=\frac{\Gamma((1-s)/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(1-s/2)}. \tag{2.14} \] All error estimates here are ordinary convergent-series estimates at \(1-r^2=0\); no boundary interchange in (2.11) is needed.

For a principal series, \(s=it\), \(t\ne0\). The gamma function has no zeros, and none of the arguments in (2.14) is a pole; equivalently this follows from the Euler product for \(1/\Gamma\). Thus both coefficients are nonzero. They are complex conjugates. The integral over \([0,T]\) of the squared absolute value of \(C_n(it)e^{-itu/2}+C_n(-it)e^{itu/2}\) equals \(2|C_n(it)|^2T+O(1)\). Multiplying (2.13) squared by \(\sinh u\) changes this by an integrable error and a factor tending to \(1/2\). The coefficient is not square-integrable.

At \(s=0\) in even parity, the poles \(C_0(s)=-2/(\pi s)+O(1)\) and \(C_0(-s)=2/(\pi s)+O(1)\) coalesce. Taking the limit in the convergent local expansions gives \[ \varphi_0(0,u)=e^{-u/2}\left(\frac{2u}{\pi}+O(1)\right). \tag{2.15} \] Indeed the pole parts in (2.13) combine as \(4\sinh(su/2)/(\pi s)\); subsequent series terms are bounded by \(O((1+u)e^{-u})\) inside the parentheses. This coefficient is again not square-integrable. Odd parity at \(s=0\) is \(D_1\), already excluded by the disk calculation.

For a complementary series choose \(0<s<1\), using the equivalence of the parameters \(s\) and \(-s\) proved in Lesson 11. It has even parity. The coefficient of the slower term in (2.13) is \[ C_0(-s)=\frac{\Gamma(s/2)}{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((1+s)/2)}>0. \] Thus \(|\varphi_0(s,u)|^2\sinh u\) grows as a positive constant times \(e^{su}\). A unitary finite-dimensional member of the real list is one-dimensional, and its coefficient has constant absolute value; the group modulo the centre has infinite volume, so it too is excluded.

For completeness, a nonintegrable coefficient of the chosen compact weight excludes the existence of any nonzero square-integrable coefficient. In an irreducible unitary representation every nonzero vector is cyclic. If \(\langle\pi(g)v,w\rangle\) were a nonzero square-integrable coefficient, choose translates of \(v,w\) whose projections onto the selected one-dimensional compact weight are nonzero. Such translates exist by cyclicity. The two compact projections express the new coefficient as left and right compact averages of translates of the old one. These operations preserve \(L^2\) by Minkowski's inequality. The new coefficient is a nonzero scalar multiple of \(\varphi_n\), contradicting the calculations above. For the full group, translates may use either component; its compact weight spaces are still one-dimensional in the principal and complementary models. The Haar integral on the positive component already diverges. Together with the full unitary list of Lesson 11, this proves (2.10). \(\square\)

Lemma 2.4 — the disk trace on the elliptic set. Write \(D_k^+\), \(k\ge2\), for the positive ladder on \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)\), with rotation weights \(k+2j\), \(j\ge0\). On its regular elliptic set the character distribution is represented by the smooth function which, at a rotation, is \[ \Theta_k^+(r_\theta)=\frac{e^{ik\theta}}{1-e^{2i\theta}}, \qquad\theta\notin\pi\mathbb Z. \tag{2.16} \] The negative ladder has the same formula with \(\theta\) replaced by \(-\theta\).

Proof. We use precisely the disk Hilbert space and inverse-pullback action constructed in Lesson 11, Section 4.1. Its orthonormal basis and action are \[ e_j(z)=\sqrt{\frac{(k)_j}{j!}}z^j,\qquad \sigma_k(g)f(z)=j_g(z)^{-k}f(\phi_g(z)), \] where \(\phi_g\) is the disk automorphism associated with \(g^{-1}\) and \(j_g(z)\) is its linear denominator. The multiplier has no zero or pole in the disk. For \(0<r<1\), the dilation operator \[ R_r e_j=r^j e_j,\qquad (R_r f)(z)=f(rz), \qquad \|R_r\|_1=(1-r)^{-1} \tag{2.17} \] is trace class. In particular \(R_r\sigma_k(g)\) is trace class. It is weighted composition with \(\Psi_{r,g}(z)=\phi_g(rz)\) and weight \(w_{r,g}(z)=j_g(rz)^{-k}\).

The map \(\Psi_{r,g}\) has a unique fixed point \(z_{r,g}\) in the disk. To see this, use the complete hyperbolic disk metric. A disk automorphism is an isometry, whereas multiplication by \(r\) has hyperbolic differential norm \[ \frac{r(1-|z|^2)}{1-r^2|z|^2}\le r<1. \] The contraction theorem gives the fixed point. Conjugate weighted composition by a unitary disk automorphism taking that fixed point to zero. The new symbol fixes zero, its derivative there is \(\Psi_{r,g}'(z_{r,g})\), and its new weight at zero is \(w_{r,g}(z_{r,g})\); the conjugating multipliers cancel at a fixed point. The diagonal coefficient on \(e_j\) is therefore \(w_{r,g}(z_{r,g})\Psi_{r,g}'(z_{r,g})^j\). Trace invariance under unitary conjugacy and the absolute convergence of a trace-class diagonal give \[ \operatorname{tr}(R_r\sigma_k(g)) =\frac{j_g(rz_{r,g})^{-k}} {1-r\phi_g'(rz_{r,g})}. \tag{2.18} \] The denominator is nonzero since the fixed-point derivative has absolute value at most \(r\).

If \(g\) is regular elliptic, \(\phi_g\) has an interior fixed point \(z_g\) and its derivative there differs from one. The implicit function theorem applied to \(z-\phi_g(rz)=0\) proves that \(z_{r,g}\) tends to \(z_g\) smoothly as \(r\uparrow1\), uniformly when \(g\) ranges over a compact subset of the regular elliptic set. Uniqueness of the contraction fixed point identifies this local solution with \(z_{r,g}\). Thus (2.18) has a uniform smooth limit on each such compact subset. Conjugating \(g\) to \(r_\theta\) computes this limit: at zero its multiplier is \(e^{ik\theta}\), and its symbol derivative is \(e^{2i\theta}\). The limiting expression is conjugation invariant because a conjugating multiplier again cancels at the fixed point. This proves the candidate function (2.16).

It remains to identify the limit with the character distribution, rather than a merely formal sum of weights. For \(f\in C_c^\infty(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R))\), integrating by parts in the left and right rotation variables gives, for every \(N\), \[ |\langle\sigma_k(f)e_j,e_i\rangle| \le C_{f,N}(k+2i)^{-N}(k+2j)^{-N}. \tag{2.19} \] The constant can be taken to be the \(L^1\)-norm of the corresponding derivatives of \(f\): coefficients of unitary operators between unit vectors are at most one. For \(N>1\) the matrix entries are absolutely summable. Its sum of rank-one matrix operators converges in trace norm and agrees with \(\sigma_k(f)\) on all basis vectors. Hence \(\sigma_k(f)\) is trace class.

The bounded operators \(R_r\) tend strongly to the identity and have norm at most one. Finite-rank approximation of a trace-class operator then gives \(\|R_r\sigma_k(f)-\sigma_k(f)\|_1\to0\). For each fixed \(r<1\), the trace-class bound in (2.17) permits integration of (2.18) against \(f\). If the support of \(f\) is regular elliptic, its uniform limit just proved gives \[ \operatorname{tr}\sigma_k(f) =\lim_{r\uparrow1}\int f(g)\operatorname{tr}(R_r\sigma_k(g))\,dg =\int f(g)\Theta_k^+(g)\,dg. \tag{2.20} \] This proves the assertion as a distribution on the entire regular elliptic set. Complex conjugation gives the negative ladder. \(\square\)

Theorem 2.5 — real local Jacquet–Langlands, proved. The real case of (2.2)–(2.4) is the unique map \[ a^{i\lambda}\operatorname{Sym}^m(\mathbb C^2) \longmapsto D_{m+2}\otimes|\det|^{i\lambda/2}, \qquad a>0,\quad m\ge0. \tag{2.21} \] In particular it includes every real discrete series in the stated admissible category, with both ladders for the full group.

Proof. An irreducible unitary representation of a compact group is finite-dimensional: averaging a rank-one orthogonal projection over the group gives a nonzero positive compact operator commuting with the representation; a positive eigenspace is finite-dimensional and invariant, hence is the entire irreducible representation. Norm continuity of the translated rank-one projections makes this average a compact operator, and its trace is one, so it is nonzero. The positive scalar group in \(\mathbb H^\times\) acts by a character, by the unitary Schur lemma. Its \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) restriction is irreducible, since that character is scalar.

For clarity, the compact classification used here follows from the finite-dimensional \(\mathfrak{sl}_2\) calculation. Choose a highest-weight vector \(v\) of weight \(m\). Then \(EF^jv=j(m-j+1)F^{j-1}v\). If \(F^Nv\ne0\) and \(F^{N+1}v=0\), this identity forces \(m=N\ge0\); the span of these vectors is invariant and hence is the whole representation. It is \(\operatorname{Sym}^m(\mathbb C^2)\), with weights \(m,m-2,\ldots,-m\). A connected group representation is determined by its derivative, so this also identifies the \(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) representation. Its value at \(-1\) is \((-1)^m\).

Lemma 2.3 now makes (2.21) a bijection preserving the entire central character. For a matching elliptic pair, remove its positive scalar \(a\) and conjugate its unit part to \(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\), or to the matching rotation. By Lemma 2.4 and the two-ladder decomposition in Lesson 11, \[ \begin{aligned} \Theta_{D_k}(r_\theta) &=\frac{e^{ik\theta}}{1-e^{2i\theta}} +\frac{e^{-ik\theta}}{1-e^{-2i\theta}}\\ &=-\frac{\sin((k-1)\theta)}{\sin\theta} =-\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}e^{i(k-2-2j)\theta}. \end{aligned} \tag{2.22} \] The same integration-by-parts proof gives trace-class test operators on the full real group, using its two sets of compact weights. On the negative-determinant component the representation exchanges the ladders, so the smoothed diagonal trace is zero; on the positive component it is the sum used in (2.22). A positive scalar multiplies the character by \(a^{i\lambda}\). Equation (2.22) is exactly the negative of (2.7) with \(m=k-2\), as required. The distinct Laurent polynomials in (2.22) show uniqueness among the discrete series with the fixed positive central character.

The real formal degrees can also be compared with explicit measures. Give each component of \(G/Z\) the Cartan integration formula with probability measures on its two compact angular variables and radial density \(\sinh u\,du\). Give the compact group \(\mathbb H^\times/\mathbb R^\times\) volume \(2\). Define the coefficient map \(T\) into the centrally covariant \(L^2(G/Z)\) space on its maximal domain, consisting of vectors whose coefficients are square-integrable. 2.3. Prove the finite-place character theorem on the regular set

We now prove the regular-set character statement used in (2.1), for every nonarchimedean local field, including residue characteristic two and positive characteristic. This proof concerns the restriction of the distribution to the regular set. Section 2.4 separately proves smooth orbital transfer and the scalar orbital germ. It does not identify the character distribution globally across the singular set.

Write \(\mathcal O\) for the valuation ring, \(\mathfrak p=(\varpi)\), and \(v(\varpi)=1\). The lattice tree \(\mathcal T\) has vertices the homothety classes of rank-two \(\mathcal O\)-lattices in \(F^2\). Two vertices are adjacent if representatives satisfy \(\varpi L\subset L'\subset L\), with \(L'/\varpi L\) a line over the residue field. Each vertex has \(q+1\) neighbors. The metric is the number of edges in a path, rather than a Euclidean metric on a drawing.

Here are the elementary tree facts we will use. Elementary divisors, proved by the row and column elimination in Lesson 6, equation (5.4), put any pair of vertices in the form \([\mathcal O^2]\), \([\mathcal O\varpi^N e_1+\mathcal Oe_2]\), \(N\ge0\). Their path consists of the intermediate exponents. At a vertex other than the initial one, reduction of the elementary-divisor basis modulo \(\mathfrak p\) identifies exactly one neighbor whose distance to the initial vertex decreases; the other \(q\) increase it. Induction gives the unique path, so the graph is a locally finite tree. Its ends are \(\mathbb P^1(F)\): a ray starting at \([\mathcal O^2]\) specifies compatible primitive line data modulo every \(\mathfrak p^N\), and completeness gives its unique limiting line. Conversely a primitive vector on a line gives these data and a ray. The usual action of \(G\) on the ends is the action on lines; it is continuous. The tree with its ends is compact after adjoining the ends: a sequence escaping every finite ball has a subsequence with each finite initial path fixed, by a diagonal selection in the finite branching tree.

For a vertex \(x=[L]\), and an integer \(m\ge1\), set \[ U_x(m)=1+\mathfrak p^m\operatorname{End}_{\mathcal O}(L), \qquad e_x=\frac{1_{U_x(m)}}{\operatorname{vol}U_x(m)}, \qquad V_x=e_xV. \tag{2.25} \] The order, group and average do not depend on the representative of the homothety class. We use the same symbol \(e_x\) for the convolution operator on a representation. For an edge \(a=\{x,z\}\), put \(e_a=e_xe_z\) and \(V_a=e_aV\).

Lemma 2.6 — the exact tree complex and its finite projection. For any smooth representation, the averages at adjacent vertices commute. If \(z\) is the first neighbor of \(x\) on the path to \(y\ne x\), then \[ e_xe_z=e_ze_x,\qquad e_xe_ze_y=e_xe_y. \tag{2.26} \] For a finite subtree \(\Sigma\), the operator \[ u_\Sigma=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{(0)}}e_x -\sum_{a\in\Sigma^{(1)}}e_a \tag{2.27} \] is an idempotent with image \(S_\Sigma=\sum_{x\in\Sigma^{(0)}}V_x\), and acts as the identity on that image. With an arbitrary orientation for each edge, the sequence \[ 0\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{a\in\Sigma^{(1)}}V_a \xrightarrow{\partial}\bigoplus_{x\in\Sigma^{(0)}}V_x \xrightarrow{\mathrm{sum}}S_\Sigma\longrightarrow0 \tag{2.28} \] is exact. The edge boundary is its inclusion at the terminal vertex minus its inclusion at the initial vertex. If an irreducible smooth representation has \(V_{x_0}\ne0\), the sum of all \(V_x\) is the entire representation.

Proof. Conjugate a pair and its path to \(x=[\mathcal O^2]\), \(z=[t_1\mathcal O^2]\), \(y=[t_N\mathcal O^2]\), where \(t_j=\operatorname{diag}(\varpi^j,1)\) and \(N\ge1\). In this basis \(U_z(m)\) has diagonal entries in \(1+\mathfrak p^m\), upper entries in \(\mathfrak p^{m+1}\), and lower entries in \(\mathfrak p^{m-1}\). It is contained in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathcal O)\), so it normalizes \(U_x(m)\). Conversely \(t_1^{-1}U_x(m)t_1\) is integral, so \(U_x(m)\) normalizes \(U_z(m)\). Their product is therefore a compact subgroup, and either order of averaging is its normalized average. Its fixed space is \(V_x\cap V_z\), which proves the first identity and the assertion about \(V_a\).

Gaussian elimination in \(U_z(m)\) factors an element into upper root, diagonal and lower root elements with precisely those entry bounds. The upper root and diagonal elements lie in \(U_x(m)\); the lower root lies in \(U_y(m)\), because \(\mathfrak p^{m-1}\subset\mathfrak p^{m-N}\). The first two act trivially on \(e_xe_yV\). The last also acts trivially there: it normalizes \(U_x(m)\), so commutes with \(e_x\), and it acts trivially on \(e_yV\). Thus \(e_ze_xe_y=e_xe_y\). The adjacent commutation gives the second identity in (2.26). These arguments are identities of compact averaging operators and do not assume unitarity. Inverting the compact measures gives the reversed identity \(e_ye_ze_x=e_ye_x\).

Prove (2.27) by adding a leaf \(x\), with neighbor \(z\), to a smaller subtree \(\Sigma'\). Put \(p_x=e_x(1-e_z)\). Adjacent commutation makes \(p_x\) an idempotent. For every vertex \(y\in\Sigma'\), equation (2.26) gives \(p_xe_y=e_yp_x=0\), and the same holds with each edge average of \(\Sigma'\). Hence \(u_{\Sigma'}p_x=p_xu_{\Sigma'}=0\) and \(u_\Sigma=u_{\Sigma'}+p_x\) is an idempotent. It is the identity on every old \(V_y\). On \(V_x\), use \(e_xe_zV\subset V_z\), where \(u_{\Sigma'}\) is the identity, and \(u_{\Sigma'}p_x=0\), to obtain \(u_\Sigma e_x=e_x\). Every summand in (2.27) has image in \(S_\Sigma\), proving the claimed image. This starts with a one-vertex tree.

For exactness, suppose finitely many vertex vectors sum to zero. At a leaf \(x\), apply \(p_x\) to this sum; every other vertex term is killed, so \(p_xv_x=0\). Thus \(v_x=e_zv_x\in V_x\cap V_z=V_a\). Subtract the boundary of this edge vector, with the appropriate sign, to remove the leaf term and transfer it to \(z\). Repeat on the smaller tree. This proves exactness in the middle. An edge cycle is zero by inspecting a leaf, where its unique incident edge vector must vanish, and then removing the leaf. Surjectivity is the definition of \(S_\Sigma\). Finally a nonzero \(V_{x_0}\) generates an irreducible representation; its translates lie in the corresponding \(V_{gx_0}\). \(\square\)

Every irreducible smooth representation permits an \(m\ge1\) with \(V_{[\mathcal O^2]}\ne0\), by smoothness of a nonzero vector. Admissibility, proved in Lesson 6, Theorem 5.4, makes all the vertex and edge spaces finite-dimensional. We fix such an \(m\) from now on. For a general coefficient-system comparison, see Meyer–Solleveld, Definition 2.1, Lemma 2.6 and Theorem 2.12. The rank-two group identities and leaf proof needed here have just been supplied explicitly.

Theorem 2.7 — finite-place regular characters, proved. For every irreducible smooth complex representation \(\pi\) of \(\mathrm{GL}_2(F)\), the character distribution on the regular set is integration against a unique conjugation-invariant locally constant function \(\Theta_\pi\). This holds in either characteristic, with no restriction on residue characteristic.

Proof. First consider an element compact modulo the centre. It fixes a vertex or an edge midpoint of \(\mathcal T\). For split eigenvalues of equal absolute value this follows by scaling them to units in a diagonal basis. For an irreducible quadratic polynomial, its field \(E=F[g]\) has the integral lattice \(\mathcal O_E\). Multiplication by a unit preserves that lattice; if the ramification index is two, an element of odd \(E\)-valuation instead exchanges the lattice classes of \(\mathcal O_E\) and \(\mathfrak p_E\). Scaling by \(F^\times\) reduces the valuation to these cases. This is also the compact-adjoint criterion of Lesson 10, Lemma 7.1.

Choose a fixed point \(c\) that is a vertex or an edge midpoint, and let \(C=G_c\) be its stabilizer. This is an open subgroup, compact modulo the centre. Finite balls \(\Sigma_N\) about \(c\), including their edges, are invariant under \(C\). If \(f\) is a compactly supported smooth function in \(C\), its operator has finite rank by admissibility. Its image lies in some \(S_{\Sigma_{N_0}}\), because Lemma 2.6 exhausts \(V\). For \(N\ge N_0\), the exact finite sequence (2.28) is equivariant for \(C\); traces add along it. Therefore \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}\pi(f)&=\int_C f(h)\,T_N(h)\,dh,\\ T_N(h)&=\sum_{\substack{x\in\Sigma_N^{(0)}\\hx=x}} \operatorname{tr}(\pi(h)|_{V_x}) -\sum_{\substack{a\in\Sigma_N^{(1)}\\ha=a}} \varepsilon(h,a)\operatorname{tr}(\pi(h)|_{V_a}). \end{aligned} \tag{2.29} \] Here \(\varepsilon(h,a)=1\) if both endpoints are fixed and \(-1\) if they are exchanged. An edge orientation contributes that sign to the chain action. For a cell moved to a different cell the diagonal block on the direct sum is zero. This proves (2.29) without a character theorem. The threshold \(N_0\) may initially depend on \(f\).

For a regular elliptic \(g\), the set of its fixed vertices and edges is finite. Otherwise their geometric fixed-point set contains a ray, since it is convex in a locally finite tree; its end would be an \(F\)-eigenline. Such a line cannot exist for an elliptic element. This fixed-cell set is uniformly bounded on a neighborhood of \(g\). Indeed, if \(g_j\to g\) fixed cells escaping every finite ball, a subsequence of their centres would converge to an end \(\ell\). Continuity of the action on the tree and its ends gives \(g\ell=\ell\), again an eigenline. This reasoning also applies to inverted edges, by using their midpoints. A neighborhood of \(g\) consists of elliptic elements: the set of matrices fixing a line is closed, since \(\mathbb P^1(F)\) is compact. Shrink it to be regular as well and to lie in \(C\).

Within the resulting bounded ball, further shrinking makes the permutation of all cells constant and makes every relevant finite-dimensional matrix trace constant. For the latter, \(g^{-1}h\) may be taken in all the finitely many compact groups \(U_x(m)\) involved, so acts trivially on their fixed spaces; edge spaces are subspaces of their endpoint spaces. Thus for all sufficiently large \(N\), \(T_N\) is the same locally constant finite sum on this neighborhood. Equation (2.29), applied to arbitrary tests there, proves the character assertion at \(g\).

Compact split elements require more care: their fixed set is infinite, and an unqualified infinite vertex sum is not a character formula. After conjugation and a scalar translation, let \(g=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)\), \(a,b\in\mathcal O^\times\), \(a\ne b\). Put \(\delta=a/b\) and \(r=v(\delta-1)\ge0\). Let \(A\) be the diagonal apartment, with vertices \(A_n=[\mathcal O\varpi^ne_1+\mathcal Oe_2]\), \(n\in\mathbb Z\).

Every vertex has a unique presentation \[ L(n,x)=\mathcal O\varpi^ne_1+\mathcal O(e_2+xe_1), \qquad x\in F/\mathfrak p^n. \] To obtain it, scale the projection of a lattice to the second coordinate to \(\mathcal O\); its intersection with the first coordinate is \(\mathfrak p^ne_1\), and a lift of \(e_2\) gives \(x\) modulo that ideal. For \(x=0\), this is \(A_n\). Otherwise choose its representative with \(v(x)=l<n\). The successive lattices \(L(j,x)\), \(l\le j\le n\), give its path from \(A_l\), with the first step off the apartment. The two apartment directions at \(A_l\) are the coordinate lines, whereas this first step has both coordinates nonzero after scaling. Thus its distance from \(A\) is \(d=n-l\). Direct multiplication shows \[ gL(n,x)=L(n,x) \ \Longleftrightarrow\ (\delta-1)x\in\mathfrak p^n \ \Longleftrightarrow\ d\le r. \tag{2.30} \] Its fixed vertices are exactly the tube of radius \(r\) about \(A\); its fixed edges have both endpoints in that tube. There is no edge inversion, since the determinant valuation is even.

Consider \(t=\operatorname{diag}(1+u,1+w)\), with \(u,w\in\mathfrak p^M\), \(M\ge m+r\). In the basis \(\varpi^ne_1,e_2+xe_1\), its only off-diagonal entry is \((u-w)x\varpi^{-n}\), of valuation at least \(M-d\). Consequently \(t\in U_{L(n,x)}(m)\) for every vertex fixed by \(g\), and it is the identity on every corresponding edge space too. Require also \(M>r\). For all sufficiently small changes \(a',b'\) of the two eigenvalues, their ratio has the same \(r\), and \(\operatorname{diag}(a',b')=gt\) with such a \(t\). Equations (2.29)–(2.30) therefore give \[ T_N(\operatorname{diag}(a',b'))=T_N(g) \qquad\text{for every }N. \tag{2.31} \] The use of \(M\ge m+r\) is uniform along the infinite apartment.

Every sufficiently near matrix \(h\) is conjugate to such a diagonal matrix by an element of \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathcal O)\) near the identity. For completeness, write its monic characteristic polynomial as \(P\). Near \(a\), choose a radius \(\rho<|a-b|\) and shrink the matrix perturbation so that \(|P(a)|\le\rho|P'(a)|\) and \(|P'(a)|=|a-b|\). The equation for a root \(a+z\) is \(z=-P(a)/P'(a)-z^2/P'(a)\). On \(|z|\le\rho\) this map preserves the ball and strictly contracts it. Successive approximation gives a unique root there; the same argument near \(b\) gives the other root. Taking \(\rho\) arbitrarily small proves their continuous dependence. The projectors \((h-b')/(a'-b')\) and \((h-a')/(b'-a')\), applied to \(e_1,e_2\), then give an eigenbasis near the original one. This argument divides by \(a'-b'\), never by two, so it works in characteristic two. Conjugating by an integral element preserves every ball about \(A_0\) and transports all the fixed-vector spaces. Hence (2.31) holds on one neighborhood \(U\) of \(g\) in \(C=G_{A_0}\), for every \(N\).

Choose a test \(f_0\) supported in \(U\), with integral one. Equation (2.29) gives \(T_N(g)=\operatorname{tr}\pi(f_0)\) for all sufficiently large \(N\). Thus these constants stabilize, and do so uniformly on \(U\). For any other test \(f\) supported there, take \(N\) larger than both its finite-image threshold and the threshold for \(f_0\). Then (2.29) is the claimed integration against the same constant on \(U\). This establishes local constancy and the distributional identity at every compact split regular element, rather than only convergence of an averaged expression.

It remains to treat split elements with unequal absolute values. Supercuspidals have zero character distribution on a neighborhood of each such element by Lesson 10, Theorem 7.3. By the classification in Lesson 7, the other irreducibles are principal series, Steinberg twists and determinant characters. We supply the principal-series calculation on the entire regular set.

In the chart with representative \(\overline n(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\x&1\end{pmatrix}\) for the row line \((x,1)\), a normalized induced representation \(I(\mu_1,\mu_2)\) acts by weighted composition. For \(h=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix}\), put \[ \phi_h(x)=\frac{Ax+C}{Bx+D},\qquad w_h(x)=\mu_1\left(\frac{\det h}{Bx+D}\right) \mu_2(Bx+D)\left|\frac{\det h}{(Bx+D)^2}\right|^{1/2}. \tag{2.32} \] These formulas follow by multiplying \(\overline n(x)h\) and performing its upper-triangular-times-\(\overline n\) decomposition. Other charts give the same induced line bundle, with their usual transition multipliers.

A compactly supported smooth group test gives a locally constant integral kernel on each pair of small projective charts. To see this directly, in (2.32) use \(y=\phi_h(x)\) and \(C=(Bx+D)y-Ax\) as a group coordinate. With additive measures, Haar measure is a constant times \(|\det h|^{-2}\,dA\,dB\,dC\,dD\), and \(dC=|Bx+D|\,dy\). On a sufficiently small chart the denominator is nonzero and bounded away from zero over the compact group support. The resulting integrand is locally constant in \((x,y)\), uniformly over a compact parameter set. Integrating in \((A,B,D)\) gives the claimed kernel. Finitely many such charts cover \(\mathbb P^1(F)\). Refining their clopen partitions makes the kernel constant on each pair of cells; the matrix trace is therefore its diagonal integral, since each diagonal entry is the diagonal kernel value times the measure of its cell.

Near \(g=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)\), \(a\ne b\), only its two fixed projective points can contribute to that diagonal trace. On the compact complement of small neighborhoods of those points, \(g\) fixes no line, and the same exclusion holds for all sufficiently near \(h\). In the chart near zero the diagonal condition is \(C=Bx^2+(D-A)x\). Its derivative in \(x\) is \(D-A+2Bx\), of fixed nonzero absolute value on a sufficiently small neighborhood. Here the change of variables can be checked without a general inverse function theorem. Choose the ball about zero with radius \(\rho\) so that \(|B|\rho<|D-A|\). On it the equation \(C=(D-A)x+Bx^2\) is solved by the strict contraction \(x=C/(D-A)-Bx^2/(D-A)\). Differences satisfy \(|C(x)-C(y)|=|D-A|\,|x-y|\); successive approximation proves bijectivity onto the ball of radius \(|D-A|\rho\), and this exact scaling of subballs proves the Jacobian. Thus \(x\) is a unique coordinate for the corresponding \(C\)-ball. The Jacobian in the diagonal integral is consequently \[ \frac{|Bx+D|}{|D-A+2Bx|} =\frac{1}{|1-\phi_h'(x)|}, \tag{2.33} \] since at a fixed point \(\phi_h'(x)=(A-Bx)/(D+Bx)\). All these substitutions are in compact chart integrals. They remain valid in characteristic two, where the derivative term \(2Bx\) is simply zero. The chart at infinity gives the other fixed point. The two fixed-point multipliers and derivatives for the diagonal matrix are \(\mu_1(a)\mu_2(b)|a/b|^{1/2},a/b\) and \(\mu_1(b)\mu_2(a)|b/a|^{1/2},b/a\). Thus \[ \Theta_{I(\mu_1,\mu_2)}(\operatorname{diag}(a,b)) =\frac{\mu_1(a)\mu_2(b)+\mu_1(b)\mu_2(a)} {|1-a/b|^{1/2}|1-b/a|^{1/2}}. \tag{2.34} \] The same local eigenbasis argument as before proves that this function represents the distribution on a neighborhood, and is locally constant there. On the elliptic set there is no fixed projective point, so the diagonal kernel is zero and the principal-series character is zero.

Taking traces in the exact induced sequence (2.9), which is exact on every compact fixed space, gives the remaining explicit characters. In particular \[ \begin{aligned} \Theta_{\chi\circ\det}(h)&=\chi(\det h),\\ \Theta_{\mathrm{St}\otimes(\chi\circ\det)}(\operatorname{diag}(a,b)) &=\chi(ab)\left(\frac{|a|+|b|}{|a-b|}-1\right),\\ \Theta_{\mathrm{St}\otimes(\chi\circ\det)}(h) &=-\chi(\det h)\qquad(h\text{ regular elliptic}). \end{aligned} \tag{2.35} \] These expressions and supercuspidal support handle every noncompact split element. They also agree with the compact argument by uniqueness of a representing function: testing on arbitrarily small open sets identifies any two locally constant representatives. Conjugation invariance of the character distribution then gives conjugation invariance of the unique function. This proves the theorem on the entire regular set. \(\square\)

Proposition 2.8 — the finite elliptic compact-type character sum. Let \(\pi=\mathrm{c\!-\!Ind}_J^G\tau\), where \(J\) is open, contains the centre and is compact modulo the centre, and \(\tau\) is finite-dimensional. Assume \(\pi\) is irreducible. For a regular elliptic element \(g\), \[ \Theta_\pi(g)=\sum_{\substack{Jx\in J\backslash G\\xgx^{-1}\in J}} \operatorname{tr}\tau(xgx^{-1}). \tag{2.36} \] The sum is finite and is uniformly a finite sum on a neighborhood of \(g\). Every supercuspidal has this description by Lesson 10, Theorem 7.8, including residue characteristic two.

Proof. The compact group \(J/Z\) has a finite orbit on the vertices of the locally finite tree. The convex hull of that orbit is finite; its centre is a vertex or edge midpoint fixed by all of \(J\). Call it \(c_J\). The index \([G_{c_J}:J]\) is finite, since \(J\) is open and both groups contain \(Z\), while \(G_{c_J}/Z\) is compact.

If \(xgx^{-1}\in J\), then \(g\) fixes \(x^{-1}c_J\). The fixed vertices and edge midpoints for an elliptic element are finite and uniformly bounded on a neighborhood, as proved in Theorem 2.7. For each possible point, the fibre of \(Jx\mapsto x^{-1}c_J\) has at most \([G_{c_J}:J]\) elements: after selecting one \(x\) it is a translate of \(J\backslash G_{c_J}\). This proves both finiteness assertions. Membership in the open subgroup \(J\) and the finite-dimensional smooth trace of \(\tau\) are locally constant for this finite list.

The compact-induction space is the algebraic direct sum of copies of the space of \(\tau\), indexed by \(J\backslash G\). Right translation by \(h\) has a diagonal block at \(Jx\) exactly when \(xhx^{-1}\in J\), and that block is \(\tau(xhx^{-1})\). For a test supported in the chosen elliptic neighborhood only the uniformly finite list can contribute. Its integrated operator has finite rank by admissibility, so its trace is the integral of that finite diagonal sum. This is (2.36), identified with Theorem 2.7's representative. \(\square\)

On the division side no infinite-dimensional character analysis is needed. If a smooth irreducible representation of \(D^\times\) has a scalar central character, choose a nonzero vector fixed by an open subgroup \(U\). Compactness of \(D^\times/F^\times\) makes \(F^\times\backslash D^\times/U\) finite, so the orbit of that vector spans a finite-dimensional space. Irreducibility makes it the whole space. A common open stabilizer of a finite basis makes its ordinary trace function locally constant on all of \(D^\times\), and integrating its matrices gives its character distribution. This proves the division-side regular character statement too.

2.4. Construct finite-place transferred tests and their scalar values

We prove smooth test transfer over every nonarchimedean local field. The construction includes characteristic two. It uses an Euler test for the scalar part and an explicit orbital-coordinate lift for the remainder; no character correspondence or density theorem enters it. In the orbital-coordinate construction, elliptic means that the quadratic polynomial is irreducible; the calculations also cover inseparable nonscalar classes. The correspondence itself uses the separable regular elliptic classes.

Put \(H=G/Z\), \(H'=D^\times/F^\times\), and normalize the common central Haar measure by \(\operatorname{vol}\mathcal O^\times=1\). For \(H\), give a vertex stabilizer \(K\) of the lattice tree volume one. Let \(I\) fix the two endpoints of an edge, and let \(J\) be its full setwise stabilizer. The edge orientation character is \(\epsilon:J\to\{1,-1\}\). A representative \(\begin{pmatrix}0&\varpi\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) exchanges the endpoints; its square is scalar. Since \(K/I\) has \(q+1\) elements, \[ \begin{gathered} \operatorname{vol}I=\frac1{q+1},\qquad \operatorname{vol}J=\frac2{q+1},\qquad \operatorname{vol}H'=\frac2{q-1},\\ f_{\rm EP}=\frac{1_K}{\operatorname{vol}K} -\frac{\epsilon\,1_J}{\operatorname{vol}J},\qquad e'=\frac1{\operatorname{vol}H'},\qquad f_{\rm EP}(1)=-\frac{q-1}{2}=-e'(1). \end{gathered} \tag{2.37} \] The two functions in this display are on the projective groups. A class-invariant cutoff below will give compactly supported functions on the full groups.

These group measures are paired by the reduced trace convention. With additive measure of volume one on \(\mathcal O\), the split measure is \(\kappa|\det x|^{-2}d^4x\), where \(\kappa=((1-q^{-1})(1-q^{-2}))^{-1}\). Indeed the additive volume of \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathcal O)\) is \((1-q^{-1})(1-q^{-2})\). In the quaternion model of Lesson 17, equation (1.2), the trace pairing on \(\mathcal O_L\oplus\mathcal O_Lj\) has one perfect quadratic block and one block multiplied by \(\varpi\). Its four-dimensional determinant has valuation two. Thus additive self-dual measure for that pairing gives \(\mathcal O_D\) volume \(q^{-1}\). This is the block calculation of Lesson 17, Section 4, and works over any residue field. The unit fraction is \(1-q^{-2}\), because the residue field of \(\mathcal O_D\) has \(q^2\) elements. Consequently the paired measure \(\kappa|\operatorname{nrd}x|^{-2}dx\) gives \(\mathcal O_D^\times\) volume \(1/(q-1)\). Its projective image has index two in \(H'\), by the valuation parity in Lesson 17, proving the volume in (2.37). Equivalently, these formulas specify the measures directly.

Lemma 2.9 — the Euler orbital integrals. Pair the centralizer measures under the polynomial matching. If \(T\) is an elliptic centralizer, write \(v_T=\operatorname{vol}(T/Z)\), with that measure. Then \[ O_g(f_{\rm EP})=\frac1{v_T}=O_b(e') \quad(g\leftrightarrow b),\qquad O_g(f_{\rm EP})=0\quad(g\text{ split regular}). \tag{2.38} \] The quotients defining these orbital integrals are unchanged when the common centre is removed.

Proof. Right compact cosets for a vertex stabilizer are the vertices. Decomposing its orbital integral into \(T\)-orbits gives, for each vertex fixed by \(g\), the weight \(1/\operatorname{vol}(T_x/Z)\). For the signed edge function the analogous weight is \(\epsilon(g,a)/\operatorname{vol}(T_a/Z)\). This follows directly by integrating over the double coset \(T xK\), or \(T xJ\): its quotient volume is the compact subgroup volume divided by the centralizer stabilizer volume, and the normalized test cancels the former.

For elliptic \(g\) the invariant cells form a finite set by Theorem 2.7. Since \(T/Z\) is compact, an orbit of size \(d\) has weight \(d/v_T\). Their signed Euler sum is one. If vertices are fixed, they and their connecting edges form a nonempty finite tree, with one more vertex than edge. If no vertex is fixed, \(g\) exchanges the endpoints of its unique fixed edge; that edge contributes \(0-(-1)=1\). There cannot be two such edges, since their connecting path would force a fixed vertex. These are all cases, because \(g\) fixes a vertex or midpoint. Thus the first integral is \(1/v_T\). On \(H'\) the constant test gives \(\operatorname{vol}(H')/(\operatorname{vol}(H')v_T)=1/v_T\).

A split element with unequal eigenvalue absolute values fixes no vertex or midpoint and meets neither support. For a compact split element the fixed vertices are the tube of radius \(r=v(a/b-1)\) about its apartment, by (2.30). Its split centralizer \(T/Z\) preserves the apartment ends and acts transitively on its vertices and on its edges. Both stabilizers are the same compact unit subgroup \(T_0\); these two orbital weights cancel. Every vertex off the apartment has a unique parent toward it. Sending that vertex to its parent edge is a \(T\)-equivariant bijection between the off-apartment fixed vertices and edges, with equal stabilizers and equal weights. These cancel too. There are only finitely many orbits: a fundamental apartment vertex has finitely many branches of depth at most \(r\). Hence the argument is a finite cancellation, not subtraction of divergent orbital integrals. The split centralizer never inverts an apartment edge, and an edge off the apartment has its parent distinguished; all signs here are positive. This proves the second assertion. \(\square\)

We next prove the lift away from scalars. For a monic irreducible quadratic polynomial \(P(X)=X^2-tX+n\), including an inseparable one in characteristic two, put \[ S(t,n)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-n\\1&t\end{pmatrix},\qquad M(t,n;A,C)= \begin{pmatrix} A&(tA-A^2-n)/C\\ C&t-A \end{pmatrix}\quad(C\ne0). \tag{2.39} \] Every matrix with this polynomial has \(C\ne0\), since an upper triangular matrix would give a root in \(F\). Thus \((A,C)\in F\times F^\times\) parameterizes its entire orbit.

Use on its centralizer \(E^\times\), \(E=F[S]\), Haar measure \(|N(\alpha+\beta S)|^{-1}d\alpha\,d\beta\), and use the identical measure under the quaternion embedding. No division by the discriminant occurs in this choice. The exact orbital formula is \[ O_{S(t,n)}(f) =\kappa\int_{F\times F^\times} f(M(t,n;A,C))\,\frac{dA\,dC}{|C|}. \tag{2.40} \] To verify the measure, every group element has a unique factorization \(h(\alpha+\beta S)\), \(h=\begin{pmatrix}u&v\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(u\ne0\). Its bottom row determines \(\beta,\alpha\), and this element of \(E\) is nonzero, hence invertible. The multiplication map has additive Jacobian \(|N(\alpha+\beta S)|\), while its determinant is \(uN(\alpha+\beta S)\). Dividing the group Haar measure by the stated centralizer measure leaves \(\kappa|u|^{-2}du\,dv\). In \(hSh^{-1}\), \(C=1/u\), \(A=v/u\); the change of variables has absolute Jacobian \(|C|^{-3}\), yielding (2.40). These are algebraic identities in either characteristic.

Here is the quaternion-side local constancy needed in that lift. It concerns every nonscalar \(b\), even an inseparable one. The differentials of its trace and norm are \[ X\longmapsto \bigl(\operatorname{trd}X,\, \operatorname{trd}((t-b)X)\bigr). \tag{2.41} \] The reduced trace pairing is nondegenerate: after a splitting extension it is the matrix trace pairing, whose matrix-unit dual bases prove this assertion in every characteristic; nondegeneracy descends. The two linear forms in (2.41) are independent because \(b\) is nonscalar. Select two coordinate directions on which their matrix is invertible, and fix the other two coordinates. The trace-norm map then has a continuous local section \(b(t,n)\). One can prove this assertion directly: after applying the inverse linear matrix, its equations are a linear map plus a quadratic remainder. On a sufficiently small coordinate ball, the difference of the remainder has Lipschitz constant less than one. For parameter changes in the corresponding small ball, solving by successive approximation gives the unique section, continuously. No derivative of a quadratic root and no division by two is used.

The polynomial of a nonscalar quaternion is irreducible. A factorization over \(F\) would give \((b-a)(b-d)=0\), even for a repeated root, and force \(b\) scalar. Irreducibility is open also at an inseparable polynomial: roots of nearby quadratics, if any, lie in a common bounded ball by the quadratic equation; compactness of that ball would give a root of the limiting polynomial. The section can therefore be chosen over a neighborhood consisting entirely of irreducible polynomials.

Each such trace-norm fibre is one quaternion conjugacy class, without a separability restriction. For \(b,c\) with the same nonscalar polynomial, \(x=b+c-t\) satisfies \(xb=cx\); if nonzero it is invertible. If it is zero, use a nonzero commutator \(x=by-yb\), with \(y\) not commuting with \(b\). The polynomial identity gives \(xb+bx=tx\), so again \(xb=cx\). For \(c=b\) one can simply use \(x=1\).

The centralizer is \(F[b]^\times\). After a splitting extension, a nonscalar two-by-two matrix has two-dimensional centralizer by the cyclic-basis calculation. The subalgebra \(F[b]\) already has that dimension, so equality descends. Its projective group is compact. The following explicit volume formula also proves continuous local constancy for the varying centralizer measure: \[ v_T=(1-q^{-1})\left( \int_{\mathcal O}\frac{ds}{|1+ts+ns^2|} +\int_{\mathfrak p}\frac{ds}{|s^2+ts+n|} \right). \tag{2.42} \] The two projective charts represent \((\alpha,\beta)=(1,s)\), \(s\in\mathcal O\), and \((s,1)\), \(s\in\mathfrak p\); they partition \(\mathbb P^1(F)\). Writing the remaining scalar coordinate as \(z\), additive Jacobian \(|z|\) cancels one of the two norm powers, leaving \(dz/|z|\). The central normalization supplies \(1-q^{-1}\). Since the polynomial is irreducible, both denominators are nonzero on their compact chart domains. Their absolute values are uniformly unchanged by sufficiently small parameter changes. The volume is therefore finite, positive and locally constant, including at an inseparable quadratic.

For a compact smooth test \(f'\), integration over the compact group \(H'\) gives \[ O_{b(t,n)}(f') =\frac1{v_T}\int_{H'}f'(x^{-1}b(t,n)x)\,dx. \tag{2.43} \] The integrand is uniformly locally constant in \((t,n)\): cover the compact \(H'\) by finitely many neighborhoods and use smoothness of \(f'\) and continuity of the section. The numerator and (2.42) are locally constant. This proves the required assertion without a regular-character theorem.

If \(f'\) is supported away from the centre, its trace-norm image is a compact set of irreducible polynomials disjoint from the scalar curve. The function \(V(t,n)=O_{b(t,n)}(f')\) just proved is locally constant on the irreducible locus, supported in that compact set. Extend it by zero outside that locus. This extension is a compactly supported smooth function: the compact support lies in an open irreducible neighborhood. Choose \(\phi\in C_c^\infty(F\times F^\times)\) with \(\kappa\int\phi(A,C)dA\,dC/|C|=1\), and define \[ f_0\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D_0\end{pmatrix} =V(A+D_0,AD_0-BC)\phi(A,C)\quad(C\ne0), \tag{2.44} \] and zero otherwise. Its support is compact: \(A,C,t,n\) are bounded and \(C,n\) are bounded away from zero; (2.39) bounds the remaining entries. It is smooth also across \(C=0\), since \(\phi\) is supported away from zero. Formula (2.40) gives precisely \(O_g(f_0)=V(t,n)\). Its split orbital integrals vanish because \(V\) is zero there, and it vanishes at every scalar matrix. For example \(\phi=(1-q^{-2})1_{\mathcal O\times\mathcal O^\times}\) has the required integral with the present \(\kappa\).

Theorem 2.10 — finite-place smooth transfer, proved. With the measures above, every \(f'\in C_c^\infty(D^\times)\) has an \(f\in C_c^\infty(G)\) such that \[ \begin{aligned} O_g(f)&=O_b(f')&&\text{on matching regular elliptic classes},\\ O_g(f)&=0&&\text{on regular split classes},\\ f(zI)&=-f'(z)&&\text{for every }z\in F^\times. \end{aligned} \tag{2.45} \] This construction holds for every nonarchimedean local field, in either characteristic.

Proof. The scalar curve in the trace-norm plane is \(z\mapsto(2z,z^2)\), \(z\ne0\). It is closed and is a proper homeomorphism onto its image. For characteristic different from two the first coordinate supplies its inverse; for characteristic two, \(|z-w|^2=|z^2-w^2|\) supplies continuity of the inverse. Bounds on the nonzero norm give properness. Hence the compact smooth function \(c(z)=f'(z)/e'(1)\) extends to some \(a\in C_c^\infty(F\times F^\times)\). To see the extension without a general topological theorem, cover the compact scalar support by finitely many compact open parameter neighborhoods on which \(c\) has a single value and which miss scalar points with other values. Refine their overlaps to disjoint compact open pieces. Set that value there and zero elsewhere. Closedness and properness of the scalar curve ensure that these neighborhoods can be chosen compact and avoid its zero-value part.

Pull the projective functions of (2.37) back to the full groups, and put \[ F'(b)=a(\operatorname{trd}b,\operatorname{nrd}b)e'([b]), \qquad F(g)=a(\operatorname{tr}g,\det g)f_{\rm EP}([g]). \tag{2.46} \] Both have compact support. Their projective supports are compact, and the norm cutoff bounds the scalar valuation in both directions; the remaining central units are compact. They are smooth. Multiplication by the class-invariant cutoff preserves (2.38), and (2.37) gives \(F(zI)=-F'(z)\). By construction \(F'(z)=f'(z)\).

The compact smooth remainder \(r'=f'-F'\) vanishes on an open neighborhood of the centre. Its compact support is therefore disjoint from the centre. Apply (2.44) to it, obtaining \(r\) with the matching elliptic integrals, zero split integrals and zero scalar values. Then \(f=F+r\) proves all three identities in (2.45). In characteristic two, the remainder's support may contain nonscalar inseparable elements; (2.41)–(2.43) explicitly include them, so no support has been discarded. \(\square\)

Lemma 2.11 — the scalar orbital germ and the value of every matching pair. Fix \(z\in F^\times\), and put \(u_z=\begin{pmatrix}z&0\\1&z\end{pmatrix}\). Continue to use the centralizer measure with basis \(1,g\) from (2.40). For each compact smooth test \(f\), all sufficiently close regular parameters \((t,n)\) to \((2z,z^2)\) satisfy \[ O_g(f)=O_{u_z}(f)+\Gamma_z(g)f(zI),\qquad \Gamma_z(g)= \begin{cases} 0,&g\text{ split},\\ -\operatorname{vol}(H')/v_{G_g},&g\text{ elliptic}. \end{cases} \tag{2.47} \] The first term is the nontrivial unipotent orbital integral; its coefficient is exactly one in this normalization. In particular, every matching pair with zero regular split orbital integrals satisfies \(f(zI)=-f'(z)\).

Proof. First extend (2.40) to every nonscalar cyclic class, including a repeated-root class. The factorization \(G=hF[g]^\times\) used there misses only matrices whose bottom-row norm is zero. For a split or repeated-root quadratic these rows lie in at most two proper linear subspaces and have additive measure zero. This set is right-centralizer invariant; the quotient integration formula therefore gives it orbital measure zero as well. The same Jacobian computation applies on the complement. Thus (2.40) remains valid.

An orbital integral of a compact smooth test supported away from the scalar matrices is locally constant in \((t,n)\), even at a repeated-root parameter. Indeed every nonscalar matrix has a conjugate with lower-left entry nonzero. Cover the compact support by finitely many such fixed conjugate charts, and partition the test by compact open subsets on which that entry is bounded away from zero. In each chart (2.40) integrates a locally constant function over a fixed compact domain in \((A,C)\). Smoothness is uniform there. Conjugation preserves the integral and the centralizer normalization. This proves the assertion also at \(u_z\).

Choose \(m\) large enough that \(U=zI+\mathfrak p^mM_2(\mathcal O)\) lies in \(G\) and \(f\) is constant on \(U\). Multiply by a compact open trace-norm cutoff equal to one near \((2z,z^2)\). The cutoff can be chosen so that all scalar points in its support lie in \(U\), by the proper inverse of the scalar curve used in Theorem 2.10. The remainder after subtracting \(f(zI)1_U\) then has compact support away from all scalars. Its orbital integral is constant near this parameter and equals its value at \(u_z\).

Here is the remaining integral, with all constants visible. Write \(t=2z+s\), \(n=z^2+zs+d\), and take \(s\in\mathfrak p^m\), \(d\in\mathfrak p^{2m}\). Set \(S=s/\varpi^m\), \(N=d/\varpi^{2m}\). Substituting \(A=z+\varpi^m a\), \(C=\varpi^m c\) into (2.39), membership in \(U\) is equivalent to \(a,c\in\mathcal O\), \(c\ne0\), and \(v(a^2-Sa+N)\ge v(c)\). Each shell for \(c\) has \(dc/|c|\)-volume \(1-q^{-1}\), while \(dA=q^{-m}da\). Consequently \[ \begin{aligned} O_g(1_U)&=\kappa q^{-m}(1-q^{-1}) \int_{\mathcal O}\bigl(1+v(a^2-Sa+N)\bigr)\,da,\\ \Gamma_z(g)&=\kappa q^{-m}(1-q^{-1}) \int_{\mathcal O}\bigl(v(a^2-Sa+N)-2v(a)\bigr)\,da. \end{aligned} \tag{2.48} \] At \(u_z\) the polynomial is \(a^2\). All these integrals are finite. For an irreducible polynomial its value is nonzero on the compact domain; for split polynomials each linear-factor valuation is integrable. Values at the finitely many zeros do not affect the integrals. The same observations justify summing the shells by monotone integration.

For a nearby split polynomial its two roots \(\alpha,\beta\) lie in \(\mathcal O\). Translation invariance and the residue balls give \(\int_{\mathcal O}v(a-\alpha)\,da=\sum_{j\ge1}q^{-j}=1/(q-1)\), and likewise for \(\beta\). Thus the second line of (2.48) is zero on split classes, in every characteristic. The germ is independent of an eligible \(m\): the difference of two corresponding indicators vanishes near the centre, so its orbital integral is constant and equals its unipotent value.

To identify the elliptic germ, multiply the projective Euler test by a trace-norm cutoff equal to one near this scalar parameter. Its scalar value is \(-(q-1)/2=-1/\operatorname{vol}(H')\). Its split orbital integrals vanish by Lemma 2.9. The already proved expansion and the split value of the germ force its unipotent integral to be zero. On elliptic classes Lemma 2.9 gives orbital integral \(1/v_{G_g}\). Substitution into the expansion yields exactly the elliptic value in (2.47).

Finally let \(f,f'\) be any matching pair with zero split integrals. The split part of (2.47) forces \(O_{u_z}(f)=0\). Compactness of \(H'\) implies that, for every quaternion \(b\) sufficiently close to \(z\), its entire conjugacy orbit lies in the neighborhood where \(f'\) equals \(f'(z)\). Hence on matching nearby elliptic classes, \[ -\frac{\operatorname{vol}(H')}{v_{G_g}}f(zI) =O_g(f)=O_b(f') =\frac{\operatorname{vol}(H')}{v_{G_g}}f'(z). \tag{2.49} \] There are arbitrarily close such classes: take \(b=z+\varpi^k x\) with a nonscalar integral generator \(x\) of the unramified quadratic subfield. The volume factor is finite and positive, and cancels. This proves the scalar identity for every matching pair. No division by two, separability of the parameter polynomial, or characteristic-zero germ theorem was used. \(\square\)

Proposition 2.12 — the special formal degree. For these paired measures and every unitary \(\chi\), \[ d\bigl(\mathrm{St}\otimes(\chi\circ\det)\bigr) =\frac{q-1}{2} =d(\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd}). \tag{2.50} \]

Proof. Steinberg unitarity and square-integrability were proved in Lesson 7, Section 7. In its boundary model \(C^\infty(\mathbb P^1(F))/\mathbb C\), an oriented edge \(a\) determines the class \(v_a\) of the indicator of the ends on its terminal side. Reversing orientation gives \(-v_a\); the outward indicators at a vertex sum to the constant one, so their classes sum to zero. The two endpoint-fixing Iwahori orbits on the ends give an invariant space of dimension two before removing constants, hence one after. The full edge stabilizer acts on that line by its orientation sign.

Normalize the vector for a base edge to length one. The stabilizer is transitive on the edges at each distance \(l\ge1\) from it, after allowing endpoint exchange: there are \(2q^l\) such edges. This transitivity can be checked with primitive row coordinates. Fixing a finite initial lattice path, the appropriate upper or lower root subgroup varies the next residue digit arbitrarily; induction reaches each of its \(q\) forward branches. The edge flip exchanges the two initial sides. The outward sum relation at the first endpoint gives coefficient absolute value \(1/q\) on the first sphere. At every subsequent endpoint it gives the preceding coefficient divided by \(q\): the path stabilizer fixes the base vector and permutes its \(q\) forward branches. Orientation may change the sign, but \(|\langle\mathrm{St}(g)v,v\rangle|=q^{-l}\) when the base edge and its translate have distance \(l\).

Each right \(J\)-coset has volume \(2/(q+1)\). This proves (2.50). \(\square\)

The following argument proves a complete family of the finite local correspondence in every residue characteristic. It includes both the bijection and the negative character identity, rather than just a comparison of dimensions. Theorem 2.30 includes positive-level representations that cannot be reduced to this family by a norm or determinant twist.

Use the local model of Lesson 17, equation (1.2), and write \(\Pi=j\), \(k_2=\mathcal O_L/\mathfrak p_L\), and \[ U_D^1=1+\Pi\mathcal O_D,\qquad H_D=F^\times\mathcal O_D^\times,\qquad K^1=1+\mathfrak pM_2(\mathcal O). \tag{2.52} \] The group \(U_D^1\) is normal in \(D^\times\). Lemma 2.13 — every division-side depth-zero representation. Let \(\theta:k_2^\times\to\mathbb C^\times\) be regular, meaning \(\theta\ne\theta^q\), and let \(A\in\mathbb C^\times\). Extend \(\theta\) to \(H_D\) by \(\widetilde\theta(\varpi^r u)=A^r\theta(\bar u)\), and put \(T_{\theta,A}=\operatorname{Ind}_{H_D}^{D^\times}\widetilde\theta\). It is irreducible of dimension two. Its isomorphism class is determined by \(A\) and the unordered pair \(\{\theta,\theta^q\}\). The other depth-zero irreducibles are exactly \(\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd}\) with \(\chi\) trivial on \(1+\mathfrak p\).

Proof. The decomposition defining \(\widetilde\theta\) is unique, since \(v_D(\varpi)=2\), and the residue character already prescribes scalar-unit values. The subgroup \(H_D\) has index two. In the two coset copies, its unit and uniformizer actions can be written \[ T_{\theta,A}(u)= \begin{pmatrix}\theta(\bar u)&0\\0&\theta(\bar u^q)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T_{\theta,A}(\Pi)=\begin{pmatrix}0&A\\1&0\end{pmatrix}. \tag{2.53} \] The relations above verify this representation directly. Finite-character averaging projects onto its two distinct unit-character lines, and \(\Pi\) exchanges them. Any nonzero invariant subspace therefore contains both lines. The same lines and the central value at \(\varpi\) prove the stated uniqueness.

Conversely, the division-side argument following Proposition 2.8, or Lesson 17, Theorem 1.1, makes an irreducible smooth representation finite-dimensional. If it is trivial on \(U_D^1\), decompose its units under the finite abelian group \(k_2^\times\). The operator \(\Pi\) exchanges Frobenius-related eigenspaces and its square is the central scalar \(A\). Irreducibility leaves one Frobenius orbit. For an orbit of length two, a vector and its \(\Pi\)-translate span a stable two-dimensional space, giving (2.53). For a fixed character, \(\Pi\) acts on one weight space and satisfies \(X^2-A\). Its two distinct nonzero complex roots give an invariant line, so irreducibility forces dimension one.

If its \(\Pi\)-value is \(c\), define the tame character \(\chi\) by \(\chi|_{\mathcal O^\times}=\eta\) on residues and \(\chi(\varpi)=c/\eta(-1)\). Theorem 2.14 — the complete depth-zero local bijection. The assignments \(T_{\theta,A}\mapsto P_{\theta,A}\) and \(\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd}\mapsto\mathrm{St}\otimes(\chi\circ\det)\) give a bijection from division-side depth-zero irreducibles onto split-side depth-zero essentially discrete series. They preserve the whole central character and have the negative character relation on every matching regular elliptic class. For unitary representations their formal degrees agree in the paired measures (2.37).

Proof of construction and exhaustion. Lesson 10, Theorem 3.1, proves that \(P_{\theta,A}\) is irreducible supercuspidal; its proof uses the finite cuspidal table and unipotent averaging, including \(q=2\). The cross-type Cartan calculation and finite Schur orthogonality distinguish precisely the unordered parameters above. Lemma 7.6 and the level-zero case of Theorem 7.8 there prove that these exhaust the supercuspidals with \(K^1\)-fixed vectors. The whole split-side discrete-series list is Lesson 7's special/supercuspidal list. The special twists with \(K^1\)-invariants are exactly the tame twists: the subgroup \(K^1\cap\mathrm{SL}_2(F)\) acts transitively on each residue ball of \(\mathbb P^1(F)\), using the upper or lower root subgroup with parameter in \(\mathfrak p\). Its invariant space in the boundary model is therefore the \(q+1\) residue indicators modulo constants. The action of \(K^1\) on this space is trivial, so a determinant character can give an invariant only if it kills \(\det K^1=1+\mathfrak p\). Exactness of compact averaging justifies taking this quotient invariant space. This proves exhaustion on both sides. The dimensions separate the norm and regular families on the division side, while special and supercuspidal representations are distinct on the split side. Both regular models have central values \(A\) at \(\varpi\) and \(\theta(a)\) at a residue scalar \(a\).

Proof of the full elliptic character identity. Proposition 2.8 identifies the character of the compact induction with the finite sum over fixed vertices: \(ZK\backslash G\) indexes the lattice vertices, and the diagonal block at a fixed vertex is \(\rho_\theta\) of the integral action there. Take a matching pair \(g\leftrightarrow b\). If \(v_D(b)=v_F(\det g)\) is odd, no conjugate of \(g\) lies in \(ZK\), whose determinant valuations are even. The split character is zero. Equation (2.53) makes the division character zero on the odd coset as well.

If the valuation is \(2r\), scale both elements by \(\varpi^{-r}\). Then \(b\) is a unit, and let \(\lambda\in k_2^\times\) be its residue. The common reduced polynomial is integral and has unit norm. On the split side it preserves an integral lattice. Its fixed vertices form a nonempty finite tree by the elliptic fixed-set argument of Theorem 2.7; the even determinant valuation prevents edge inversion. At any fixed vertex, fixed adjacent vertices correspond exactly to eigenlines of the reduction on its two-dimensional residue lattice.

If \(\lambda\notin k\), the residue polynomial is irreducible. There is no such eigenline, so the connected fixed tree is one vertex. The finite character there is \(-\theta(\lambda)-\theta(\lambda^q)\), whereas (2.53) gives its opposite on the division side. If \(\lambda\in k^\times\), every residue action has that repeated eigenvalue. It is either scalar, with all \(q+1\) neighbors fixed, or a nontrivial Jordan block, with exactly one neighbor fixed. Write \(i\) and \(l\) for the numbers of these interior vertices and leaves. 2.6. The formal degree of every compact-induction model

Let \(F\) be any nonarchimedean local field, \(G=\mathrm{GL}_2(F)\), \(Z=F^\times\), and normalize Haar measure on \(G/Z\) by \(\operatorname{vol}(ZK/Z)=1\), \(K=\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathcal O_F)\). Let \(J\supseteq Z\) be open and compact modulo \(Z\), and let \(\lambda\) be a finite-dimensional irreducible smooth representation of \(J\), with unitary central character \(\omega\). Assume \[ \pi=\mathrm{c\!-\!Ind}_J^G\lambda \] is irreducible and admissible. These hypotheses are proved for every supercuspidal in Lesson 10, Theorems 2.1 and 7.8. The inducing subgroup may be \(ZK\) or \(E^\times U_{\mathfrak A}^{l}\); it is not restricted to the depth-zero family or odd residue characteristic. Inner products are linear in the first variable.

The representation \(\lambda\) can be made unitary: average any positive definite Hermitian form over \(J/Z\). The integrand is independent of the representative because \(|\omega|=1\); right translation preserves the compact quotient Haar measure. Irreducibility is unchanged.

Put \(d=\dim\lambda\) and \(v_J=\operatorname{vol}(J/Z)\). The Hilbert model is the space of functions \(u:G\to V_\lambda\) with \(u(jg)=\lambda(j)u(g)\) and \[ \|u\|^2=\sum_{Jx\in J\backslash G}\|u(x)\|^2<\infty. \] Right translation is unitary. The finite-support smooth subspace is the original compact induction. Admissibility ensures that its compact-open fixed spaces remain the same finite-dimensional spaces in the completion: averaging the dense smooth subspace has that finite-dimensional closed image. If a nonzero closed invariant subspace exists, sufficiently small compact-open averaging produces a nonzero vector in one of those fixed spaces. Smooth irreducibility then includes the whole dense smooth representation, so the closed subspace is the full Hilbert space. Thus this is an irreducible unitary completion.

Theorem 2.16 — every compact-induction formal degree. In the preceding hypotheses, the unitary representation has formal degree \(d_\pi=\dim\lambda/\operatorname{vol}(J/Z)\). In particular this computes the degree of every supercuspidal in Lesson 10, Theorem 7.8, in every residue characteristic.

Proof.

For \(w\in V_\lambda\), let \(f_w\) be supported on \(J\), with \(f_w(j)=\lambda(j)w\). Its norm is \(\|w\|\). For any vector \(u\) of the Hilbert model, \[ \langle\pi(g)u,f_w\rangle=\langle u(g),w\rangle. \tag{2.58} \] Finite-dimensional projective Schur averaging on \(J/Z\) gives \[ \int_{J/Z}|\langle\lambda(j)a,w\rangle|^2\,dj =\frac{v_J}{d}\|a\|^2\|w\|^2. \tag{2.59} \] Here conjugation of a rank-one operator by \(\lambda(j)\) is independent of the central representative. Its average commutes with \(\lambda(J)\), hence is scalar by finite-dimensional Schur; its trace supplies exactly \(1/d\). This proves (2.59) and fixes its constant.

The quotient \(G/Z\) is the disjoint union of the open sets \((J/Z)x\). Their measures use the same \(dj\), because \(G/Z\) is unimodular and right translation preserves Haar measure. Applying (2.58) and (2.59), with the sum justified by nonnegative integration, gives for every Hilbert vector \(u\) \[ \int_{G/Z}|\langle\pi(g)u,f_w\rangle|^2\,dg =\frac{v_J}{d}\|u\|^2\|w\|^2. \tag{2.60} \] Thus the coefficient map with a base-coset test vector is bounded on the entire completion, with its exact norm. No closed-graph or unbounded-intertwiner assertion is needed.

Let \(z\) now be any finite-support smooth vector. It is a finite sum of translates of base-coset vectors, so \[ T_z u(g)=\langle\pi(g)u,z\rangle \] is a bounded map to the central-character \(L^2\) space, by (2.60) and the triangle inequality. It intertwines right translation. Consequently \(A_z=T_z^*T_z\) commutes with \(\pi(G)\). To justify scalarity directly, \(A_z\) preserves every compact-open fixed space. Choose one nonzero such finite-dimensional space and an eigenvector of \(A_z\) there. The eigenspace on the smooth representation is nonzero and invariant under \(G\), so smooth irreducibility makes it the whole smooth representation. Density and boundedness give \(A_z=c_z I\) on the Hilbert completion.

For a base-coset vector \(f_a\), unitarity and inversion, which preserves Haar measure on \(G/Z\), show \[ \begin{aligned} c_z\|a\|^2 &=\int_{G/Z}|\langle\pi(g)f_a,z\rangle|^2\,dg\\ &=\int_{G/Z}|\langle\pi(g)z,f_a\rangle|^2\,dg =\frac{v_J}{d}\|z\|^2\|a\|^2. \end{aligned} \tag{2.61} \] Thus \(c_z=(v_J/d)\|z\|^2\). This uniform norm bound extends the coefficient maps continuously from the dense finite-support test vectors to every Hilbert test vector. On compact sets their pointwise coefficients also converge uniformly, by the unitary Cauchy–Schwarz bound, so the extension is the actual coefficient function. Polarizing (2.60)–(2.61) in the two vectors proves the full mixed Schur relation and identifies the positive formal degree as \[ d_\pi=\frac{\dim\lambda}{\operatorname{vol}(J/Z)}. \tag{2.62} \] All changes of measure are explicit: multiplying \(dg\) by \(c>0\) multiplies \(v_J\) by \(c\) and divides \(d_\pi\) by \(c\).

\(\square\)

Corollary 2.17 — the paired compact-type degree identity. For the paired division measure, every unitary division representation has degree \((q-1)\dim T/2\). Thus equality for a proposed compact-induction pair is exactly (2.64) below.

Proof.

For every irreducible representation \(T\) of the quaternion division group with unitary central character, Lesson 17, Theorem 1.1 and the same finite-dimensional averaging give \[ d_T=\frac{\dim T}{\operatorname{vol}(D^\times/Z)}. \] In the paired measures (2.37), \[ \operatorname{vol}(D^\times/Z)=\frac2{q-1},\qquad d_T=\frac{q-1}{2}\dim T. \tag{2.63} \] Therefore for a proposed positive-level pair \((\mathrm{c\!-\!Ind}_J^G\lambda,T)\), degree equality is exactly the arithmetic identity \[ 2\dim\lambda=(q-1)\operatorname{vol}(J/Z)\dim T. \tag{2.64} \] This equivalence alone does not construct \(T\) or prove its character relation; Theorem 2.30 establishes the bijection and equality for every corresponding pair. For depth zero, \(J=ZK\), \(\dim\lambda=q-1\), and \(\dim T=2\), so it recovers the proved equality \(d_\pi=q-1=d_T\).

Every unitary determinant or norm twist leaves coefficient absolute values unchanged, hence preserves the degrees. If an essentially unitary compact type has nonunitary central character, its absolute value is trivial on scalar units and is determined by the scalar uniformizer. An unramified positive determinant twist can cancel that value, since the determinant of a scalar uniformizer has valuation two. This supplies a unitary representative. Formula (2.62) applies to that representative; it does not attach a positive Hilbert formal degree to a nonunitary realization.

\(\square\)

Proposition 2.18 — the two positive-level subgroup volumes. In the compact-induction exhaustion of Lesson 10, Lemmas 7.6–7.7, write \(J=E^\times U_{\mathfrak A}^l\), \(l\ge1\). Use the same normalization \(\operatorname{vol}(ZK/Z)=1\). Then \[ \begin{array}{c|c|c} \mathfrak A,E&\operatorname{vol}(J/Z)&d_\pi\\ \hline M_2(\mathcal O),\ E/F\text{ unramified} &\dfrac{q^{1-2l}}{q-1} &(q-1)q^{2l-1}\dim\lambda\\[5pt] \mathfrak I,\ E/F\text{ ramified} &\dfrac{2q^{1-l}}{q^2-1} &\dfrac{(q^2-1)q^{l-1}}2\dim\lambda. \end{array} \tag{2.65} \] The formulas include residue characteristic two. The pointwise edge stabilizer \(ZI/Z\) has volume \(1/(q+1)\), since its vertex stabilizer acts transitively on the \(q+1\) adjacent edges. 2.7. Real smooth transfer, including the scalar value

The calculation in Lemma 2.4 was initially made on the regular elliptic set. We now identify the character distribution on arbitrary tests, construct smooth transfers with common compact support, and prove the scalar identity for every matching pair.

First remove the positive unitary central character by the twist in (2.10), and let \(\kappa\in\{0,1\}\) be its parity at \(-I\). Tests transform by \(\omega^{-1}\) under the centre and are compactly supported modulo it. Keep the measures of (2.23)–(2.24): each component of \(G/Z\) has probability compact angular measures and radial density \(\sinh u\,du\); \(D^\times/Z\) has volume \(2\). Give matching compact centralizers \(T/Z\) the same volume \(v_T\). One may set \(v_T=1\); retaining it clarifies the scalar calculation. Restore the positive central twist afterwards.

Lemma 2.19 — the real character distribution across the singular set. The ladder characters in Lemma 2.4 are locally integrable on the entire group, and give the trace on every smooth compact test. On \(a_u=\operatorname{diag}(e^{u/2},e^{-u/2})\), \(u>0\), each ladder has character \[ \Theta_k^\pm(a_u)=\frac{e^{-(k-1)u/2}}{2\sinh(u/2)}. \tag{2.66} \] The elliptic values remain (2.16). A negative scalar multiplies the character by \((-1)^k\). For the full \(D_k\), add the ladders on the positive-determinant component and use zero on the other component.

Proof. Use \(R_r\), its fixed point and trace formula (2.17)–(2.19). Write the matrix inducing the inverse disk action as \[ \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\\bar b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |a|^2-|b|^2=1,\qquad a=x+iy. \] The weighted symbol of \(R_r\sigma_k(g)\) comes from this matrix with its first column multiplied by \(r\). At its interior fixed point \(z=z_{r,g}\), put \(\lambda=r\bar b z+\bar a\). The eigenvalues of that matrix are \(\lambda,r/\lambda\). Thus \[ \begin{aligned} \lambda^2-(ra+\bar a)\lambda+r&=0,\\ \operatorname{tr}(R_r\sigma_k(g)) &=\frac{\lambda^{1-k}}{\lambda-r/\lambda},\\ \Delta_r&=((1+r)x-i(1-r)y)^2-4r. \end{aligned} \tag{2.67} \] The fixed-point disk identity gives \[ |\lambda|^2=\frac{1-r^2|z|^2}{1-|z|^2}\ge1. \] Therefore the absolute trace is at most \(|\Delta_r|^{-1/2}\).

Put \(p=1+r,q=1-r,X=x^2,Y=y^2\). Direct expansion gives \[ |\Delta_r|^2=(p^2X+q^2Y-4r)^2+16rq^2Y. \tag{2.68} \] On a fixed compact set \(Y\le M\), and \(X+Y\ge1\). For \(X\ge1\), the first parenthesis is \(p^2(X-1)+q^2(1+Y)\), so its absolute value is at least \(p^2(X-1)\). For \(X<1\), write \(d=1-X\); then \(Y\ge d\). Either \(q^2(1+Y)\le p^2d/2\), giving absolute value at least \(p^2d/2\) in the first parenthesis, or \(q^2Y\ge p^2d^2/(2(1+M))\), and the second summand supplies the bound. Uniformly for \(r\ge1/2\), \[ |\operatorname{tr}(R_r\sigma_k(g))| \le C_M|x^2-1|^{-1/2} \tag{2.69} \] off a Haar-null set. The constant is independent of \(r\).

This majorant is locally integrable. At a noncentral unipotent element, \(x\) is a submersion coordinate; its transverse inverse square root is integrable. Near either scalar, use \((\Re b,\Im b,y)\) and solve for \(x\) with its fixed sign. The exact identity \(x^2-1=|b|^2-y^2\) reduces the question to a quadratic cone. In polar coordinates the majorant is \(\rho^{-1}|Q(\vartheta)|^{-1/2}\), and the volume factor is \(\rho^2\,d\rho\,d\vartheta\). The angular zero set of the nondegenerate quadratic form is smooth on the sphere; its transverse inverse square root is integrable. The radial integral is finite as well.

The elliptic limit was proved in Lemma 2.4. At a split element the fixed point tends to the attracting boundary fixed point and its multiplier tends to \(e^{u/2}\), giving (2.66). Conjugation gives the same split-class value; negative scalars introduce their weight sign. Dominated convergence by (2.69), together with the trace-norm convergence in (2.19)–(2.20), now identifies the distribution on every test. There is no additional singular-supported distribution. Complex conjugation gives the second ladder. On the full group the other component exchanges the ladders and has zero diagonal trace after smoothing too. \(\square\)

The principal trace on arbitrary tests follows directly from the compact induced model of Lesson 11, (3.1). Its smoothed kernel on the circle of real lines is obtained by integrating the test over the stabilizer of a line, and is smooth. Its diagonal integral is the trace. At a regular matrix the diagonal condition selects its two real eigenlines in the split case and none in the elliptic case. The Jacobian at an eigenline is \(|1-t_1/t_2|\), with the half-density already present in normalized induction. Hence \[ \Theta_{I_\epsilon(s)}(\pm a_u) =(\pm1)^\epsilon \frac{e^{su/2}+e^{-su/2}}{2|\sinh(u/2)|}, \qquad u\ne0, \tag{2.70} \] and the character is zero on regular elliptic classes.

There is no omission at repeated eigenvalues in this kernel calculation. Parameterize the stabilizer by its diagonal ratio and unipotent coordinate before taking the line integral. The map from a group element and a line to its image line is a submersion: an infinitesimal lower unipotent moves any line in a suitable affine chart. The diagonal integral is consequently a smooth-kernel integral with no singular-supported term. Changing variables to the eigenline solutions gives (2.70) almost everywhere. In these diagonal-kernel coordinates the repeated-eigenvalue locus has diagonal ratio one, a codimension-one set of measure zero in the line-times-stabilizer integral; its pushforward contributes no singular measure. Absolute convergence is also verified by the quadratic-cone calculation: on a compact set the character is bounded by a constant times the inverse square root of the discriminant. This proves the formula on arbitrary tests. It holds for complex \(s\), since the compact test integral is entire in that parameter. The full-group eigenline calculation on the other component is identical in principle and is supported on split classes there too.

Lemma 2.20 — the real scalar germ for every matching pair. If \(f,f'\) have equal orbital integrals on matching regular elliptic classes, and all regular split orbital integrals of \(f\) vanish, then \[ f'(z)=-f(zI)\qquad(z\in\mathbb R^\times). \tag{2.71} \]

Proof. Work first at \(1\). In the traceless real algebra choose coordinates with determinant \(z_0^2-x_0^2-y_0^2\), and compact generator \((0,0,1)\). Its full-group conjugates are \((\sinh u\cos\phi,\sinh u\sin\phi,\pm\cosh u)\). The two signs come from the two components; the quotient measure on either sheet is \(v_T^{-1}\sinh u\,du\,d\phi/(2\pi)\), by the Cartan normalization.

Denote the angular average of \(f(cI+X)\) by \(F(c,\rho^2,z_0)\), where \(\rho^2=x_0^2+y_0^2\). It is smooth in \(\rho^2\ge0\): at \(\rho=0\), Taylor expansion removes the odd linear term on averaging and bounds the second-derivative remainder; applying the same argument to derivatives gives the smooth radial assertion. A compact smooth extension to a neighborhood of the determinant-one section can be used for these estimates.

For \(s=\sin\theta>0,c=\cos\theta\), change variables \(t=s\cosh u\). This gives exactly \[ O_{r_\theta}(f)=\frac1{v_Ts} \int_s^\infty [F(c,t^2-s^2,t)+F(c,t^2-s^2,-t)]\,dt. \tag{2.72} \] Compact support on the determinant-one section bounds \(t\) uniformly for small \(\theta\). Write \(I_0=\int_0^\infty[F(1,t^2,t)+F(1,t^2,-t)]\,dt\). On \(t\ge s\), the change in the integrand is \(O(s^2)\), by radial smoothness and \(c-1=O(s^2)\). The omitted interval has integral \(2s f(1)+O(s^2)\). Therefore \[ O_{r_\theta}(f)=\frac{I_0}{v_T\sin\theta} -\frac{2f(1)}{v_T}+O(\theta). \tag{2.73} \]

A positive split orbit of radius \(h=\sinh(u/2)\) has \(\rho^2=z_0^2+h^2\). Its fixed invariant orbit measure is a nonzero positive constant times \(h^{-1}dz_0\,d\phi\). To see the radius factor, write Euclidean volume as \(\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi\,dz_0=\tfrac12\,d(\rho^2-z_0^2)\,d\phi\,dz_0\). Scaling the orbit coordinates by \(h\) scales this coarea measure by \(h\), while the quotient measure on the conjugating group is fixed; the latter must therefore be a fixed multiple of \(h^{-1}dz_0\,d\phi\). Invariance determines the constant, independently of \(h\). Thus \(h\,O_{a_u}(f)\) tends to a nonzero constant times \(I_0\). Vanishing of split orbital integrals forces \(I_0=0\).

On the compact quaternion side every conjugate of \(\cos\theta+\sin\theta\,i\) tends uniformly to \(1\), and its orbital measure has total volume \(2/v_T\). Its limiting orbital integral is \(2f'(1)/v_T\). Equate this with (2.73), now with \(I_0=0\), to obtain \(f'(1)=-f(1)\). Central translation proves the assertion at every scalar; centrally covariant tests acquire the same scalar factor on both sides. \(\square\)

Lemma 2.21 — compact smooth tests selecting adjacent real weights. For each \(k\ge2\) of parity \(\kappa\), there is a centrally covariant smooth test \(p_k\), supported in the positive-determinant component, such that \[ \operatorname{tr}D_n(p_k)=\delta_{nk},\qquad \operatorname{tr}I_\kappa(s)(p_k)=0. \tag{2.74} \] All \(p_k\) have one common compact support modulo the centre. For each smooth derivative \(D\), \(\|Dp_k\|_\infty\le C_D(1+k)^{N_D}\), with constants independent of \(k\).

Proof. The real ladder operators of Lesson 11 satisfy \[ \Omega=H^2-2H+4EF,\qquad E^*=-F,\qquad A=-\Omega-1. \] On weight \(j\), \[ A_j=4F_j^*F_j-(j-1)^2, \tag{2.75} \] where \(F_j\) lowers the weight by two. This is elliptic on the homogeneous line bundle over the hyperbolic plane. Its positive principal symbol is the hyperbolic metric, up to a fixed scale; its connection and potential coefficients on fixed charts are polynomial in \(j\). The factorization gives \(A_j\ge-(j-1)^2\).

We give the analytic construction needed here. The shifted positive quadratic form on compact smooth sections gives a self-adjoint Friedrichs operator. Completeness of the hyperbolic plane makes the extension unique: multiply a deficiency solution by radial cutoffs with gradient tending to zero and use integration by parts. The real potential is bounded below, and the cutoff errors tend to zero; the positive shifted energy then forces that solution to vanish. The wave equation \(\partial_t^2v+A_jv=0\) has propagation speed bounded independently of \(j\). Integrate its energy outside an expanding metric ball. The principal boundary flux is nonpositive at the metric propagation speed. Connection terms preserve that principal flux, while a sufficiently large \(j\)-dependent multiple of \(|v|^2\) absorbs the potential terms. Gronwall's inequality implies that initially zero exterior energy stays zero. Approximation gives the same support assertion for distributional initial data. The energy constant may depend on \(j\); the speed does not.

Let \(e_j\) be the probability compact-group idempotent selecting weight \(j\). The wave kernel with initial value \(e_j\) is the convolution distribution \(e_j\cos(t\sqrt A)\). For a negative spectral value this denotes the entire function \(\cosh(t\sqrt{-A})\). Its support projects into a metric ball of radius a fixed constant times \(|t|\).

Choose \(R=3\) and nonzero nonnegative \(b\in C_c^\infty((1,2))\). Set \[ \begin{aligned} \phi_k(t)&=b(k(R-|t|)),\\ \Phi_k(a)&=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi_k(t)\cos(t\sqrt a)\,dt,\\ B_k&=\Phi_k(-(k-1)^2)>0,\\ p_k&=B_k^{-1}(e_k-e_{k-2})\Phi_k(A). \end{aligned} \tag{2.76} \] The last expression denotes its convolution kernel. The time bump is smooth and supported where \(R-2/k<|t|<R-1/k\), away from zero.

For \(a\ge0\), integration by parts \(2N\) times bounds the time integral using \(\|\phi_k^{(2N)}\|_1\le C_Nk^{2N-1}\). For \(-(k-1)^2\le a\le0\), use \(|\cos(t\sqrt a)|\le e^{R(k-1)}\). Together these give \[ \sup_{a\ge-(k-1)^2} (1+|a|)^N|\Phi_k(a)| \le C_Nk^{2N+1}e^{R(k-1)},\qquad B_k\ge c\,k^{-1}e^{R(k-1)}. \tag{2.77} \] For the lower bound substitute \(v=k(R-t)\) in the positive-time integral. On the support of \(b\), \(e^{-(k-1)v/k}\ge e^{-2}\), and \(\cosh((k-1)t)\ge \tfrac12e^{(k-1)t}\). The constants are independent of \(k\). Both weights \(k\) and \(k-2\) have spectral lower bound in the interval in (2.77).

Consequently every power of the positive elliptic shift \(1+A_j+C(1+j^2)\), applied to the normalized integrated wave operator, has operator norm bounded by a polynomial in \(k\). Its local Sobolev estimates have constants polynomial in \(j\). Indeed derive the first estimate by integration by parts, absorbing connection coefficients of size \(C(1+|j|)\); differentiate and iterate, since their chart derivatives have the same polynomial bounds. A point mass and its derivatives have fixed negative Sobolev order with polynomial bounds in \(j\). Applying the operator estimates followed by these elliptic estimates shows that the kernel has every smooth derivative bounded polynomially in \(k\). The adjoint argument gives the other kernel variable as well. This proves smoothness and the stated seminorm bounds. Finite propagation places all kernels in one ball of fixed radius proportional to \(R\); its compact angular fibres give common compact support modulo the centre. Extending by zero to the other component is smooth.

To calculate traces, no Plancherel theorem is needed. Apply the compactly supported wave distribution to a compact-weight vector of any admissible representation. Its operator solves the ordinary wave equation with initial projection \(\pi(e_j)\); uniqueness gives \[ \pi(e_j\Phi_k(A)) =\Phi_k(-\pi(\Omega)-1)\pi(e_j). \] This holds for complex principal parameters too, by the same equation or analytic continuation. In a principal series the two adjacent weight dimensions are both one, so their traces cancel. In the full \(D_n\), the positive weight \(j\) occurs once precisely when \(j\ge n\) with the correct parity. The difference of weights \(k,k-2\) is therefore one exactly for \(n=k\). Its Casimir is \(n(n-2)\), so the surviving multiplier is \(B_k/B_k=1\). This proves (2.74). \(\square\)

This is a rank-one adjacent-weight version of the Euler–Poincaré construction; compare Deitmar, Section 4. Here the wave multiplier is explicit, its support is proved, and shrinking time bumps give the polynomial bounds required for a smooth infinite sum.

Theorem 2.22 — complete real smooth and spectral transfer. Every smooth compact centrally covariant quaternion test \(f'\) has a smooth compact centrally covariant split transfer \(f\): \[ O_g(f)=O_b(f')\text{ on matching regular elliptic classes}, \qquad O_g(f)=0\text{ on regular split classes}. \tag{2.78} \] Every pair satisfying these identities satisfies (2.71) and \[ \operatorname{tr}D_{m+2}(f) =-\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Sym}^m(f'),\qquad \operatorname{tr}I_\kappa(s)(f)=0. \tag{2.79} \] A determinant character with the prescribed central character has trace equal to that of the corresponding norm character against \(f'\). The same transfer existence holds for ordinary compact smooth tests on the groups before taking the central quotient.

Proof. First determine the orbital integrals of \(p_k\). Weyl integration is valid by the locally integrable character formulas above. On split classes its Jacobian is \(4\sinh^2(u/2)\). Formula (2.70) expresses the principal trace as the Fourier cosine transform of a fixed constant times \(|\sinh(u/2)|O_{a_u}(p_k)\). This is even, integrable and compactly supported in \(u\), since eigenvalue ratios are bounded on the test's support. The bound \(O_{a_u}(p_k)=O(1/|u|)\) near zero follows from the one-sheeted calculation in Lemma 2.20. Fourier uniqueness and (2.74) give zero split orbital integrals. On the other component they vanish because the test is zero there.

At elliptic classes the two groups have the same Weyl Jacobian \(4\sin^2\theta\), Weyl order two and torus measures. The constants really agree: the real Cartan normalization gives tangent density \(2/\pi^2\) in the three coordinates of Lemma 2.20; the compact quaternion quotient of volume \(2\) has that same density in its three imaginary coordinates. The conjugation Jacobian is the stated discriminant on each side. Dividing by the paired centralizer measure gives identical Weyl measures on the matching torus.

The compact target for \(p_k\) is the class function \(-\Theta_{\operatorname{Sym}^{k-2}}/2\). With compact volume \(2\), its trace in \(\operatorname{Sym}^m\) is \(-\delta_{m,k-2}\), by Schur orthogonality. Use (2.22) and (2.74) in the elliptic Weyl integral. The difference between the two orbital integrals is orthogonal to every compact character of the given parity, with weight \(\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\). It lies in this weighted \(L^2\) space: (2.72) gives endpoint growth at most \(C/|\sin\theta|\). The characters are complete there, since multiplication by \(\sin\theta\) sends \(\Theta_{\operatorname{Sym}^m}\) to \(\sin((m+1)\theta)\), the ordinary Fourier sine basis; restricting central parity restricts to the corresponding reflection parity. Thus the difference is zero almost everywhere and hence everywhere on the regular set by smoothness. This proves transfer for \(p_k\).

Conjugation-average \(f'\); this changes none of its orbital integrals, traces or scalar values. Put \(a_m=\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Sym}^m(f')\). Compact character expansion gives \[ f'_{\rm av}=\frac12 \sum_{\substack{m\ge0\\m\equiv\kappa\ (2)}} a_m\Theta_{\operatorname{Sym}^m}. \tag{2.80} \] The coefficients decrease faster than every power of \(m\): integrate by parts repeatedly with the compact Casimir, whose eigenvalue grows quadratically. Additional compact derivatives give convergence in \(C^\infty\).

Set \(f=-\sum_m a_m p_{m+2}\). The polynomial seminorm bounds and common support in Lemma 2.21 make this a convergent compact smooth sum. Orbital integration on a fixed compact support is continuous at each regular class, so the sum has exactly the orbital integrals in (2.78). For every matching pair, the same Weyl formulas and character identities prove (2.79), and Lemma 2.20 proves its scalar identity.

A one-dimensional determinant character is constant on each determinant fibre. Its split contribution is zero. Its elliptic Weyl integral equals the compact Weyl integral of the norm character, with positive sign. This proves the one-dimensional assertion and distinguishes it from the negative discrete-series relation.

Finally, ordinary compact tests require no assumption of a fixed positive central character. Write each quaternion as \(c u\), \(c>0\), with unit quaternion \(u\), split the \(u\)-test into its two parities under \(u\mapsto-u\), and apply the preceding construction for each \(c\). The coefficients \(a_m(c)\) and all their \(c\)-derivatives decrease rapidly, uniformly on the compact \(c\)-support. The common support and polynomial bounds therefore give a compact smooth split test depending smoothly on \(c\), extended by zero to negative determinant. Orbital matching is pointwise at each \(c\). For fixed centrally covariant tests restore their positive central twist on both sides. \(\square\)

The next three sections supply the finite all-level correspondence and its spectral transfer. The full global trace comparison remains a separate argument.

2.8. Construct every division-side local image

This section constructs a split representation from every irreducible quaternion representation, without restricting its level or using a quadratic-character classification of supercuspidals. The bijection, elliptic character comparison and paired degrees will require the orthogonality argument following the construction.

Let \(F\) be any nonarchimedean local field, \(D\) its quaternion division algebra, \(N=\operatorname{nrd}\), \(\tau=\operatorname{trd}\), and \(\iota(x)=\tau(x)-x\). Put \(D^1=\ker N\). Use the additive Fourier transform \[ \widehat\Phi(y)=\int_D\Phi(x)\psi(\tau(xy))\,dx \] with self-dual measure. The polar transform of the quadratic norm is \(\mathcal F_Q\Phi(y)=\widehat\Phi(y^\iota)\), since \(N(x+y)-N(x)-N(y)=\tau(xy^\iota)\). It is nondegenerate in characteristic two as well. Involution preserves measure, and \(\mathcal F_Q^2\Phi(x)=\Phi(-x)\).

Lemma 2.23 — the quaternion norm operators, in every characteristic. If \(T\) is an irreducible smooth representation of \(D^\times\) on \(U\), with central character \(\eta\), the space \[ \mathcal S(D,T)= \{\Phi\in\mathcal S(D,U): \Phi(xu)=T(u)^{-1}\Phi(x),\ u\in D^1\} \tag{2.81} \] carries a smooth admissible representation \(r_T\) of \(\mathrm{GL}_2(F)\). Its formulas are \[ \begin{aligned} r_T(n(b))\Phi(x)&=\psi(bN(x))\Phi(x),\\ r_T(m(a))\Phi(x)&=|a|^2\Phi(ax),\\ r_T(w_0)\Phi(x)&=-\widehat\Phi(x^\iota),\\ r_T(d(Nh))\Phi(x)&=|Nh|\,T(h)\Phi(xh). \end{aligned} \tag{2.82} \] Here \(m(a)=\operatorname{diag}(a,a^{-1})\), \(d(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t,1)\), and \(w_0=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). A scalar \(aI\) acts by \(\eta(a)\).

Proof. The quaternion structure in Lesson 17 lets us write \(D=E\oplus E\Pi\), with \(E/F\) unramified quadratic, \(\Pi^2=\varpi\), and \(\Pi z=z^\sigma\Pi\). Thus \(N(x+y\Pi)=N_E(x)-\varpi N_E(y)\). The norm maps \(D^\times\) onto \(F^\times\): norms from the unramified units give every unit, and \(N(\Pi)=-\varpi\) gives valuation one. Its kernel is compact.

The exact norm Gaussian in Lesson 10, Lemmas 6.1–6.2, applies to both summands. If \(\eta_E\) is the unramified quadratic norm character and \(\lambda=\epsilon(1/2,\eta_E,\psi)\), then \(\lambda^2=\eta_E(-1)\), and scaling the second summand's character by \(-\varpi\) changes its phase to \(\eta_E(-\varpi)\lambda\). Their product is \(\eta_E(-1)\eta_E(-\varpi)=\eta_E(\varpi)=-1\). Use the product of the corresponding self-dual measures; it is precisely the measure for the polar pairing of \(N\). Multiplying the two Gaussian identities gives \[ \int_D^{\rm st}\psi(bN(x))\,dx=-|b|^{-2}, \qquad \mathcal F_Q(\psi(bN))(y)=-|b|^{-2}\psi(-N(y)/b). \tag{2.83} \] These are distributional identities, established on compact tests; no division by two is involved.

The upper-triangular relations, Fourier scaling and \(w_0^2=m(-1)\) follow directly from (2.82). For the remaining Bruhat relation \[ w_0n(b)w_0= n(-b^{-1})m(-b^{-1})w_0n(-b^{-1}) \quad(b\ne0), \] (2.83) gives the same kernel and phase on both sides. The amplitude has absolute value \(|b|^{-2}\); on the left the two Weyl signs combine with the negative Gaussian sign, agreeing with the single Weyl sign on the right. The two Bruhat cells give a presentation exactly as in Lesson 10, Theorem 6.3, so these operators define the norm representation of \(\mathrm{SL}_2(F)\).

The operators preserve (2.81). For the polar transform, its covariance follows by substituting \(x\mapsto xu\) and using \(N(u)=1\). More explicitly, the trace-pair Fourier transform of a right-covariant test is left-covariant: \(\widehat\Phi(uy)=T(u)\widehat\Phi(y)\), \(u\in D^1\); applying involution changes this to the required right covariance.

The last operator in (2.82) is independent of the chosen \(h\). Replacing \(h\) by \(hu\), \(u\in D^1\), cancels \(T(u)\) by (2.81). Products have the required determinant product. It conjugates \(n(b)\) to \(n(Nh\,b)\), and commutes with \(m(a)\). Fourier change of variables, whose additive Jacobian is \(|Nh|^2\), verifies the Weyl relation. To track noncommutativity explicitly, for \(t=Nh\) the composition of the determinant operator, the polar transform and the determinant operator has argument \(xh^2/t\). This factor \(h^2/t\) has norm one; covariance cancels \(T(h)^2\) and leaves \(T(t)\). It follows that \(r_T(d(t))r_T(w_0)r_T(d(t))=\eta(t)r_T(w_0)\), the relation \(d(t)w_0d(t)=t w_0\). Together with \(d(a^2)m(a^{-1})=aI\), these checks give the full group and its stated central action.

Smoothness follows from compact support, local constancy and the openness of the norm on arbitrarily small unit neighborhoods. For that last assertion, at the identity the norm polynomial has differential \(\tau\), which is nonzero: the unramified separable subfield already has surjective residue trace. Solving along that direction by a contraction on a sufficiently small ideal proves openness, in characteristic two too. A small upper-unipotent fixes \(\Phi\); apply the same assertion to its polar transform to obtain a small lower-unipotent fixing subgroup. Small diagonal units fix it by the determinant formula. Gaussian elimination near the identity gives an open group fixing \(\Phi\).

Finally, vectors fixed by an upper-unipotent group \(n(\mathfrak p^a)\) have support in one bounded fractional quaternion ideal: if \(\psi\) has annihilator \(\mathfrak p^{-\ell}\), its norm valuations must be at least \(-a-\ell\). Fixing the opposite unipotent bounds the polar Fourier support in the same way. Fourier orthogonality then makes such a test constant on cosets of the annihilator of that bounded Fourier-support ideal. It is a function on a finite quotient of a bounded ideal, with values in finite-dimensional \(U\). Every sufficiently small compact-open fixed space is contained in such a finite-dimensional space. This proves admissibility. \(\square\)

A unitary \(T\) makes all operators in (2.82) unitary on their subspace of \(L^2(D,U)\): the determinant Jacobian cancels \(|Nh|^2\), and the polar Fourier operator is an isometry. Every \(T\) with unitary central character can be made unitary by averaging over \(D^\times/F^\times\), as in Lesson 17.

Lemma 2.24 — the complete division-side matrix functional equation. If \(\dim T>1\), put \[ Z_D(s,\Phi,T)= \int_{D^\times}|Nx|^{s+1/2}T(x)\Phi(x)\,d^\times x. \tag{2.84} \] For all vector tests this is an entire Laurent polynomial in \(q^{-s}\). There is a nonzero monomial \(\epsilon_D(s,T,\psi)\) such that \[ \int_{D^\times}|Ny|^{3/2-s}T(y)^{-1}\widehat\Phi(y)\,d^\times y =-\epsilon_D(s,T,\psi)Z_D(s,\Phi,T). \tag{2.85} \] These statements hold after every norm-character twist. Scalar tests and a dual vector give the corresponding matrix-coefficient equation; its Euler factors are \(L_D(s,T)=L_D(s,T^\vee)=1\).

Proof. First \(D^1\) is the commutator subgroup. If \(x\in D^1\) is not central, \(x^\iota=x^{-1}\) and its reduced polynomial \(X^2-\tau(x)X+1\) is separable. In characteristic two, a repeated root would force \((x-1)^2=0\), impossible in a division algebra unless \(x=1\). In other characteristics, repetition similarly makes \(x=\pm1\). Thus \(x\) lies in a separable quadratic subfield; central \(x=\pm1\) lies in the unramified one. Hilbert 90 gives \(x=y/y^\iota\) in that field. The quadratic embedding and conjugation result in Section 1 supplies \(j\) inducing its nontrivial involution. Hence \(x=yjy^{-1}j^{-1}\). Conversely every commutator has norm one. Therefore a representation with a \(D^1\)-fixed vector, by normality and irreducibility, is a character of \(D^\times/D^1=F^\times\), and is one-dimensional.

Average a test to \(\Phi_1(x)=\int_{D^1}T(u)\Phi(xu)\,du\), with probability measure. It belongs to (2.81), leaves (2.84) unchanged, and has \(\Phi_1(0)=0\) when \(\dim T>1\). Thus it vanishes in a neighborhood of zero. Its Fourier transform is left-covariant and also vanishes near zero. Both projected tests have compact support in \(D^\times\), so their zeta integrals are finite sums of powers of \(q^{-s}\). This also proves the assertion for arbitrary tests and for the left side of (2.85).

We prove its scalar proportionality, rather than assume a local correspondence. On scalar tests in \(\mathcal S(D^\times)\), let \(A_s(\phi)\) be the operator obtained from the right side of (2.84), and \(B_s(\phi)\) the operator obtained from the integral on the left of (2.85). For \(\lambda(h)\phi(x)=\phi(h^{-1}x)\), \(\rho(h)\phi(x)=\phi(xh)\), direct change of variables gives \[ \begin{aligned} A_s(\lambda(h)\phi)&=|Nh|^{s+1/2}T(h)A_s(\phi),\\ A_s(\rho(h)\phi)&=|Nh|^{-s-1/2}A_s(\phi)T(h)^{-1}. \end{aligned} \tag{2.86} \] Fourier transformation exchanges these two translations and supplies the factor \(|Nh|^{\pm2}\). Consequently \(B_s\) has exactly the same two identities.

Here is the distributional uniqueness in this assertion. On the open homogeneous space \(D^\times\), smooth equivariance under left translations identifies the distribution with the integral of a locally constant matrix density: average over a compact open subgroup on which \(T\) and the norm character are trivial, then translate its cosets. Its density is determined by its value at the identity. The right-translation identity forces that value to commute with every \(T(h)\), hence to be scalar by Schur. Thus \(B_s=c_sA_s\). The conclusion extends from \(\mathcal S(D^\times)\) to \(\mathcal S(D)\) by the \(D^1\) projections above; both projections vanish near zero, so a distribution at zero cannot supply an extra term. Define \(c_s=-\epsilon_D(s,T,\psi)\).

For \(u\in U\), the test \(\Phi_u(x)=T(x)^{-1}u\,\mathbf1_{\mathcal O_D^\times}(x)\) has \(Z_D(s,\Phi_u,T)=v_Du\), where \(v_D=\operatorname{vol}(\mathcal O_D^\times)\), independent of \(s\). Evaluating (2.85) on such tests shows that \(\epsilon_D\) is a Laurent polynomial. Transpose the matrix equation to use \(T^\vee\), apply it twice and use \(\widehat{\widehat\phi}(x)=\phi(-x)\). This gives \[ \epsilon_D(s,T,\psi) \epsilon_D(1-s,T^\vee,\psi)=\eta(-1). \tag{2.87} \] The same test for \(T^\vee\) shows that the second factor is a Laurent polynomial. A Laurent polynomial with a Laurent-polynomial inverse is a nonzero monomial: its highest and lowest powers add under multiplication, so the width of each factor must be zero. This proves (2.85), its nonvanishing, and attainment of the Euler factor one. A norm twist preserves the absence of \(D^1\)-fixed vectors, so the entire argument applies to it. \(\square\)

Theorem 2.25 — a supercuspidal image for every noncharacter \(T\). If \(\dim T=d>1\), then \(r_T\) is a direct sum of \(d\) copies of a single irreducible admissible supercuspidal \(\pi_T\), with central character \(\eta\). Every norm twist and dual has the corresponding determinant twist and dual, and \[ L(s,\pi_T\otimes\chi)=1,\qquad \epsilon(s,\pi_T\otimes\chi,\psi) =\epsilon_D(s,T\otimes(\chi\circ N),\psi) \tag{2.88} \] for every smooth quasicharacter \(\chi\). If \(T\) is unitary, so is \(\pi_T\).

Proof. Every test in (2.81) vanishes near zero, by Lemma 2.24. For \(a=Nh\), define \[ f_\Phi(a)=|a|T(h)\Phi(h). \tag{2.89} \] It is independent of the norm-fibre representative. It gives a bijection \(\mathcal S(D,T)\to\mathcal S(F^\times,U)\): the inverse is \(\Phi(x)=|Nx|^{-1}T(x)^{-1}f(Nx)\), with zero at zero. Properness of the quaternion norm and its open compact fibres prove compact support and local constancy in both directions. The upper Borel acts by \[ r_T(n(b))f(a)=\psi(ab)f(a),\qquad r_T(d(t))f(a)=f(at), \] and the central character is already \(\eta\).

For \(g=r_T(w_0)f\), multiplicative Mellin transformation of (2.89), followed by \(y=h^\iota\), gives \[ \int g(a)\chi(a)^{-1}\eta(a)^{-1}|a|^{1/2-s}\,d^\times a =\epsilon_D(s,T\otimes(\chi\circ N),\psi) \int f(a)\chi(a)|a|^{s-1/2}\,d^\times a. \tag{2.90} \] Indeed \(h=a(h^\iota)^{-1}\) implies \(T(h)=\eta(a)T(h^\iota)^{-1}\); it cancels the displayed \(\eta^{-1}\), and the minus sign in \(r_T(w_0)\) cancels the minus in (2.85). Use probability norm-one fibre measure in (2.84); any common positive multiplicative normalization cancels. These checks track the exact phase and both exponents.

Every linear subspace \(U_0\subset U\) is therefore preserved in \(\mathcal S(F^\times,U_0)\) by the Weyl operator. Apply a linear functional annihilating \(U_0\) to (2.90); every Mellin transform of the resulting compact function is zero, so compact-unit Fourier orthogonality and the finite valuation support give the function zero. The upper Borel preserves that subspace as well. Hence each line of \(U\) gives an invariant scalar copy \(\mathcal S(F^\times)\).

The affine action on this scalar space is irreducible. From a nonzero compact locally constant function, additive-character averaging in \(n(b)\) isolates a nonzero constant value on a sufficiently small multiplicative ball. Diagonal translations give every translate of that ball. Repeating the character projections makes it arbitrarily small; its translates span all compact locally constant functions on \(F^\times\). A nonzero invariant subspace must therefore be the entire scalar space. This proves irreducibility under the upper Borel, and hence under the full group. Admissibility follows from Lemma 2.23. The scalar space is exactly the compact Kirillov space; the Jacquet quotient is zero by those same character projections. The compact-Kirillov supercuspidal criterion proved in Lessons 6–7 identifies it as supercuspidal and gives \(L(s,\pi_T\otimes\chi)=1\).

Equation (2.90) is precisely the local functional equation in Lesson 9, with its normalized Mellin exponent \(s-1/2\). It proves (2.88). The scalar Weyl operator is determined by these Mellin equations, so the copies for different lines are all equivalent. A basis of \(U\) now gives the stated direct sum.

For a norm twist, its zeta equation is obtained by multiplying the test by \(\chi(Nx)\); the resulting central character is \(\eta\chi^2\). Its Euler and epsilon data agree with the determinant twist. Equation (2.87), together with the corresponding split duality identity in Lesson 9, gives the same data for \(\pi_{T^\vee}\) and \(\pi_T^\vee\). The local converse theorem of that lesson, with their central characters and every character twist, identifies these representations. No existence or surjectivity statement from a local Langlands correspondence is used.

For unitary \(T\), norm-fibre disintegration of additive measure gives \(dx=C|a|^2\,d^\times a\,du\). In (2.89) the factor \(|a|\) cancels this density: \(\|\Phi\|_{L^2(D,U)}^2=C\int\|f(a)\|^2\,d^\times a\). Thus each scalar copy inherits a unitary Hilbert norm from (2.82). \(\square\)

Lemma 2.26 — norm characters give the special representation and its factors. Every one-dimensional \(T\) is \(\chi\circ N\), and its construction is the special representation \(\mathrm{St}\otimes(\chi\circ\det)\). Proof. The commutator calculation in Lemma 2.24 gives the first assertion. In this case (2.81) consists of scalar norm-fibre-constant tests. Its image (2.89) contains \(\mathcal S(F^\times)\) and has one additional small-\(a\) germ, \(\chi(a)|a|\) times \(\Phi(0)\). Thus the compact subspace has codimension one. Any nonzero invariant subspace contains a nonzero compact vector: apply \(n(b)-1\), which kills the germ sufficiently near zero and is nonzero for a suitable \(b\). The affine irreducibility argument includes the whole compact subspace. A nonnegative, nonzero test supported on a unit shell has nonzero Fourier value at zero. The Weyl operator consequently sends a compact vector to one with nonzero germ. The invariant subspace is the whole space. This proves irreducibility.

Its unique Jacquet germ transforms under \(d(t)\) by \(\chi(t)|t|\). The normalized induction classification in Lesson 7 identifies this irreducible generic module as \(\mathrm{St}\otimes\chi\), with inducing characters \(\chi|\cdot|^{1/2},\chi|\cdot|^{-1/2}\). The central character is \(\chi^2\), consistent with (2.82).

A scalar test is constant near zero. Its unramified small-ball norm-shell tail is a geometric series in \(\chi(\varpi)q^{-s-1/2}\), giving (2.91); if \(\chi\) is ramified, averaging over the surjective unit norm kills that tail. Unit-shell tests attain the normalized factor. Under (2.89), this is the split Kirillov Mellin integral with exponent \(s-1/2\). The polar Weyl operator is the same as before, including its minus sign. Its Mellin equation and the exact special functional equation in Lesson 9 therefore identify the epsilon factor too. Scalar tests are reduced to norm-fibre-constant ones by probability averaging over \(D^1\), so the equality holds for every matrix zeta test, rather than only a radial sample. \(\square\)

Computed example — the Steinberg phase from the quaternion integer ring. Choose \(\psi\) of conductor \(\mathcal O_F\) and an unramified \(\chi\), with \(\alpha=\chi(\varpi)\). The trace-dual of \(\mathcal O_D=\mathcal O_E\oplus\mathcal O_E\Pi\) is \(\mathfrak P_D^{-1}=\mathcal O_E\oplus\varpi^{-1}\mathcal O_E\Pi\). The construction covers every irreducible \(T\) in every residue and field characteristic, with its full central character, all twisted factors and a unitary discrete-series image. The next two sections prove injectivity and surjectivity by Fourier comparison and elliptic orthogonality.

2.9. Character integration through the finite singular set

The regular character theorem does not by itself give the trace of a test whose support meets singular classes. We prove that extension now. This is also where characteristic two needs its own estimate: the unmodified inverse square root of the Weyl discriminant is not a suitable integrable bound there.

Choose an additive character of conductor \(\mathcal O_F\) for this proof; changing it rescales the measures by positive constants. Put \(K=\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathcal O_F)\), and let \(C_R\subset G/Z\) consist of the classes with elementary-divisor gap at most \(R\). These are compact, exhaust \(G/Z\), and are invariant under multiplication by \(K\) on both sides. For a separable quadratic field \(E/F\), let \(d_E\) be its discriminant exponent and \(c_E=q^{-d_E/2}\). On the regular set put \[ \delta(g)=\left|\frac{(\alpha-\beta)^2}{\alpha\beta}\right|, \qquad \xi(g)= \begin{cases} \delta(g)^{-1/2},&\alpha,\beta\in F,\\ c_E\delta(g)^{-1/2},&F[g]=E. \end{cases} \tag{2.92} \] The unspecified value on the singular set is irrelevant to integration. Lemma 2.27 — an integrable majorant and every finite-place trace. The function \(\xi\) is locally integrable on \(G\) and \(G/Z\), and is bounded below by a positive number on a compact set. Proof: orbital bounds. We first establish the estimates that will justify dominated convergence. For an anisotropic torus, regard \(F^2\) as \(E\). Every lattice, up to multiplication by \(E^\times\), is an order \(R_j=\mathcal O_F+\mathfrak p^j\mathcal O_E\), \(j\ge0\). Here is the elementary lattice argument. Scale a lattice by an element of minimal \(E\)-valuation; it then contains 1 and lies in \(\mathcal O_E\). Its quotient by \(\mathcal O_F\) is torsion free, so it has basis \(1,y\). Integrality gives \(y^2\in\mathcal O_F+\mathcal O_Fy\), hence it is an order. Two such orders are homothetic only if their multiplier orders, and therefore their \(j\)'s, agree.

In characteristic two write \(F=k((\varpi))\). Lemma 2.28 — split and division matrix Fourier identities. Let \(T\) be a noncharacter division representation and \(\pi=\pi_T\) from Theorem 2.25. Write \(W=\pi(w_0)\). For \(\phi\in\mathcal S(F^\times)\), a vector \(v\) and a smooth dual vector \(v^*\), put \[ \Phi_G(g)=\phi(\det g)|\det g|^{-1} \langle\pi(g^{-1})v,v^*\rangle, \] with zero on singular matrices. Proof of the split identity. Compactness modulo the centre of a supercuspidal coefficient, and the compact norm support of \(\phi\), make \(\Phi_G\) compactly supported away from the singular set. The zero Jacquet module says that every integral of a translated coefficient along the full upper unipotent is zero. Hence its Fourier transform vanishes at a rank-one matrix: by left and right translation reduce to \(\operatorname{diag}(a,0)\), and integrate first over right \(n(x)\). The determinant and the Fourier kernel are constant in this variable, while its coefficient integral is zero. The same argument gives zero at the zero matrix. All integrals here can be made over compact cutoffs and then the compact coefficient support removes the cutoff.

It suffices to compute \(\widehat\Phi_G(I)\). We give the unit-shell calculation to determine its exact constant. In the compact Kirillov model use the pairing of Lesson 9, equation (2.2), \(\langle f,f^*\rangle=\int f(a)f^*(-a)\,d^\times a\), with unit volume one. Let \(f,f^*,\phi\) have supports \(\varpi^{n_1}\mathcal O^\times\), \(\varpi^{n_2}\mathcal O^\times\), \(\varpi^n\mathcal O^\times\), and values there \(\gamma_1^{-1},\gamma_2^{-1},\nu^{-1}\). Such functions form bases of their Schwartz spaces. Put \(\eta(\varepsilon\varpi^r)=\eta_0(\varepsilon)z^r\), and write \(C_r^\mu\) for the Weyl coefficients in Lesson 9, equation (2.3). Unit Fourier inversion gives \[ \langle f,f^*\rangle =\mathbf1_{n_1=n_2}\mathbf1_{\gamma_1\gamma_2=1}\gamma_2(-1), \quad (W\phi)(1)=z^{-n}C_n^{\nu^{-1}\eta_0^{-1}}. \tag{2.105} \] The product condition on the two unit characters in this formula is essential.

Set \(c=\operatorname{vol}_{dx}(\mathcal O^\times)\), and \(G_\rho(t)=\int_{\mathcal O^\times}\rho(\varepsilon)\psi(\varepsilon t)\,d^\times\varepsilon\). Fourier inversion of a unit-shell function gives \[ \int_F G_\rho(\varpi^j x)\psi(bx)\,dx =\begin{cases} c^{-1}|\varpi|^{-j}\rho(-\varepsilon),&b=\varpi^j\varepsilon,\\ 0,&v(b)\ne j. \end{cases} \tag{2.106} \] For \(j=0\), this is the Fourier inversion of the function \(c^{-1}\rho\mathbf1_{\mathcal O^\times}\); dilation gives the general case.

The big cell has coordinates \[ g=bI\,n(-x)d(a)w_0n(y),\quad \det g=b^2a,\quad \operatorname{tr}g=b(x-y), \] and its exact additive Jacobian is \(dg=c^2|b|^4|a|\,d^\times b\,d^\times a\,dx\,dy\). Indeed its entries are \(bx,b(a+xy),-b,-by\); the additive Jacobian before converting \(da,db\) to multiplicative measures is \(|b|^3\). This accounts for the constant often suppressed in this calculation.

Write \(a=\zeta\varpi^r\). Apply the Weyl coefficient formula to \(\pi(w_0^{-1}d(a^{-1})n(x))f\), pair it with \(\pi^\vee(n(y))f^*\), and integrate in \(\zeta\). Unit orthogonality selects \(\mu=\nu^{-1}\eta_0^{-1}\). The integrand remaining for \(\widehat\Phi_G(I)\), summed over \(r\), is \[ \begin{aligned} c^2\eta(b)^{-1}|b|^2\nu(-1)z^{-r-n_1} \phi(b^2\varpi^r)C_{r+n_1+n_2}^{\nu^{-1}\eta_0^{-1}}\qquad\qquad\\ \cdot G_{\nu\gamma_1^{-1}}(\varpi^{n_1}x) G_{\nu\eta_0\gamma_2^{-1}}(\varpi^{n_2}y) \psi(b(x-y))\,d^\times b\,dx\,dy. \end{aligned} \tag{2.107} \] This displayed expression can also be checked by expanding the two compact-unit Fourier series: the dilation contributes \(z^{-r-n_1}\), their valuation pairing selects the Weyl coefficient \(r+n_1+n_2\), and their unit pairing gives the two \(G\)'s.

The calculation is legitimate on compact cutoffs in the big cell. For each smooth unit character, \(G_\rho\) is compactly supported: sufficiently oscillatory additive integration on a unit coset is zero. After the unit-character selection, (2.106) restricts \(b\) to a fixed shell and \(r\) to a single value. Increasing the cutoffs therefore leaves the resulting integrals unchanged. The original compact matrix test permits dominated convergence back to the big cell; its complement is null. This justifies the expansions as finite unit-quotient Fourier calculations, rather than an unproved interchange of infinite series.

Theorem 2.30 — full finite Jacquet–Langlands, all fields and all levels. The construction \(T\mapsto\pi_T\), with norm characters treated in Lemma 2.26, is the bijection in Theorem 2.1. 3. The global correspondence and the trace comparison

Let \(D\) be a nonsplit quaternion algebra over \(\mathbb Q\), and let \(S=\operatorname{Ram}(D)\), including the real place when appropriate. Fix a unitary idele-class character \(\omega\). By Lesson 17, the central quotient of the automorphic domain for \(D^\times\) is compact; its automorphic representations belong to the discrete spectrum. The one-dimensional constituents have the form \(\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd}\), with \(\chi^2=\omega\).

Theorem 3.1 — global Jacquet–Langlands. On excluding those one-dimensional constituents, there is an injection \[ \operatorname{JL}_D: \operatorname{Aut}_\omega(D^\times)_{\dim>1} \longrightarrow \operatorname{Cusp}_\omega(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb A)). \tag{3.1} \] It satisfies \[ \pi_v=\begin{cases} \pi'_v,&v\notin S,\\ \operatorname{JL}_{\mathbb Q_v}(\pi'_v),&v\in S, \end{cases} \qquad\pi=\operatorname{JL}_D(\pi'). \tag{3.2} \] At split places we fix an algebra isomorphism to \(M_2(\mathbb Q_v)\); another choice differs by inner conjugation and gives the same representation class. The image of (3.1) is exactly the cuspidal representations with discrete-series local components at every place of \(S\). Every automorphic irreducible of \(D^\times\) has multiplicity one. Equality at almost all places implies equality globally.

A modern reference allowing real ramification is Badulescu–Renard, §18.1, Theorem 18.1(a)–(b) in the consulted author preprint; its introductory Theorem 1.4 states the same result. That theorem is formulated for the entire discrete spectrum and the extended unitary local transfer. In degree two, its noncuspidal split-side constituents are the determinant characters, as in Lesson 16, Theorem 5.3. They correspond to the quaternion norm characters. Removing these leaves the cuspidal assertion (3.1). For cuspidal representations, the local genericity from Lesson 14, Theorem 2.1 makes extended compatibility at division places precisely the discrete-series condition of (3.2). We do not assert that every cuspidal representation of a higher-rank inner form transfers to a cuspidal representation.

The original references are Jacquet–Langlands, Theorems 14.4 and 16.1. Theorem 14.4 has a split-place infinite-dimensional hypothesis, while §16 explicitly presents the analytic trace comparison with formal qualifications. Those qualifications are not a proof of the general analytic theorem. Sections 3.3–3.6 give the rational quaternion proof: Poisson summation computes the paired volumes, rank-one truncation gives the trace comparison, and positive outside trace sums give existence and multiplicity.

If \(D=M_2(\mathbb Q)\), the correspondence on cuspidal automorphic representations is simply the identity. The restriction is essential: an infinite-dimensional Eisenstein representation is not made cuspidal by an identity map.

3.1. What the comparison must accomplish

For trivial central character, put \(H'=D^\times/\mathbb G_m\) and \(\Gamma'=H'(\mathbb Q)\). Its adelic quotient is compact. For a factorizable projective test, Lesson 18 writes its trace as \[ \sum_{\pi'}m(\pi')\operatorname{tr}\pi'(f') =\sum_{[\gamma']}\operatorname{vol} \bigl(\Gamma'_{\gamma'}\backslash H'_{\gamma'}(\mathbb A)\bigr) O_{\gamma'}(f'), \tag{3.3} \] where the classes are in \(\Gamma'\), and orbital integrals and centralizers are projective. A prescribed nontrivial central character uses the corresponding equivariant kernel on the central quotient, as in Lesson 17. The corresponding split quotient is noncompact. Its trace formula requires truncation, the continuous-spectrum terms and the residual terms of Lesson 16. One cannot apply the compact formula to that quotient unchanged.

The comparison has four jobs. First choose transferred local tests, using (1.2) and the analytic transfer theorem. Next compare geometric terms, including the singular terms determined by regular orbital-integral germs. Then compare the resulting spectral distributions: local character relations identify the division-place contributions, and the norm-character terms must be accounted for separately. Finally vary the spherical tests outside a finite set. Their eigenvalues specify the unramified components; strong multiplicity one separates the global representations. The converse theorem gives an alternative route in settings where the transferred twisted \(L\)-functions satisfy its analytic hypotheses. Neither route reduces to just an equality of finite-dimensional traces.

Arthur, §25, especially Theorems 25.5–25.6 and equation (25.28), explains the invariant geometric and spectral comparisons. Theorem 3.4 proves the noncompact trace identity used here, and Theorem 3.5 compares it with the compact formula.

3.2. Identity values and the minus sign

The identity is singular, so it is not a regular elliptic class in (1.2). Nevertheless, the regular transfer theorem also constrains the value of a transferred test function at the identity. With the paired measures used in Arthur, §25, the identity preceding the Plancherel argument in the proof of Lemma 25.2 is, in our notation, \[ f'(1)=e(D^\times)f(1)=-f(1). \tag{3.4} \] Here \(e(D^\times)=(-1)^{1-0}=-1\): the semisimple split ranks of the split group and the division group differ by one. The corresponding spectral identity in Theorem 25.1(b) is \[ \operatorname{tr}\pi(f)=-\operatorname{tr}\pi'(f'). \tag{3.5} \] At finite places, (3.4) is proved directly for every matching pair in Lemma 2.11, with the paired measures calculated in (2.37). The full scalar germ (2.47) includes the nontrivial unipotent orbital integral; it is not just an evaluation on a specially chosen transfer. The real instance of (3.4) and the general spectral identity (3.5) still use the transfer and Plancherel/formal-degree normalization theorem. Proposition 2.12 independently verifies the special representation's formal degree, and Theorem 2.16 with Proposition 2.18 gives every compact-induction degree and the two positive-level subgroup volumes. Corollary 2.17 identifies the exact dimension equality still needed for a proposed positive-level pair. Independent Haar choices must be reconciled with these explicit paired measures; the compact trace formula alone does not establish these relations.

The compact formula makes the relevance of (3.4) visible: its identity contribution is \(\operatorname{vol}(X)f(1)\), by Lesson 18, equation (3.10). The locally transferred value therefore has the same relative sign as the spectral transfer. Globally, quaternion reciprocity makes \(|S|\) even, so \[ \prod_{v\in S}e(D_v^\times)=(-1)^{|S|}=1. \tag{3.6} \] This explains how local negative character relations fit a global correspondence with positive multiplicities.

3.3. A volume identity from two theta integrals

We first fix the measures needed by the global comparison. This is a comparison over \(\mathbb Q\); the finite local results above retain their full local-field generality. Let \(\psi\) be the standard character of \(\mathbb Q\backslash\mathbb A\), with real component \(e^{2\pi i x}\). On either four-dimensional algebra use the self-dual additive measure for \((x,y)\mapsto\psi(\operatorname{trd}(xy))\). Put \[ d^\times x_v=\alpha_v|\operatorname{nrd}x_v|_v^{-2}\,dx_v, \quad \alpha_p=((1-p^{-1})(1-p^{-2}))^{-1}, \quad\alpha_\infty=(2\pi^2)^{-1}. \tag{3.7} \] Use the same scalar-subgroup measures on both sides: \(\mathbb Z_p^\times\) has multiplicative volume one, and the real scalar measure is \(dz/|z|\). These are the paired measures of (2.37) and (2.23)–(2.24). At a finite division place they give projective volume \(2/(p-1)\); at a real division place they give volume two. At split finite places \(\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb Z_p)\) has volume one. The real split measure is \(1/(2\pi)\) times the quotient normalization of Lesson 1, Section 2. Thus that lesson's volume \(\pi/3\) becomes \(1/6\).

Lemma 3.2 — equal global quotient volumes. For every nonsplit quaternion algebra \(D/\mathbb Q\), in (3.7)'s measures, \[ \operatorname{vol}\bigl(D^\times(\mathbb Q)Z(\mathbb A) \backslash D^\times(\mathbb A)\bigr) =\operatorname{vol}\bigl(G(\mathbb Q)Z(\mathbb A) \backslash G(\mathbb A)\bigr)=\frac16. \tag{3.8} \]

Proof. We will compare residues of scalar matrix integrals; this avoids using a quaternion Tamagawa-number formula as a prerequisite. On \(M_2(\mathbb A)\), take \(\Phi_M=\prod_{i,j}\phi(x_{ij})\), where \(\phi_p=1_{\mathbb Z_p}\) and \(\phi_\infty(x)=e^{-\pi x^2}\). Its additive integral and its value at zero are both one. Write \[ \Lambda(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s),\qquad Z_M(s)=\int_{G(\mathbb A)}\Phi_M(g) |\det g|^{s+1/2}d^\times g. \] For \(\operatorname{Re}s>3/2\), Iwasawa integration gives \[ Z_M(s)=\frac1{2\pi}\Lambda(s-1/2)\Lambda(s+1/2), \qquad\operatorname*{Res}_{s=3/2}Z_M(s)=\frac1{12}. \tag{3.9} \] Here is the measure calculation. Write \(g=n(x)\operatorname{diag}(a,b)k\), with compact Haar measure of total mass one. The matrix test is right compact invariant. The integral in \(x\) is \(\int\phi(xb)dx=|b|^{-1}\), and the lower-left entry contributes \(\phi(0)=1\). The Iwasawa modulus is \(|a/b|^{-1}\). The resulting powers of \(|a|,|b|\) are \(s-1/2,s+1/2\), giving the two scalar Tate integrals. The finite Iwasawa constants are one, since the multiplicative units and the matrix compact subgroup both have volume one. At the real place the additive Jacobian of \((x,a,b,\theta)\mapsto n(x)\operatorname{diag}(a,b)r(\theta)\) is \(|b|^2\). Allowing both signs of \(a,b\) and both components of \(\mathrm O(2)\), its fourfold diagonal-sign redundancy makes the raw matrix Iwasawa constant \(\pi\) when compact measure is probability measure. Multiplication by \(\alpha_\infty\) gives \(1/(2\pi)\). This proves the first formula. The scalar Poisson proof of the Tate integral gives \(\operatorname{Res}_{u=1}\Lambda(u)=1\), and \(\Lambda(2)=\pi/6\); hence the residue is \(1/12\). These scalar identities are also the ones used in Lesson 16, Proposition 5.1.

Choose an adelic Schwartz function \(\Phi_D\) on \(D(\mathbb A)\). At a split place use \(\Phi_M\) after fixing an algebra isomorphism. At a finite division place use \(1_{\mathcal O_D}\). If the real place is division, use \(e^{-2\pi\operatorname{nrd}x}\). In quaternion coordinates the trace pairing has determinant of absolute value sixteen, so its self-dual additive measure is four times ordinary four-dimensional Lebesgue measure. The last Gaussian consequently has integral one. At a finite division place \(\mathcal O_D\) has self-dual additive volume \(p^{-1}\), as calculated in the integer-ring example following Lemma 2.26. Therefore \[ H_D:=\widehat\Phi_D(0)=\prod_{p\in\operatorname{Ram}_f(D)}p^{-1}>0. \] Initially define \(Z_D(s)=\int_{D^\times(\mathbb A)}\Phi_D(x) |\operatorname{nrd}x|^{s+1/2}d^\times x\). The Euler products for \(Z_D\) and \(Z_M\) differ at only the ramified places. Each local integral is holomorphic in a neighborhood of \(s=3/2\). For our matrix tests the local Iwasawa calculation in (3.9) gives convergence for \(\operatorname{Re}s>1/2\): the two scalar exponents are \(s-1/2\) and \(s+1/2\). At a finite division place, shell integration converges for \(\operatorname{Re}s>-1/2\); at the real division place the radial Gaussian integral has the same region. These calculations also justify differentiation in a neighborhood of \(3/2\). At the critical point the norm weight in the additive integral disappears, so exactly \[ Z_{D,v}(3/2)=\alpha_v\widehat\Phi_{D,v}(0), \qquad Z_{M,v}(3/2)=\alpha_v\widehat\Phi_{M,v}(0). \] For the finite division test, shell integration gives \[ Z_{D,p}(s)=\frac{1}{(p-1)(1-p^{-(s+1/2)})}, \quad \frac{Z_{D,p}(s)}{Z_{M,p}(s)} =\frac{1-p^{-(s-1/2)}}{p-1}. \] At a real division place radial integration gives \(Z_{D,\infty}(s)=2(2\pi)^{-s-1/2}\Gamma(s+1/2)\). The split integral in (3.9) and the gamma duplication identity give \(Z_{D,\infty}(s)/Z_{M,\infty}(s)=s-1/2\). These formulas verify the critical ratios directly, including the real one. The denominator is positive at the critical point. Thus the finite product of local ratios is holomorphic near this point, has value \(H_D\), and (3.9) implies \[ \operatorname*{Res}_{s=3/2} Z_D(s)=\frac{H_D}{12}. \tag{3.10} \] No division representation or correspondence is used in this calculation.

We now compute that same residue by unfolding. Put \(X_D=D^\times(\mathbb Q)Z(\mathbb A)\backslash D^\times(\mathbb A)\). It is compact by Lesson 17, Theorem 3.3. The fibre of \(D^\times(\mathbb Q)\backslash D^\times(\mathbb A)\to X_D\) is \(C=\mathbb Q^\times\backslash\mathbb A^\times\). With the scalar measures above, its norm-one subgroup has volume one and its norm measure is \(dt/t\). This follows directly by making each finite valuation zero with a rational number and then using the real sign to divide by \(-1\); the remaining finite units carry probability measure. A compact set of representatives for \(X_D\) exists: choose finitely many relatively compact local sections of the quotient and take their union. We may therefore write the unfolded integral as an integral over representatives \(h\) in such a compact set and over \(z\in C\), with integrand \[ |\operatorname{nrd}h|^{s+1/2}|z|^{2s+1} \sum_{\gamma\in D(\mathbb Q),\ \gamma\ne0}\Phi_D(z\gamma h). \tag{3.11} \] Unfolding is absolute in the initial half-plane. Every nonzero rational quaternion is invertible, which is precisely where the division hypothesis is used.

Poisson summation on the four-dimensional rational vector space \(D\) gives, as \(t=|z|\to0\), \[ \sum_{\gamma\ne0}\Phi_D(z\gamma h) =t^{-4}|\operatorname{nrd}h|^{-2}H_D-\Phi_D(0) +O_A(t^A) \tag{3.12} \] for every \(A\), uniformly for the compact representatives \(h\) and the compact norm-one part of \(z\). Indeed right multiplication by \(h\) has additive determinant \(|\operatorname{nrd}h|^2\). The rational lattice has covolume one in the global self-dual trace measure: in any rational basis the determinant is rational, and the product formula cancels its local changes of covolume. The nonzero dual-lattice terms decrease rapidly; a common finite denominator lattice and the real Schwartz bounds prove uniformity on the two compact sets. The same lattice bounds give rapid decrease of the original nonzero sum as \(t\to\infty\).

Split the norm integral at one. All terms except the first in (3.12) are holomorphic near \(s=3/2\); the large-norm part is entire there. The first gives \[ \operatorname*{Res}_{s=3/2}Z_D(s) =\frac12 H_D\operatorname{vol}(X_D), \tag{3.13} \] since \(\int_0^1t^{2s-3}dt/t=(2s-3)^{-1}\) and \(|\operatorname{nrd}h|^{s-3/2}=1\) at the pole. Equating (3.10) and (3.13) and cancelling \(H_D\) proves (3.8). The argument applies equally to definite and indefinite quaternion algebras. \(\square\)

3.4. Removing the cusp boundary

A test in the fixed-central-character algebra satisfies \(f_v(zg)=\omega_v(z)^{-1}f_v(g)\) and has compact support modulo the centre. At infinity it is smooth and finite under left and right \(\mathrm O(2)\); at finite places it is locally constant. Measures on compact groups in this subsection are probability measures. Define its averaged constant term by \[ C_{f_v}(a)=\int_{K_v}\int_{F_v} f_v(k^{-1}a n(x)k)\,dx\,dk, \qquad a=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,\beta). \tag{3.14} \] The integral is smooth, or locally constant, as a function of the diagonal entries away from zero. On a compact set of such entries, compact support bounds \(x\) uniformly. This proves the assertion also at \(\alpha=\beta\).

Lemma 3.3 — transfer has zero averaged constant term. Every split test matching a division test at a ramified place satisfies \(C_{f_v}(a)=0\) for every \(a\).

Proof. For \(\alpha\ne\beta\), conjugation by \(n(x)\) sends \(a\) to \(a n((1-\beta/\alpha)x)\), up to replacing \(x\) by its negative. The Iwasawa integration formula for the split-torus quotient therefore expresses its ordinary orbital integral as a nonzero measure constant times \(|1-\beta/\alpha|^{-1}C_{f_v}(a)\). That orbital integral is zero by the definition of matching. Hence the constant term is zero on all distinct diagonal entries. Continuity gives zero at equal entries too. This applies at the real place without an assertion about individual unipotent matrices. \(\square\)

We next prove the precise trace formula needed here. Write \(X=G(\mathbb Q)Z(\mathbb A)\backslash G(\mathbb A)\). For an elliptic rational matrix \(\gamma\), let \(E_\gamma=\mathbb Q[\gamma]\) and \(T_\gamma=E_\gamma^\times\). Use the same quotient measure on this torus in the split and quaternion groups. Set \[ a(\gamma)=\frac{\operatorname{vol} (T_\gamma(\mathbb Q)Z(\mathbb A)\backslash T_\gamma(\mathbb A))} {s(\gamma)},\qquad s(\gamma)=|\{c\in\mathbb Q^\times:c\gamma\text{ is conjugate to }\gamma\}|. \tag{3.15} \] The denominator is one if \(\operatorname{tr}\gamma\ne0\), and two if its trace is zero. Indeed equal characteristic polynomials imply respectively \(c=1\), or \(c^2=1\); a trace-zero quadratic field generator is conjugate to its negative. This factor prevents double counting when ordinary conjugacy classes are taken modulo rational scalars. It also describes the possible disconnected projective centralizer correctly. The elliptic sum below uses one representative for each such class.

Theorem 3.4 — the trace formula with two vanishing constant terms. Let \(f=\bigotimes_v f_v\), spherical outside a finite set, and suppose (3.14) vanishes at two different places. Then \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}R_{\mathrm{disc}}(f) &=\sum_{\pi\ \mathrm{cuspidal}}m(\pi)\operatorname{tr}\pi(f) +\sum_{\chi^2=\omega}\operatorname{tr}(\chi\circ\det)(f)\\ &=\operatorname{vol}(X)f(1) +\sum_{\gamma\ \mathrm{elliptic}}a(\gamma)O_\gamma(f). \end{aligned} \tag{3.16} \] The spectral sum is absolutely convergent; only finitely many elliptic classes contribute. The continuous spectrum, the split semisimple boundary and the unipotent finite part contribute zero.

Proof. We give the convergence argument along with the two boundary calculations. Work at a finite level fixing every finite test and at the finitely many real compact weights occurring in \(f\). Lessons 1 and 4 realize this as a finite collection of congruence quotients with finitely many cusp collars. At sufficiently large height their translates are disjoint, by the lower-left-entry reduction estimate in Lesson 4, Lemma 1.2. Write \(X_T\) for the core obtained by deleting height \(y>e^T\) in each collar.

We use the piecewise orthogonal truncation \(P_T\): on each outer collar it subtracts the additive constant term, and on the core it is the identity. The disjoint collar coordinates make this a self-adjoint projection on \(L^2(X,\omega)\). On Eisenstein series it is exactly the truncation of Lesson 16, Lemma 6.3. On cusp forms it is the identity. On a residual character its norm tends to the full norm, since the removed constant tail has volume \(O(e^{-T})\).

Here are the two estimates needed for traces. First the cuspidal Casimir has polynomial eigenvalue count, not merely compact resolvent. To check this directly, fix a rotation weight \(m\), set \(u=\log y\), and remove the \(y^{1/2}\) half-density. The nonconstant cusp Fourier modes of the weight Laplacian are \(-\partial_u^2+c_n^2e^{2u}+b_ne^u+1/4\), with \(|c_n|\ge c|n|\) and \(|b_n|\le C_m|c_n|\). Completing the square bounds them below by \(-\partial_u^2+\tfrac12c_n^2e^{2u}-C_m\). Neumann bracketing first separates the compact core and collars and then divides each collar into unit \(u\)-intervals. The core is covered by finitely many coordinate rectangles; uniform ellipticity and the elementary Neumann Fourier basis on rectangles bound its eigenvalue count by \(C(1+\lambda)\). On the interval beginning at \(j\), the radial Neumann frequencies are proportional to \(k\ge0\), so the remaining count is bounded by \[ C\sum_{j\ge0}\#\{(n,k):n\ne0,\ k\ge0, 1+k^2+n^2e^{2j}\le C(1+\lambda)\} \le C'(1+\lambda). \tag{3.17} \] Indeed there are at most \(C\sqrt{1+\lambda}e^{-j}\) nonzero \(n\)'s and at most \(C\sqrt{1+\lambda}\) \(k\)'s; the sum of \(e^{-j}\) is bounded. Shifted cusp frequencies from a unitary phase have the same bound. The cusp condition excludes the zero frequency. The comparison is valid on the quadratic-form domain: separating intervals relaxes boundary matching and can only lower eigenvalues. The fixed weight's lower-order terms were absorbed in \(C_m\). Tensoring the finitely many compact fibres changes only the constant. Thus a sufficiently large inverse power of the positive shifted Casimir is trace class. Integrating by parts in the real test proves absolute cuspidal trace convergence and continuity in its smooth seminorms. The residual sum is finite at this level by Lesson 16, Theorems 5.3 and 6.1.

Second, truncated periodized kernels have a rapid nonconstant tail. For two points sufficiently far out in collars, the arithmetic support estimate of Lesson 4, Lemma 2.1 forces every contributing matrix to carry one cusp to the other. Its lower-left entry is zero; the compact finite support permits only finitely many diagonal ratios. If no such cusp correspondence exists the kernel is zero. For each remaining ratio the height quotient is bounded above and below by the real support. The upper-right rational entries then form a fixed translation lattice. Their periodization is a sum of the form \(\sum_{r\in\ell\mathbb Z}\varphi((r+x'-x)/y)\), with \(y\) comparable to both heights, a fixed \(\ell>0\), and \(\varphi\) in a bounded family of smooth compact functions. Poisson summation makes its nonzero frequencies \((y/\ell)\widehat\varphi(ky/\ell)\), \(k\ne0\). Repeated integration by parts gives their summed absolute value at most \(C_N y^{1-N}\) for every \(N\). The same bound holds for derivatives and for the finitely many matrix entries of the compact fibres. Truncation removes precisely the zero frequency. The kernel vanishes unless the two deep heights are comparable, so integration against the two cusp densities \(dx\,dy/y^2\) proves that \(P_T R(h_1)\) and \(R(h_2)P_T\) are Hilbert–Schmidt for smooth compact tests. Their product is trace class. On its diagonal the part outside \(X_T\) is bounded by \(C_N y^{1-N}\) and has integral \(O(e^{-NT})\). This proves, without a uniform unsmoothed Eisenstein bound, that the geometric trace of \(P_T R(f)P_T\) differs from the core integral of \(K_f(x,x)\) by a quantity tending to zero.

Initially take \(f\) to be a convolution of two smooth compact tests. The product-kernel identity in Lesson 18, Lemma 3.1 now applies to \(P_T R(f)P_T\), on the entire quotient. The spectral decomposition of Lesson 16, Theorem 6.1 computes the same trace. For a positive convolution all spectral interchanges follow from its Hilbert–Schmidt norm and Tonelli; polarization gives a general product. At this level only finitely many compact torus labels and compact-model basis vectors occur. We may first restrict \(|t|\le A\) and then remove the restriction using that Hilbert–Schmidt bound. The continuous term is exactly the pairing of the truncated Eisenstein series, with no replacement of a core norm by an unproved tail estimate. The cuspidal trace is unchanged, and the residual trace tends to its full value. This is a justified kernel calculation, rather than a formal subtraction of infinite traces.

We compute the geometric core integral. Separate scalar, elliptic, split semisimple and nontrivial unipotent rational classes. Scalar matrices give \(\operatorname{vol}(X_T)f(1)\), tending to the first term on the right of (3.16). Elliptic classes unfold to the orbital integral and the coefficient (3.15). Their torus quotient is compact by the bounded-lattice proof of Lesson 17, Theorem 3.3 applied to the quadratic field \(E_\gamma\). For clarity, replace the rank-four order there by a rank-two order in \(E_\gamma\), and replace reduced norm by field norm. Multiplication now has determinant equal to that norm rather than its square. On the module-one group the lattices still have fixed covolume, and the product formula still bounds their shortest nonzero vector away from zero. Bounded bases give finitely many integral right-action tuples; their commutants are left multiplications, and a nonzero rational field element proves rationality of the comparing multiplier. Thus \(E_\gamma^\times(\mathbb Q)\backslash E_\gamma^\times(\mathbb A)^1\) has compact representatives. A scalar idele adjusts any module by its square, so its quotient by \(Z(\mathbb A)\) is compact too. For a fixed regular matrix, the conjugacy orbit is closed and its parametrization by the torus quotient is proper on the support of a compact test: a cyclic vector gives a local inverse in trace/norm coordinates, and finitely many such charts cover that support. This also proves local orbital-integral continuity in the test.

There are only finitely many contributing rational polynomials up to scalar. Outside a fixed finite set, integrality of a representative in the projective maximal compact group forces the determinant valuation to be even. Multiply by a rational scalar to put the determinant in one of finitely many signed squarefree classes supported in that finite set. The finite support conditions then bound denominators of the trace in a fixed rational lattice; the real support bounds its absolute value. Thus only finitely many traces occur. The same argument bounds split rational eigenvalue ratios. At almost every place each of these regular polynomials has integral unit discriminant and its local orbital integral is the compact-unit orbital integral. The remaining conjugator supports are compact modulo their torus. This proves absolute convergence of each elliptic contribution, and allows its truncated integral to tend to its full value.

For a split semisimple element \(a=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha,\beta)\), \(\alpha\ne\beta\), there are two rational eigenlines. In Iwasawa coordinates, deleting their two cusp ends leaves a radial interval whose length is \(2T-\sum_v\log\lambda_v(n_v)\), up to a fixed constant depending only on the choice of initial collars. Here \(\lambda_v(n_v)\) is the absolute-value ratio of the diagonal entries in the Iwasawa decomposition of \(w n_v\), where \(w=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\). To verify the interval, the first end is \(|a_1/a_2|>e^T\); the second is \(|a_1/a_2|<e^{-T}\prod_v\lambda_v(n_v)\). Integrating \(d\log|a_1/a_2|\) between them gives exactly the stated length. The fixed collar constant multiplies the ordinary orbital integral.

After the remaining \(n,k\) integrals are factored, the length term is a constant times \(T\prod_v U_v(a,f_v)\), and its finite part is a sum of terms \[ W_v(a,f_v)\prod_{w\ne v}U_w(a,f_w), \quad U_v(a,f_v)=\int_{K_v,N_v}f_v(k^{-1}n^{-1}a n k)\,dn\,dk. \tag{3.18} \] The local \(W_v\) includes the one logarithmic weight \(\log\lambda_v(n)\). For this fixed regular \(a\), compact support bounds \(n\), so these local weighted integrals are finite. Changing \(n\) to \((1-\beta/\alpha)n\) identifies \(U_v\) with a nonzero constant times \(C_{f_v}(a)\). At the two assumed places it is zero. Every term in (3.18), and the length term, consequently vanishes. This argument includes the two eigenlines' rational interchange; its factor of two multiplies zero.

For the unipotent part put \(\varphi_v(x)=\int_{K_v}f_v(k^{-1}n(x)k)dk\). It is an additive Schwartz function, and \(\int\varphi_v(x)dx=C_{f_v}(1)=0\) at the same two places. Unfolding the unique rational nontrivial unipotent class and making the change \(x=(a_1/a_2)^{-1}\) gives the Mellin integral \[ U_f(z)=\prod_v Z_v(1+z,\varphi_v) =\Lambda(1+z)\prod_v\theta_v(z),\qquad \theta_v(z)=\frac{Z_v(1+z,\varphi_v)}{L_v(1+z,1)}. \tag{3.19} \] A fixed positive measure constant from the Iwasawa coordinates is immaterial here. Almost all \(\theta_v\) are one. At zero, \(L_v(1,1)\) is finite and nonzero, so \(\theta_v(0)\) is a nonzero measure constant times \(C_{f_v}(1)\). All local functions are holomorphic near zero. For finite places this follows by summing the constant small-ball part as a geometric series; at infinity it follows from the compact smooth integral at exponent one.

To relate (3.19) to the height cutoff, unfold its multiplicative integral as \(\int_C\sum_{r\in\mathbb Q^\times}\varphi(rz)|z|^{1+z_0}d^\times z\), with \(z_0\) the Mellin parameter. Poisson summation gives its small-norm sum as \(|z|^{-1}\widehat\varphi(0)-\varphi(0)\) plus a rapidly decreasing remainder. The zero Fourier term is the sole pole at \(z_0=0\); truncating the norm integral subtracts that pole and adds its coefficient times the logarithmic cutoff. Thus the remaining unipotent term is the finite part of (3.19) at zero. If \(\Lambda(1+z)=\lambda_{-1}/z+\lambda_0+O(z)\), that finite part is \[ \lambda_0\prod_v\theta_v(0) +\lambda_{-1}\sum_v\theta_v'(0) \prod_{w\ne v}\theta_w(0). \tag{3.20} \] Both expressions are zero because there are two zero factors. The pole coefficient is zero too. The subtracted \(\varphi(0)\) term has an integrable norm tail tending to zero. Repeated integration by parts in the real additive Fourier transform bounds all nonzero Poisson tails by an arbitrarily large inverse power of the cutoff; the finite lattice denominators remain fixed. This justifies the limit, including the non-absolutely convergent raw unipotent sum. There is no surviving distribution supported at the scalar set.

We finally compute the spectral boundary. The common constant rescaling of the quotient measure in (3.7) scales the truncated pairing and the spectral-kernel coefficient reciprocally; it cancels in the trace. Use Lesson 16, Lemma 6.3 with \(s=r=it\), its unitary scattering and its full-axis packet measure \(dt/(4\pi)\). The limit of the first two boundary terms is \(2T\,1-M(it)^{-1}M'(it)\). The mixed terms are the two reflected exponentials divided by \(2it\). The first term multiplies \(\operatorname{tr}I(it,f)\). The compact-model diagonal-kernel formula is its multiplicative Fourier transform of (3.14); hence every local principal-series trace is zero at either of the specified places. The linear term is therefore identically zero.

Normalize the local intertwiners as in Lesson 16, Theorem 4.2 and the explicit local factors in Lessons 7 and 11, and write \(M(s)=m(s)\bigotimes_v R_v(s)\) on each inducing pair. The scalar \(m\) is meromorphic, unitary and nonzero on the unitary axis after removable points are filled. Differentiating this finite nontrivial tensor product gives \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{tr}(M^{-1}M'I(f)) &=\frac{m'}m\prod_v\operatorname{tr}I_v(f_v)\\ &\quad+\sum_v \operatorname{tr}(R_v^{-1}R_v'I_v(f_v)) \prod_{w\ne v}\operatorname{tr}I_w(f_w). \end{aligned} \tag{3.21} \] Only finitely many local derivative terms occur: the normalized spherical intertwiner is the identity on the spherical line at every other place. The identity holds first away from possible local removable points and then there by continuity. Each term is exactly zero, with two vanishing local traces. We cancel it before removing a spectral cutoff, so no unproved growth bound for a global logarithmic derivative is required.

For completeness the reflected terms really have the claimed limit. On the finite compact-model space, unitarity gives \(\|M(it)\|=1\). Integration by parts in the compact real test with the scalar Casimir eigenvalue \(1/4+t^2\) gives \(\|I(it,f)\|\le C_N(1+t^2)^{-N}\) for every \(N\). Thus the reflected trace amplitude is integrable. Away from zero, divide by \(t\) and apply Fourier inversion or the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma. Near zero, it is smooth by the proved regularity of \(M\); subtract its value at zero. The remaining quotient is integrable. The only limiting contribution is the familiar integral of \(\sin(2Tt)/t\), times the trace of \(M(0)I(0,f)\), with its factor \(1/4\). That trace is zero. The exchanged inducing labels have no diagonal block unless they are equal. On an equal pair, \(I_v(\chi_v,\chi_v)\) is irreducible at every place, by Lessons 7 and 11; its normalized self-intertwiner is scalar, by Schur's lemma. The global trace then factors into scalar multiples of the same local principal traces, at least two of which vanish. This proves cancellation of the endpoint as well as of the derivative integral. The truncated-kernel and absolute discrete-trace estimates above justify the spectral limit as the discrete trace, with no omitted continuous or residual part. The residual part has been retained on the left of (3.16).

To remove the initial convolution restriction, at the finitely many real weights convolve with a common approximate identity obtained from a smooth compactly supported Fourier multiplier of the shifted Casimir. Finite propagation and the smooth estimates in Lemma 2.21 give compact kernels, with uniformly bounded support, converging to the identity on smooth vectors. Choose the same multiplier on every selected weight. On every principal series it is the same scalar times the selected compact-type projection. The local principal trace remains zero; multiplicative Fourier inversion of its compact-model diagonal kernel consequently preserves (3.14). Finite-place factors stay fixed. The resulting tests are convolutions, converge to \(f\) in every smooth seminorm, and retain the two zero constant terms. Absolute discrete trace convergence follows from the first estimate, and the finite geometric sum is continuous by the orbit-chart argument. Taking the limit proves (3.16) for the stated test class. \(\square\)

3.5. Equality of the two discrete traces

Theorem 3.5 — global trace transfer. At every \(v\in S\) choose a matching pair \((f_v,f'_v)\) from Theorems 2.10 and 2.22. At every other place use the same test through the chosen split algebra isomorphism. Assume the real test is compact-group finite. Then \[ \operatorname{tr}R_{\mathrm{disc}}(f) =\operatorname{tr}R_D(f'). \tag{3.22} \] The division trace includes norm characters, and the split discrete trace includes determinant characters.

Proof. A quaternion division algebra over \(\mathbb Q\) has a nonempty even ramification set, by Lesson 17, Section 2. Hence it has at least two ramified places. Lemma 3.3 gives the two zero constant terms required by Theorem 3.4. The compact formula of Lesson 18, Theorem 3.2 applies on the division side, with the same fixed-central-character kernel.

Consider a contributing elliptic polynomial \(P\) on the split side, with field \(E=\mathbb Q[T]/(P)\). If \(E\otimes\mathbb Q_v\) is split at a ramified place, its split orbital integral is zero. Otherwise \(E\) embeds in \(D\), by Lesson 18, Section 5, the quadratic-embedding lemma and its exact Brauer prerequisite. The two matrices then correspond to the same element of \(E\); their rational conjugacy classes are unique by the same cyclic-basis and Skolem–Noether argument as in Theorem 1.1, now over the rational field. Their torus is \(E^\times\), with identical adelic measure and identical rational lattice. Thus their coefficient \(a(\gamma)\), including the scalar-stabilizer factor in (3.15), agrees. Local matching makes their orbital integrals equal at ramified places, and the algebra isomorphisms do so at all other places. This identifies every noncentral geometric term in both directions.

Every rational nonscalar quaternion has a quadratic field as centralizer. There are no split semisimple or unipotent quaternion classes to add. The scalar-value identity at each ramified place is \(f'_v(1)=-f_v(1)\), by Lemmas 2.11 and 2.20. The evenness of \(|S|\) makes their global product positive. Lemma 3.2 identifies the two global volumes. Consequently the identity terms agree too. Equation (3.16) and the compact trace formula now give (3.22). All sums and limits have the convergence established in Theorem 3.4 and Lesson 18. \(\square\)

3.6. From traces to representations

We explain why the trace equality implies existence and both multiplicity assertions, including tuples consisting only of local norm characters. Merely separating spherical eigenvalues would not by itself establish that existence.

Fix an irreducible unitary division representation \(T_v\) with central character \(\omega_v\) at every \(v\in S\). Put \[ f'_v(g)=-\frac{\overline{\Theta_{T_v}(g)}}{V_v}, \qquad V_v=\operatorname{vol}(D_v^\times/F_v^\times). \tag{3.23} \] Character orthogonality on the compact central quotient gives \(\operatorname{tr}T'_v(f'_v)=-\delta_{T_v,T'_v}\). The function is smooth; at finite places it factors through a finite quotient, and at the real place it is a compact-character test. Choose a split transfer. Corollary 2.31 and Theorem 2.22 give \[ \begin{array}{ll} \operatorname{tr}\pi_v(f_v)=\delta_{\pi_v,\operatorname{JL}(T_v)}, &\pi_v\text{ a discrete series},\\ \operatorname{tr}I_v(f_v)=0, &I_v\text{ a principal series},\\ \operatorname{tr}(\chi_v\circ\det)(f_v) =-\delta_{T_v,\chi_v\circ\operatorname{nrd}}. \end{array} \tag{3.24} \] These are trace projectors: the split test need not be an operator projector. At infinity the finite adjacent-weight wave test in Lemma 2.21 transfers (3.23), so it has the compact finiteness required above. We normalize no multiplicity by an unproved formal degree.

Let \(G^S=\prod'_{v\notin S}G(\mathbb Q_v)\), and write \(\pi^S\) for the outside tensor product. Define two unitary Hilbert sums of representations of this group: \[ \begin{aligned} M_T^D&=\bigoplus_{\pi':\ \pi'_v=T_v\ (v\in S)} m_D(\pi')\,\pi'^S,\\ M_T^G&=\bigoplus_{\substack{\pi\ \mathrm{cuspidal}\\ \pi_v=\operatorname{JL}(T_v)\ (v\in S)}} m_G(\pi)\,\pi^S \ \oplus\bigoplus_{\substack{\chi^2=\omega\\ T_v=\chi_v\circ\operatorname{nrd}\ (v\in S)}} (\chi\circ\det)^S. \end{aligned} \tag{3.25} \] Every summand's multiplicity is nonnegative. The restricted tensor-product theorem of Lesson 12 makes each outside summand irreducible. Applying (3.22) to any outside test \(a\), and using (3.23)–(3.24), gives \[ \operatorname{tr}M_T^D(a)=\operatorname{tr}M_T^G(a). \tag{3.26} \] The products of the local negative traces on the division side and on the split character side are \((-1)^{|S|}=1\). The cuspidal products are one. Thus (3.26) compares positive representation traces; there is no subtraction of a hypothetical missing constituent.

Lemma 3.6 — separation of the outside representation sums. The two sums in (3.25) are unitarily equivalent, with their irreducible multiplicities.

Proof. If infinity lies in \(S\), fix a compact open subgroup \(J\) outside \(S\). The \(J\)-fixed spaces of both sums are finite dimensional. On the division side this follows from compactness and smooth finite-level arithmetic finiteness, with the fixed real compact type. On the split side the fixed real discrete series has fixed Casimir; choose one of its nonzero compact weights, and at every finite ramified place choose a subgroup fixing a nonzero vector of \(\operatorname{JL}(T_v)\). Then each summand embeds, with its multiplicity, into the finite-dimensional space of Lesson 4, Section 4 at this level, weight and Casimir. The residual lines are finite in number. The operators of the \(J\)-corner Hecke algebra consequently act on two finite-dimensional spaces, and (3.26) says their traces agree for every element.

The following finite-dimensional fact applies. A unital complex algebra closed under adjoints in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is a direct sum of full matrix algebras on its simple modules. One can see this without a representation classification: diagonalize a self-adjoint element, take its spectral projections by polynomial interpolation, and refine these projections until the invariant blocks are irreducible. On an irreducible block, a nonzero minimal projection \(e\) has rank one. Indeed \(eAe=\mathbb C e\); if orthogonal nonzero \(v,w\) lay in its range, then \(\langle av,w\rangle=\langle eae v,w\rangle=0\) for every \(a\), contradicting cyclicity of \(v\) on an irreducible block. Products with the rank-one projection give every matrix unit, because its range is cyclic. Different irreducible blocks are separated by central sums of these minimal projections. Their traces count module multiplicities. Apply this to the image algebra on the direct sum of our two spaces. Equal traces on the two halves force equal multiplicities of every simple corner module.

The corner remembers any irreducible unitary representation that it meets. Indeed, let \(e\) denote compact averaging onto its \(J\)-fixed space. A nonzero corner-invariant subspace \(W\) generates a dense group-invariant subspace by irreducibility. But \(e\pi(g)e\) preserves \(W\), so its projection back to the finite-dimensional corner is \(W\) itself. Hence \(W\) is the whole corner. If two such corner modules are unitarily equivalent, their compressed matrix coefficients \(e\pi(g)e\) agree, by approximating the group element with smooth convolution tests. For corner vectors \(v,w\), this gives equality of \(\langle\pi(g)v,w\rangle\). The map \(\pi(g)v\mapsto\rho(g)Uv\) is then isometric on their dense spans, since inner products are coefficients at \(g^{-1}h\); it extends to a unitary intertwiner. Thus distinct irreducibles cannot have isomorphic nonzero corner modules. Increasing \(J\) therefore identifies the complete irreducible sums and multiplicities.

If infinity is outside \(S\), first fix a finite level \(J\) and a finite real compact-type projection \(Q\). At these types choose a common constant \(c\) so that \(L=\Delta+c\) is positive on every unitary representation having one of them; the local classification in Lesson 11 gives a uniform lower bound. The spectra of this operator in both sums are discrete with finite multiplicities and polynomial count. On the split side this is (3.17), together with the finite residual part; fixing nonzero vectors at the finite ramified types embeds the sum into that finite-level spectrum. On the division side the corresponding finite-level real quotient is compact; the coordinate-rectangle elliptic comparison used above gives the same polynomial count there. It also proves finite multiplicity in a bounded spectral interval.

For any smooth compact outside test \(a\), multiply its \(J,Q\)-corner operator by a smooth even Fourier multiplier of \(\sqrt L\). Finite propagation and the weight-bundle construction of Lemma 2.21 realize every multiplier with compactly supported smooth time transform as another compact outside test. Equation (3.26) thus identifies the distributions \(\sum_\lambda\operatorname{tr}(a|_{\lambda,J,Q})\delta_{\sqrt\lambda}\) on the two sides. To isolate a bounded spectral interval, take a smooth function supported in that interval's square-root image and approximate its inverse Fourier transform by compact time cutoffs. The multiplier converges in all Schwartz seminorms. The polynomial eigenvalue count and \(\|a\|\le\|a\|_1\) dominate the traces, so their equalities pass to the limit. On each bounded interval both discrete spectra contain finitely many points. For each point choose a smooth multiplier equal to one there and zero at every other point of the union in a slightly larger interval, with support in that larger interval. The Schwartz convergence just proved isolates its finite-dimensional eigenspace. Adding finitely many such multipliers gives equality of all corner traces in any bounded spectral window whose endpoints avoid the spectra. These spectral projections commute with the outside group action because the Casimir is central. The finite-dimensional argument just proved, now with the compact-type and level projection \(e=Qe_J\), applies unchanged. Smooth averaging of \(e\pi(g)e\) recovers its compressed coefficients. Increasing the type packet, the level and the spectral interval proves equivalence of the entire sums. This proves the lemma in both cases. \(\square\)

Proof of Theorem 3.1. Start with an automorphic irreducible \(\pi'\) of the division group and take its tuple \(T\). Lemma 3.6 gives at least one summand of \(M_T^G\) with the same outside representation. If this summand is a determinant character, (3.25) forces every \(T_v\) to be its corresponding norm character. All local factors of \(\pi'\) are then those of \(\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd}\). The tensor-product uniqueness in Lesson 12 identifies \(\pi'\) with that one-dimensional representation. A non-one-dimensional \(\pi'\) must therefore give a cuspidal \(\pi\), with exactly the factors (3.2).

Conversely a cuspidal \(\pi\) with discrete-series components at every ramified place has a unique inverse local tuple by Theorems 2.5 and 2.30. Its occurrence in \(M_T^G\) implies occurrence of \(\pi^S\) in \(M_T^D\). The resulting \(\pi'\) cannot be one dimensional: a one-dimensional split local factor outside \(S\) has no Whittaker model, whereas a cuspidal factor has one by Lesson 14, Theorem 2.1. It is the required preimage.

Strong multiplicity one for split cusp forms, proved in Lesson 15, Theorem 3.1, says that two cuspidal summands with this outside representation are the same. Their multiplicity is one by the Whittaker multiplicity proof in Lesson 14. A split determinant character cannot share that outside representation: at any good finite place it is nongeneric. Distinct determinant characters with the same outside data are also equal, since weak approximation makes \(\mathbb Q^\times\) dense in the product of the finitely many remaining local multiplicative groups. On the division side fixed outside factors and fixed \(T\) determine the full tensor product. Equality of the multiplicities in Lemma 3.6 therefore gives \(m_D(\pi')=1\).

The same argument handles the one-dimensional part rather than assuming its classification. Any one-dimensional automorphic \(\pi'\) has one-dimensional split factors outside \(S\), so the corresponding summand in \(M_T^G\) cannot be cuspidal. It must be a determinant character, forcing \(\pi'=\chi\circ\operatorname{nrd}\), \(\chi^2=\omega\), at every place. Conversely each such Hecke character defines that automorphic norm function. Its right-equivariant line has multiplicity one: a function with that right transformation rule is a scalar multiple of it. Thus the residual and norm character lines correspond exactly.

Finally, if two non-one-dimensional division constituents agree at almost all places, their cuspidal images agree there and hence everywhere by split strong multiplicity one. The local bijections recover their ramified factors, and tensor-product uniqueness recovers the division constituents. A norm character cannot agree almost everywhere with a non-one-dimensional constituent, since their split factors would contradict genericity. Two norm characters agreeing almost everywhere agree by the character argument above. This proves strong multiplicity one on the entire division spectrum, the image assertion, and every claim in Theorem 3.1. \(\square\)

The argument proves the quaternion global theorem over \(\mathbb Q\), with arbitrary unitary central character and with or without real ramification. The higher-degree number-field and function-field assertions in Section 6 retain their separate scope; they are not consequences of the rank-two truncation proved here.

4. Weight two and the dimension equality

Take \(D=B_{p,\infty}\), ramified at the prime \(p\) and at infinity, and a maximal order \(\mathcal O\). Let \(I_1,\ldots,I_h\) represent its right ideal classes, with left orders \(\mathcal O_i\), and put \[ w_i=|\mathcal O_i^\times|/2,\qquad M(\mathcal O)=\mathbb C^{\operatorname{Cls}(\mathcal O)},\qquad \langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{h}\frac{x_i\overline{y_i}}{w_i}. \tag{4.1} \] These are the column-function and row-sum Brandt conventions of Lesson 17. Write \(M^0(\mathcal O)=\mathbf1^\perp\). Its dimension is \(h-1\).

Corollary 4.1 — the classical specialization. From the stated global correspondence one obtains a Hecke-module isomorphism \[ M^0(\mathcal O)\simeq S_2(\Gamma_0(p)), \qquad B(\ell)\longleftrightarrow T_\ell\quad(\ell\ne p). \tag{4.2} \] The right side consists entirely of newforms. This is the prime-level instance of Eichler's basis problem and the Jacquet–Langlands–Shimizu correspondence.

Deduction from Theorem 3.1. Lesson 17, Section 4 identifies \(M(\mathcal O)\) with automorphic functions invariant under \(\widehat{\mathcal O}^{\times}\) and the real quaternion group, with trivial centre. The only norm character in this space is constant: its idele-class character is unramified at every finite prime and trivial on \(\mathbb R_{>0}\), and the idele decomposition for \(\mathbb Q\) from Lesson 1 forces it to be trivial. Removing constants therefore removes precisely the excluded constituent of Theorem 3.1.

At infinity, the trivial quaternion type transfers to \(D_2\). At \(p\), a representation with \(\mathcal O_p^\times\)-invariants factors through \(D_p^\times/\mathcal O_p^\times\), an infinite cyclic group, and is an unramified norm character. It transfers to an unramified Steinberg twist. That twist has conductor one and a one-dimensional level-one fixed space by Lesson 8, Theorems 2.1 and 3.2. At other primes the spherical space is one-dimensional by Lesson 13, Theorem 3.2. Global multiplicity one and the classical dictionary of Lesson 8, Theorem 5.1 and Lesson 2, Theorem 4.1 therefore identify one line on each side.

Conversely, a weight-two newform of prime level and trivial character has a conductor-one component at \(p\). The principal-series conductor formula rules out a ramified principal series: trivial central character makes the two ramified unit characters inverse, hence their conductor sum is at least two. Supercuspidals also have conductor at least two. Thus its local component is an unramified Steinberg twist, and Theorem 3.1 transfers it back. Level-one weight-two cusp forms vanish [LG-MF-06, Theorem 4.1], so there are no oldforms to add. At a good prime the weight-two scaling factor in Lesson 13, Theorem 4.1 is one; the operators agree as asserted. \(\square\)

We now prove the assigned dimension check by a separate calculation. It checks (4.2) without using (4.2) to count dimensions. For compactified modular curves we use [LG-MF-03, Lemma 3.1 and Proposition 3.2]: \(X(1)\) is a sphere, the only branching is over its order-two and order-three elliptic points and its cusp, and the local indices are the stabilizer quotients and cusp widths. Riemann–Hurwitz is stated in that lesson's Section 3. The weight-two differential dictionary and \(h^0(\Omega^1)=g\) are [LG-MF-06, Theorems 2.1 and 4.1].

Theorem 4.2 — the dimension check, for every prime. \[ \dim S_2(\Gamma_0(p))=h(\mathcal O)-1. \tag{4.3} \]

Proof. Reduction maps \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)\) onto \(\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb F_p)\). Indeed, elementary row operations over the field express a determinant-one matrix as a product of elementary unipotents, and each such factor lifts integrally. The preimage of the upper triangular subgroup is \(\Gamma_0(p)\). Thus the cosets are the \(p+1\) lines of \(\mathbb F_p^2\), and the degree of \(X_0(p)\to X(1)\) is \(N=p+1\).

The order-two element \(S=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) has fixed lines represented by \((x,1)\) satisfying \(x^2+1=0\). The order-three element \(ST=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\) has fixed lines satisfying \(x^2+x+1=0\). Neither fixes the line \((1,0)\). Hence their fixed counts are \[ e_2=1+s_4,\quad e_3=1+s_3,\qquad s_4=\left(\frac{-4}{p}\right),\quad s_3=\left(\frac{-3}{p}\right), \tag{4.4} \] with Kronecker symbols. For odd primes other than the relevant discriminant divisor, completing the square gives the usual quadratic-residue counts. Directly at \(2\), the counts are \(1,0\); at \(3\), they are \(0,1\), agreeing with (4.4).

There are exactly two cusps. Write a rational cusp as \(a/c\) with \(\gcd(a,c)=1\). If \(p\mid c\), complete \((a,c)\) to a determinant-one matrix in \(\Gamma_0(p)\), showing equivalence to \(\infty\). If \(p\nmid c\), solve \(xc-ay=1\). Replacing \((x,y)\) by \((x+at,y+ct)\) makes \(p\mid y\), so \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x&a\\y&c\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\Gamma_0(p)\) sends \(0\) to \(a/c\). The two cases are distinct because the property \(p\mid c\) is preserved under \(\Gamma_0(p)\). The widths are one at \(\infty\) and \(p\) at \(0\), as follows by conjugating \(T^n\) by \(S\).

The coset action of \(S\) has \(e_2\) fixed points and \((N-e_2)/2\) two-cycles. The latter contribute \((N-e_2)/2\) to Riemann–Hurwitz. Similarly, the order-three contribution is \(2(N-e_3)/3\). The two cusps contribute \((1-1)+(p-1)=N-2\). There is no other branching, so \[ 2g-2=-2N+\frac{N-e_2}{2} +\frac{2(N-e_3)}{3}+N-2, \qquad g=\frac{p+1}{12}-\frac{e_2}{4}-\frac{e_3}{3}. \tag{4.5} \] By the differential dictionary, \(\dim S_2=g\). Concretely, \(f(z)dz\) is invariant; at a cusp of width \(h\), \(dz=h\,dq/(2\pi i q)\), so the vanishing constant term is exactly the condition that the differential extends holomorphically. The elliptic descent and the reverse pullback are included in LG-MF-06, Theorem 2.1.

The class-number evaluation proved from the Eichler trace formula in Lesson 18, equation (6.3), is \[ h(\mathcal O)=\frac{p-1}{12} +\frac{1-s_4}{4}+\frac{1-s_3}{3}. \tag{4.6} \] Its mass input is \(\sum_i1/w_i=(p-1)/12\), proved in Lesson 17, Theorem 4.3. Substituting (4.4) in (4.5) and subtracting one from (4.6) give the same quantity, since \(1/4+1/3-1=-5/12\). This proves (4.3), including \(p=2,3\). \(\square\)

5.1. Level eleven

For \(p=11\), \(s_4=s_3=-1\). 5.2. A maximal order at level thirty-seven

Let \[ D=\left(\frac{-2,-37}{\mathbb Q}\right),\qquad i^2=-2,\quad j^2=-37,\quad k=ij=-ji. \] At \(37\) its Hilbert symbol is \(\left(\frac{-2}{37}\right)=-1\); at infinity both parameters are negative. Every other odd prime has Hilbert symbol one because both parameters are units there. Hilbert reciprocity, proved by the quaternion classification deduction of Lesson 17, Section 2 and its written Brauer provider, then makes the symbol at \(2\) one. Thus \(D=B_{37,\infty}\).

Put \[ \beta=(1+i+j)/2,\qquad \gamma=(2+i+k)/4, \qquad \mathcal O=\mathbb Z\{1,i,\beta,\gamma\}. \tag{5.2} \] The complete products apart from multiplication by \(1\) are \[ \begin{array}{lll} i^2=-2,&\beta^2=\beta-10,&\gamma^2=\gamma-5,\\ i\beta=2\gamma-2,&\beta i=i-2\gamma,&i\gamma=i-\beta,\\ \gamma i=\beta-1,&\beta\gamma=5i,& \gamma\beta=\beta+\gamma-1-5i. \end{array} \tag{5.3} \] Each identity follows by expanding (5.2) and using \(ji=-ij\). They show that this lattice is an order. For further verification its reduced-trace pairing has matrix \[ \bigl(\operatorname{trd}(e_re_s)\bigr)_{r,s} =\begin{pmatrix} 2&0&1&1\\ 0&-4&-2&-1\\ 1&-2&-19&0\\ 1&-1&0&-9 \end{pmatrix},\qquad\det=-37^2, \tag{5.4} \] in the ordered basis \(1,i,\beta,\gamma\). Hence its reduced discriminant is \(37\). An order whose reduced discriminant equals the algebra discriminant is maximal, by the order/discriminant criterion used in Lesson 17, Section 5.2.

The norm, expanded in that basis, is \[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{nrd}(a+bi+c\beta+d\gamma) &=(a+c/2+d/2)^2 +2(b+c/2+d/4)^2\\ &\quad+37c^2/4+74d^2/16. \end{aligned} \tag{5.5} \] For norms at most three, its last two summands force \(c=d=0\). The units are therefore \(\pm1\), the norm-two elements are \(\pm i\), and the norm-three elements are \(\pm1\pm i\). Their counts are \(2,2,4\).

At \(37\), both symbols in (4.4) equal one. Thus \(h=3\), and the mass is \((37-1)/12=3\). Each \(1/w_i\le1\); three such terms can sum to three only if all \(w_i=1\). Every Brandt matrix is consequently symmetric in the ordinary pairing.

6. Higher degree and geometric realizations

For a nonarchimedean local field \(F\), a central division algebra \(D_0/F\) of degree \(d\), and \(n=rd\), the local correspondence is a bijection between essentially square-integrable representations of \(\mathrm{GL}_r(D_0)\) and \(\mathrm{GL}_n(F)\). On matching regular classes its character relation has sign \[ \Theta_\pi(g)=(-1)^{n-r}\Theta_{\pi'}(g'). \tag{6.1} \] This is the local theorem of Deligne–Kazhdan–Vignéras and Rogawski in characteristic zero; Arthur, Theorem 25.1 states it. Badulescu 2002, Theorem 1.1 gives (6.1) in positive characteristic. The full proof of that local theorem is not reproduced here.

Over number fields, Badulescu–Renard, Theorem 18.1 gives an injection of the inner form's discrete automorphic spectrum into the split discrete spectrum, onto the locally compatible representations, with multiplicity one and strong multiplicity one. Its transfer uses the extended unitary local correspondence; global discrete spectrum need not mean local discrete series everywhere. This distinction includes the norm/determinant characters already encountered in degree two.

For a central division algebra of degree \(n\) over a global function field, the analogous discrete-spectrum bijection with the locally compatible split representations, and multiplicity one, are Badulescu–Roche, Theorems 3.2–3.3. Compatibility uses their local unitary transfer, Theorem 2.5; their Sections 3.2–3.3 explain the central degree quotient and its relation to fixed central characters. This global positive-characteristic result is separate from Badulescu's 2002 local theorem. Badulescu–Roche's paper.

There is also a geometric question: can a correspondence between automorphic representations be realized by a relation between cohomology classes? Ichino–Prasanna's introduction studies such Hodge and cohomological realizations for quaternionic Shimura varieties. Their work assumes the relevant Jacquet–Langlands transfer; it is not the source of the basic existence theorem (3.1). The introduction distinguishes its Hodge-class construction from arithmetic cycle assertions requiring additional conjectural input. Those finer realizations are beyond this lesson.

6.1. Which regular classes match in higher degree?

The class comparison in Section 1 extends to matrix algebras over a division algebra, with a divisibility condition on every irreducible factor. We prove that condition before using higher-degree character identities. Throughout this subsection, \(F\) is a nonarchimedean local field of either characteristic, \(D_0/F\) is a central division algebra of degree \(d\), \(A=M_r(D_0)\), and \(n=rd\).

We use the local invariant and restriction rule proved in Brauer groups of local and global fields, Proposition 24.1 and Theorem 24.2. The algebraic facts that a Brauer class has a unique division representative, and that a split central simple algebra acts on its column module, are proved in Central simple algebras and the Brauer group, Sections 1–4.

Lemma 6.1 — the splitting degree of a local division algebra. The invariant of \(D_0\) has denominator exactly \(d\). For a finite separable extension \(E/F\), \[ D_0\otimes_F E\simeq M_d(E) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d\mid[E:F]. \tag{6.2} \]

Proof. Write a Brauer invariant in lowest terms as \(a/e\). Choose the unramified extension \(L/F\) of degree \(e\), with arithmetic Frobenius \(\sigma\). Its cyclic algebra \[ C=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{e-1}L u^j, \qquad ux=\sigma(x)u,\qquad u^e=\varpi^a \] has invariant \(a/e\) by Proposition 24.1. If the invariant is nonzero take \(1\le a<e\); for the zero class take \(e=1,a=0\).

For a nonzero element set \[ v_C\left(\sum_jx_ju^j\right) =\min_{x_j\ne0}\{e\,v_L(x_j)+aj\}. \] When \(e>1\), coprimality makes the nonzero terms' displayed valuations distinct modulo \(e\), so the minimum is unique. In a product, the two minimal terms give one nonzero term of valuation the sum of the two minima. Reducing powers with \(u^e=\varpi^a\) preserves that valuation. Every other product term has strictly larger valuation and cannot cancel it; Frobenius preserves the valuation of coefficients. Thus \(v_C(xy)=v_C(x)+v_C(y)\), so \(C\) has no zero divisors. A finite-dimensional algebra without zero divisors is division: left multiplication by a nonzero element is injective and hence surjective, producing an inverse. The case \(e=1\) is the field itself.

This constructs a division representative of dimension \(e^2\) for every invariant. Uniqueness of the division representative makes its degree \(e\) equal to \(d\) for the class of \(D_0\). Restriction to \(E\) multiplies the invariant by \([E:F]\). It is zero exactly when \(d\) divides that degree, and zero Brauer class for this degree-\(d\) algebra means \(M_d(E)\). This proves (6.2). \(\square\)

Theorem 6.2 — the regular matching criterion. Let \(P=\prod_{i=1}^sP_i\in F[X]\) be monic and separable of degree \(n\), with nonzero constant term and distinct monic irreducible factors \(P_i\). A regular semisimple element of \(\mathrm{GL}_n(F)\) with polynomial \(P\) matches an element of \(\mathrm{GL}_r(D_0)\) if and only if \[ d\mid\deg P_i\qquad\text{for every }i. \tag{6.3} \] When it exists, the matching conjugacy class is unique. Both centralizers are \(\prod_i E_i^\times\), with \(E_i=F[X]/(P_i)\).

Proof: necessity. Regard \(A\) as the endomorphism algebra of the right \(D_0\)-space \(V=D_0^r\). For a regular semisimple \(x\in A\), the minimal polynomial equals its reduced characteristic polynomial: after a separable splitting extension its matrix has \(n\) distinct eigenvalues, all of which a polynomial annihilating it must contain. Thus \(F[x]\simeq\prod_i E_i\). Its idempotents decompose \(V=\bigoplus_iV_i\) into right \(D_0\)-subspaces. Put \(r_i=\dim_{D_0}V_i\).

After splitting \(D_0\), a rank-\(r_i\) idempotent has matrix rank \(dr_i\). It selects exactly the roots of \(P_i\) in the reduced characteristic polynomial. Consequently \(\deg P_i=dr_i\), proving (6.3). For completeness, this rank calculation follows by applying a primitive matrix idempotent to the right \(M_d\)-module \(M_d^{r_i}\): the resulting vector space has dimension \(dr_i\), and the induced endomorphism is the corresponding block of the split degree-\(n\) matrix.

Proof: existence. Suppose (6.3), and write \(m_i=\deg P_i=dr_i\). Lemma 6.1 gives \(E_i\otimes_F D_0^{\mathrm{op}}\simeq M_d(E_i)\). Its column module \(W_i=E_i^d\) is therefore an \(E_i\)-space with a commuting right \(D_0\)-action. Its \(F\)-dimension is \(m_id=r_id^2\), so its right \(D_0\)-dimension is \(r_i\). The element \(X\bmod P_i\) acts on \(W_i\) by an invertible right-\(D_0\)-linear map.

On \(W=\bigoplus_iW_i\), of right dimension \(r\), these maps give \(x\in A^\times\). Its ordinary \(F\)-linear characteristic polynomial is \(\prod_iP_i^d=P^d\). For any element of \(A\), this ordinary characteristic polynomial is the \(d\)-th power of its reduced characteristic polynomial: after splitting, the right module is the space of \(n\)-by-\(d\) matrices, and left multiplication acts by the same degree-\(n\) matrix on each of its \(d\) columns. Both sides descend to \(F\). Unique factorization of monic polynomials now gives the reduced polynomial \(P\). This remains valid when the characteristic divides \(d\); equality of positive powers in \(F[X]\) still determines a monic polynomial.

Proof: uniqueness and centralizers. If \(x,y\in A\) have reduced polynomial \(P\), consider the \(F\)-vector space \(U=\{c\in A:cx=yc\}\). Over a separable splitting field the two regular matrices diagonalize with the same distinct eigenvalues, so \(U\) contains an invertible matrix. The reduced norm, restricted to \(U\), is consequently a nonzero polynomial over \(F\). Since \(F\) is infinite it cannot vanish on all of \(U(F)\): choose coordinates and use induction on the number of variables, with the finite root bound in one variable. Hence some \(c\in U(F)\) is invertible, and \(y=cxc^{-1}\).

The algebra centralizer of \(x\) has dimension \(n\), as is seen after splitting from the diagonal matrices commuting with \(n\) distinct eigenvalues. It contains the \(n\)-dimensional algebra \(F[x]\), so it equals \(\prod_iE_i\). Taking units proves the centralizer assertion. On the split side the companion matrix gives existence and the same intertwiner argument gives uniqueness. \(\square\)

For example, take \(F=\mathbb Q_3\), \(d=r=2\), and let \(D_0\) be quaternion division. The separable polynomial \((X^2+1)(X^2-2)\) has two distinct irreducible quadratic factors, since both reduce to an irreducible quadratic modulo three. It therefore occurs in \(M_2(D_0)\). In contrast, \((X-1)(X^3-X-1)\) has irreducible factor degrees one and three: its cubic has no root modulo three. Its companion matrix in \(\mathrm{GL}_4(\mathbb Q_3)\) has no matching class in \(\mathrm{GL}_2(D_0)\).

Finally, on a matched regular class the adjoint discriminants agree. After splitting they are both \(\prod_{i\ne j}(1-\alpha_i/\alpha_j)\), where the \(\alpha_i\) are the distinct roots of \(P\); this symmetric expression lies in \(F\) and its absolute value gives the quotient conjugation Jacobian. The relative split ranks modulo the centre are \(n-1\) and \(r-1\), so their difference is \(n-r\), the exponent in (6.1). The matching criterion and discriminant calculation supply the algebraic comparison in all degrees. The representation bijection and global discrete-spectrum assertions above require their additional character and trace arguments.

7. Exercises with complete solutions

Exercise 7.1 — an unramified quadratic field. Let \(K/\mathbb Q_p\) be the unramified quadratic extension. Choose \(\xi\in\mathcal O_K^\times\) whose residue generates \(\mathbb F_{p^2}/\mathbb F_p\). Write explicitly the matching matrix and quaternion class, and their centralizers.

Solution 7.1. Let \(\sigma\) be the nontrivial automorphism, \(t=\xi+\sigma\xi\), and \(n=\xi\sigma\xi\). The reduction of \(T^2-tT+n\) is irreducible and separable, so its lift is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q_p\). In the cyclic model \[ D_p=K\oplus K\Pi,\qquad \Pi^2=p,\qquad \Pi a=\sigma(a)\Pi, \] the element \(\xi\) has that reduced polynomial. The matching matrix is \(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-n\\1&t\end{smallmatrix}\right)\), the matrix of multiplication by \(\xi\) on \(K\) in the basis \(1,\xi\). Its centralizer is the multiplication copy of \(K^\times\); the quaternion centralizer is also \(K^\times\), by Theorem 1.1. Conjugation by \(\Pi\) exchanges \(\xi\) and \(\sigma\xi\), so either root defines the same class. This remains valid at \(p=2\), because unramified residue extensions are separable.

Exercise 7.2 — check the Steinberg character. Verify (2.3) for the trivial representation of \(D^\times\) and the Steinberg representation of \(G\), including an unramified unitary character twist.

Solution 7.2. An elliptic matrix has no invariant \(F\)-line. Proposition 2.2 consequently gives zero character for \(C^\infty(\mathbb P^1(F))\) on the elliptic regular set. The determinants and norms match by Theorem 1.1, so the negative relation persists. The central characters are both \(\chi(z^2)\). Exercise 7.4 — what transfers at the identity? Explain how the local minus sign fits the identity contribution of the compact trace formula. Specify the analytic transfer input and why global multiplicities remain positive.
